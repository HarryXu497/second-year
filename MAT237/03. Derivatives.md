# Introduction

In single variable calculus, we tend to introduce derivatives as a rate of change or slope. This does not hold up well in multivariable calculus because those concepts are inherently one dimensional.

Instead, we will interpret derivatives as linear approximations: as an approximation of a **nonlinear maps with linear maps**.

# Derivatives of One Variable

Consider maps of the form $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$.

## Definition

> [!NOTE] Definition: Derivatives of a One-Variable Function
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. The **derivative of $f$ at $a$** is defined to be
> 
> $$
> f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
> $$
> 
> provided the limit exists. If the limit exists, then $f$ is **differentiable at $a$**.

^fe1b4f

Note that the limit variable $h \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar which approaches the real number $0$. The quantity $\tfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ is the scalar $1/h \in \mathbb{R}$ multiplied by the vector $f(a + h) - f(a) \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Thus, $f'(a)$ is also an element of $\mathbb{R}^m$. You can also prove the limit is equal to 

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

using a theorem on composition. **The derivative is only defined at interior points of $A$. This choice is not necessary but it is made for simplicity.**

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be defined by $f(x) = (\cos x, \sin x)$. Then, $f$ is differentiable at $\pi$.
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f'(\pi) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{(\cos(\pi+h), \sin(\pi+h)) - (\cos \pi, \sin \pi)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left(\dfrac{\cos(\pi + h) - \cos \pi}{h}, \dfrac{\sin(\pi + h) - \sin \pi}{h}\right) \\
> &= \left(\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\cos(\pi + h) - \cos \pi}{h}, \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\sin(\pi + h) - \sin \pi}{h}\right) \\
> &= \left(\cos'(\pi), \sin'(\pi)\right) \\
> &= \left(-\sin \pi, \cos \pi\right) \\
> &= \left(0, -1\right) \\
> \end{align*}
> $$

## Basic Properties

The prior example illustrates that derivatives of parametric curves reduce to differentiation from single variable calculus. All you need to do is differentiable component by component.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Component-wise Differentiation
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. The function $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if for every $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, the component function $f_i$ is differentiable at $a$. If so,
> 
> $$
> f'(a) = (f_1'(a), \ldots, f_m'(a))
> $$
> 
> The proof follows from component-wise limits and the definition of a derivative.

^007ee0

You can prove basic properties of the derivative for parametric curves using the definition and limit laws.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $f$ and $g$ be $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued functions defined on $A$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $\varphi$ be a real-valued function defined on $A$.
> 
> 1. **Linearity**: If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $a$, then $f + \lambda g$ is differentiable at $a$ and 
> 
>    $$
>    (f + \lambda g)'(a) = f'(a) + \lambda g'(a)
>    $$
> 
> 2. **Scalar product**: If $f$ and $\varphi$ are differentiable at $a$, then $\varphi f$ is differentiable at $a$ and
> 
>    $$
>    (\varphi f)'(a) = \varphi'(a)f(a) + \varphi(a) f'(a)
>    $$
> 
> 3. **Dot product**: If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $a$, then $f \cdot g$ is differentiable at $a$ and 
> 
>    $$
>    (f \cdot g)'(a) = f'(a)  \cdot g(a) + f(a) \cdot g'(a)
>    $$

> [!NOTE] Proof: Linearity of Derivatives for Parametric Curves
> Write $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m)$ and $g = (g_1, \ldots, g_m)$. By [[#^007ee0|component-wise differentiation]], each of the components $f_i + \lambda g_i$ are differentiable at $a$ and
> 
> $$
> (f + \lambda g)'(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> (f_1 + \lambda g_1)'(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> (f_m + \lambda g_m)'(a)
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> By linearity of differentiation for maps $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the above is equal to
> 
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> (f_1 + \lambda g_1)'(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> (f_m + \lambda g_m)'(a)
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> f_1'(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> f_m'(a)
> \end{bmatrix} + \lambda \begin{bmatrix}
> g_1'(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> g_m'(a)
> \end{bmatrix} = f'(a) + \lambda g'(a)
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Chain Rule
> Let $A, B \in \mathbb{R}$ be sets. Let $\varphi: A \to B$ be a real-valued function, and let $f$ be a $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued function defined on $B$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$ such that $\varphi(a)$ is an interior point of $B$. If $\varphi$ is differentiable at $a$ and $f$ is differentiable at $\varphi(a)$, then
> 
> $$
> (f \circ \varphi)'(a) = f'(\varphi(a)) \varphi'(a)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Write $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m)$. Then we have
> 
> $$
> (f \circ \varphi)(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> f_1(\varphi(a)) \\
> \vdots \\
> f_m(\varphi(a)) \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> We are given that $\varphi$ is differentiable at $a$. Since $f$ is differentiable at $\varphi(a)$, by [[#^007ee0|component-wise differentiation]], each of the components are also differentiable at $\varphi(a)$. Then, we have
> 
> $$
> (f \circ \varphi)(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> f_1'(\varphi(a))\varphi'(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> f_m'(\varphi(a))\varphi(a) \\
> \end{bmatrix} = f'(\varphi(a)) \varphi'(a)
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

## Different Viewpoints

> [!NOTE] Viewpoint: Physical Viewpoint
> If $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is the position of a particle, then the derivative $f'(a)$ is the **instantaneous velocity** of the particle at time $a$ and position $f(a)$.
> 
> ![[physical viewpoint.png]]

> [!NOTE] Viewpoint: Geometric Viewpoint
> The derivative of a parametric curve can be used to define the tangent line at a point on the curve. The **tangent line** at the point $f(a)$ on the parametric curve is presumably given by the set 
> 
> $$
> \{f(a) = hf'(a): h \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> Note that this is not quite the definition of a tangent line, but it is close enough for now.
> 
> Notice $f(a)$ is a point on the line and $f'(a)$ is a direction vector defining the line.
> 
> ![[geometric viewpoint.png]]

> [!NOTE] Viewpoint: Analytic Viewpoint
> Fix a point $a$. We can view the derivative as an approximation of $f(x)$ for $x$ near $a$. Heuristically, you can expect that
> 
> $$
> f'(a) \approx \dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}
> $$
> 
> Multiplying both sides by the scalar $x-a$ and solving for the vector $f(x)$ yields
> 
> $$
> f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)
> $$
> 
> when $x \approx a$. The righthand side is a linear polynomial in the scalar $x$. In fact, we define the **linear approximation of $f$ at $a$** as the function $\ell: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ defined by
> 
> $$
> \ell(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
> $$
> 
> and we expect that $f(x) \approx \ell(x)$ for $x$ near $a$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f(x) = (\cos x, \sin x)$. Since $f(\pi) = (-1, 0)$ and $f'(\pi) = (0, -1)$, the linear approximation of $f$ at $\pi$ is the function $\ell: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ given by
> 
> $$
> \ell(x) = (-1, 0) + (0, -1)(x - pi) = (-1, \pi - x)
> $$
> 
> For instance, $f(3)$ is approximately $\ell(3) = (-1, \pi - 3)$.

> [!NOTE] Viewpoint: Algebraic Viewpoint
> By incorporating linear algebra, you can view the derivative from a completely novel perspective.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. The function $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if there exists a liner map $L: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{h} = 0
> $$
> 
> in which case $L(h) = f'(a)h$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Assume $f$ is differentiable at $a$ so $f'(a)$ exists. Then define $L: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ by $L(h) = f'(a)h$ for $h \in \mathbb{R}$. It follows that
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{h} = \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - f'(a)h}{h} = \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} - f'(a)
> $$
> 
> Taking $h \to 0$, we have
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} - \lim_{h \to 0} f'(a) = f'(a) - f'(a) = 0
> $$
> 
> Conversely, assume such a linear map $L: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ exists so $L(h) = Bh$ for some $m \times 1$ matrix $B$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f'(a) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h) + L(h)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left(\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{h} + B\right) \\
> &= B
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> by linearity of limits. $\blacksquare$

The above definition allows us to blend linear algebra with calculus. We give it a formal definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Differentials
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then the linear map $df_a: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ defined by $df_a(h) = f'(a)h$ is the **differential of $f$ at $a$**.

The differential has a nice relationship with the analytic viewpoint of linear approximations. Namely, for $h$ near $0$, you expect

$$
f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a) h = f(a) + df_a(h)
$$

so $df_a(h)$ approximates the difference between $f(a)$ and $f(a + h)$. 

Differentials can elegantly encapsulate properties of the derivative. A showcase example is the chain rule from single variable calculus. Let $f, g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable everywhere. Fix $a \in \mathbb{R}$. The chain rule in single variable calculus states that

$$
(g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a)) f'(a)
$$

In terms of differentials, this implies that

$$
\begin{align*}
d(g \circ f)_a (h) = (g \circ f)'(a) h &= g'(f(a))(f'(a)h) \\
&= g'(f(a)) df_a(h) \\
&= dg_{f(a)} (df_a(h)) \\
&= (dg_{f(a)} \circ df_a)(h)
\end{align*}
$$

The chain rule is therefore equivalent to the following statement about linear maps:

$$
d(g \circ f)_a = dg_{f(a)} \circ df_a
$$

In other words, the differential of the composition is the composition of differentials.

# Partial Derivatives

Consider maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m
$$

To generalize the derivative to these maps, there is a serious challenge we must first overcome for $n \ge 2$: there are countless ways to "change near a point" inside $\mathbb{R}^n$.

A partial derivative allows us to reduce everything to a one-dimenaional setting.

## Definition

From Chapter 1, we reduced high dimensional space to lower dimensional space by slicing/fixing variables.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x, y) = x^2 = y^2$. To investigate how $f$ changes near the point $(a, b) = (1, 2)$, we can investigate how it changes with respect to a single variable by fixing all other variables.
> 
> Define the single variable functions $p, q: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
> 
> $$
> p(x) = f(x, 2) = x^2 - 4, \quad p(x) = f(1, y) = 1 - y^2
> $$
> 
> such that $p$ is the function $f$ with $y = 2$ fixed and $q$ is the function $f$ with $x = 1$ fixed. Then, we have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> p'(1) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{p(1 + h) - p(1)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(1 + h, 2) - f(1, 2)}{h} \\
> q'(2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{q(2 + h) - q(2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(1, 2 + h) - f(1, 2)}{h} \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Note that $p'(1)$ and $q'(2)$ quantify how $f$ changes in the $x$ and $y$ direction at $(1, 2)$, respectively.
> 
> We also have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f(1 + h, 2) &\approx f(1, 2) + p'(1)h = -3 + 2h
> f(1, 2 + h) &\approx f(1, 2) + q'(2)h = -3 - 4h
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> and diagram
> 
> ![[partial derivatives 1.png]]

> [!NOTE] Definition: Partial Derivatives
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Fix $1 \le j \le n$. Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be the standard basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$. The **$j$th partial derivative of $f$ at $a$** is given by
> 
> $$
> \partial_j f(a) := \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + he_j) - f(a)}{h}
> $$
> 
> provided the limit exists. The **$j$th partial derivative of $f$** is the function $\partial_j f: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$, where $U$ is the set of points $a \in A$ such that $\partial_j f(a)$ exists.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Equivalent notation for $\partial_jf$ includes
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}, \quad D_{e_j}f, \quad f_{x_j}
> $$

The standard basis is used to fix all variables except one. For instance, fix $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$. If $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_{j-1}, a_j, a_{j + 1}, \ldots, a_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ then

$$
\forall h \in \mathbb{R}, a + he_j = (a_1, \ldots, a_{j-1}, a_j + h, a_{j + 1}, \ldots, a_n)
$$

so all components except the $j$th component remain fixed.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Recall $f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$. By definition
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \partial_1 f(1, 2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f((1, 2) + h(1, 0)) - f(1, 2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(1 + h, 2) - f(1, 2)}{h}
> \partial_2 f(1, 2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f((1, 2) + h(0, 1)) - f(1, 2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(1, 2 + h) - f(1, 2)}{h}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> These can be simply calculated by single variable differentiation rules or the limit definition. For example,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \partial_1 f(1, 2) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(1 + h, 2) - f(1, 2)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{((1 + h)^2 - 2^2) - (-3)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{h^2 + 2h}{h} \\
> &= 2
> \end{align*}
> $$

Note there is no obvious relationship between partial derivatives at the same point.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $H(x, t)$ be the temperature (in Celsius) in a room as a function of distance $x$ (in metres) from a heater and time $t$ (in minutes) after the heater has been turned on.
> 
> Then, the quantity
> 
> $$
> H_x(1, 30) = \lim_{\delta x \to 0} \dfrac{H(1 + \delta x, 30) - H(1, 30)}{\delta x}
> $$
> 
> refers to the rate of change of temperature with respect to the distance while $1$ metre away from the heater and $30$ minutes after it has been turned on.
> 
> Then, the quantity
> 
> $$
> H_t(1, 30) = \lim_{\delta t \to 0} \dfrac{H(1, 30 + \delta t) - H(1, 30)}{\delta t}
> $$
> 
> refers to the rate of change of temperature with respect to the time while $1$ metre away from the heater and $30$ minutes after it has been turned on.
> 
> Consider the contour plot of $H(x, t)$
> 
> ![[partial derivatives 2.png]]
> 
> Say we want to estimate $H_x(1, 30)$. We can use a nearby point on with the same fixed time as the point $(1, 30)$: the point $(4, 30)$. Thus, we can approximate
> 
> $$
> H_x(1, 30) \approx \dfrac{H(1 + 3, 30) - H(1, 30)}{3} = \dfrac{50 - 55}{3} \approx -1.67 \text{ Celsius per metre}
> $$
> 
> Similarly, you can estimate $H_t(1, 30)$ using the point $(1, 60)$ in the contour plot.

## Computations

Partial derivatives reduce to derivatives of one variable.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Component-wise Partial Derivatives
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. The **$j$th partial of $f$ at $a$** exists if and only if for every $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, the $j$th partial of the component function $f_i: A \to \mathbb{R}$ at $a$ exists. If so,
> 
> $$
> \partial_if(a) = (\partial_j f_1(a), \ldots, \partial_j f_m(a))
> $$

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $f, g: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Fix $1 \le j \le n$. Let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $\varphi$ be a real-valued function defined on $A$.
> 
> **Linearity**. If $\partial_j f(a)$ and $\partial_j g(a)$ both exist, then $\partial_j(f + \lambda g)(a)$ exists and
> 
> $$
> \partial_j (f + \lambda g)(a) = \partial_j f(a) + \lambda \partial_j g(a)
> $$
> 
> **Scalar product**. If $\partial_j f(a)$ and $\partial_j \phi(a)$ both exist, then $\partial_j(\lambda f)(a)$ exists and
> 
> $$
> \partial_j (\varphi f)(a) = f(a) \partial_j \varphi(a) + \varphi(a) \partial_j f(a)
> $$
> 
> **Dot product**. If $\partial_j f(a)$ and $\partial_j g(a)$ both exist, then $\partial_j (f \cdot g)(a)$ exists and 
> 
> $$
> \partial_j (f \cdot g)(a) = \partial_j f(a) \cdot g(a) + f(a) \cdot \partial_j g(a)
> $$

All single variable calculus differentiation rules also apply.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Definite the function
> 
> $$
> f(x, y, z) = \left(x^2 - y^2 + \dfrac{x}{x + z}, e^x - xy \right)
> $$
> 
> To compute $\partial_1 f$, we compute the partial derivative of each component $f = (f_1, f_2)$. First,
> 
> $$
> \partial_1 f_1 = \dfrac{df_1}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(x^2 - y^2 + \dfrac{x}{x + z}\right)
> $$
> 
> We have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \partial_1 f_1 &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2) + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(y^2) + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(\dfrac{x}{x + z}\right) \\
> &= 2x +  \dfrac{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} x (x + z) - \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(x + z) x}{(x+z)^2} \\
> &= 2x + \dfrac{(x + z) - x}{(x+z)^2} \\
> &= 2x + \dfrac{z}{(x+z)^2} \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> and
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \partial_1 f_2 &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x) - \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy) \\
> &= e^x - y\\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Therefore, you can conclude that
> 
> $$
> \partial_1 f = \left(2x + \dfrac{z}{(x+ z)^2}, e^x - y\right)
> $$
> 
> Note that the domain of $\partial_1 f$ is $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x + z = 0\}$.

Partial derivatives are an excellent attempt at generalizing the derivative to higher dimensions, but it seems a bit mathematically restrictive. You could attempt to find the rate of change in other directions, not just by fixing variables.

# Directional Derivatives

Partial derivatives only allow you to take derivatives in certain directions. For a function with domain in $\mathbb{R}^n$, there are $n$ such directions, given by the standard basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$.

Directional derivatives describe how a function changes along a line with any arbitrary direction.

## Definition

> [!NOTE] Definition: Directional Derivatives
> The change in $f$ at $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Fix a vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The **directional derivative of $f$ at $a$ with respect to $v$** is given by
> 
> $$
> D_v f(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + hv) - f(a)}{h}
> $$
> 
> The **directional derivative of $f$ with respect to $v$** is the function $D_v f: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$, where $U$ is the set of points $a \in A$ such that $D_v f(a)$ exists.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Since $a$ is an interior point of $A$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that the quantity $f(a + hv)$ is defined for $h \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> If $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is the standard basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $D_{e_j}f = \partial_j f$ so partial derivatives are a special case of directional derivatives.

Note that $v$ does not have to be a unit vector. We can always write a direction derivative with respect to a non-zero vector $v$ in terms of the unit vector $u = v/||v||$:

$$
\begin{align*}
D_v f(a) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + hv) - f(a)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f\left(a + (h ||v||) \left(\dfrac{v}{||v||}\right)\right) - f(a)}{(h||v||)\left(\dfrac{1}{||v||}\right)} \\
&= \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f\left(a + t \left(\dfrac{v}{||v||}\right)\right) - f(a)}{t\left(\dfrac{1}{||v||}\right)} \\
&= ||v||\lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f\left(a + tu\right) - f(a)}{t} \\
&= ||v|| D_u f(a)
\end{align*}
$$

This relation provides an information definition.

> $D_vf(a)$ is the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at the point $a$ in the direction of the unit vector $v/||v||$ **scaled by the norm** $||v||$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x, y) = 9 - x^2 - y^2$. We want to describe how $f$ changes near the point $a = (1, -1)$ in the direction $v = (2, -1) \in \mathbb{R}^2$.
> 
> Define the single variable function $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> g(h) = f(a + hv) &= f((1, -1) + h(2, -1)) \\
> &= f(1 + 2h, -1 - h)) \\
> &= 9 - (1 + 2h)^2 - (-1 - h)^2 \\
> &= 7 - 6h - 5h^2
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Note that $g$ is a single variable function with derivative $g'(h) = -6-10h$ so $g'(0) = -6$. Therefore,
> 
> $$
> D_vf(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + hv) - f(a)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{g(h) - g(0)}{h} = g'(0) = -6
> $$
> 
> The function $\ell: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $\ell(h) = f(a) + hD_vf(a)$ linearly approximates $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $g(h) = f(a + hv)$.
> 
> Also note that the line is given by the set
> 
> $$
> \{(a + hv, f(a) + D_vf(a)): h \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$

## Computations

Once again, directional derivatives reduce differentiation to single variable functions. This produces the usual lemmas and properties.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Fix $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Then, $D_vf(a)$ exists if and only if $D_v f_i(a)$ exists for every $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$. If so,
> 
> $$
> D_vf(a) = (D_v f_1(a), \ldots, D_v f_m(a))
> $$

Computing directional derivatives directly can be frustrating.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x, y) = x^2y$. We compute its directional derivative in the direction $v = (1, 2)$ for any $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ as
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> D_{(1, 2)} f(x, y) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(x + h, y + 2h) - f(x, y)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{(x+h)^2(y+2h) - x^2y}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{2xyh + yh^2 + 2x^2h + 4xh^2 + 2h^3}{h} \\
> &= 2xy + x^2
> \end{align*}
> $$

To simplify this process, we use an idea from linear algebra. Recall that we can write any vector $v= (v_1, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ as a linear combination of the standard basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
v = \sum_{j = 1}^n v_j e_j
$$

A linear map $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ will send the basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ to a set of vectors $\{T(e_1), \ldots, T(e_n)\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$. These may not be a basis for $\mathbb{R}^m$, but this finite set will space the image $\mathrm{im}(T)$. That is,

$$
T(v) = \sum_{j = 1}^n v_j T(e_j)
$$

Since partial derivatives correspond to directional derivatives with the standard basis, this suggests a pair of questions.

> If every partial derivative $\partial_1 f, \ldots, \partial_n f$ exists, does every directional derivative $D_v f$ exist for all $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. If so, can you write it as a linear combination of partials.

Without more assumptions, the answer to the first question is negative.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x, y) = |xy|^{1/2}$. By the limit definition,
> 
> $$
> \partial_1 g(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{g(h, 0) - g(0, 0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{0 - 0}{h} = 0
> $$
> 
> and
> 
> $$
> \partial_2 g(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{g(0, h) - g(0, 0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{0 - 0}{h} = 0
> $$
> 
> On the other hand, 
> 
> $$
> D_{(1, 1)} g(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{g(h, h) - g(0, 0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{|h|}{h}
> $$
> 
> does not exist. In fact, you can verify no other directional derivatives exist. You can see this issue with the graph of $g$ below.
> 
> ![[directional derivative counterexample 1.png]]

Even if you assume that the directional derivative exists, the answer to the second question is also negative without further assumptions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x, y) = \tfrac{x^2y xy^2}{x^2 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$ and $f(0, 0) = 0$. You can again verify that $\partial_1 f(0, 0) = \partial_2 f(0, 0) = 0$. However,
> 
> $$
> D_{(1, 1)} f(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(h, h) - f(0, 0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{h - 0}{h} = 1
> $$
> 
> Thus, $D_{(1, 1)} f(0, 0)$ exists yet $D_{(1, 1)} f(0)$ cannot be expressed as a linear combination of $\partial_1 f(0, 0)$ and $\partial_2 f(0, 0)$.
> 
> ![[directional derivative counterexample 2.png]]

These two examples illustrate that we will require a more powerful assumption about a non-linear map $f$ to "glue together" all directional derivatives $D_v f$ in a way that respects linearity. This assumption will ultimately be the **differentiability** of $f$.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Directional Derivatives of Differential Functions
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then for all $v = (v_1, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$,
> 
> $$
> D_v f(a) = \sum_{j = 1}^n v_j \partial_j f(a)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example.
> Consider again $f(x, y) = x^2 y$. Its partial derivatives are $\partial_1 f(x, y) = 2xy$ and $\partial_2 f(x, y) = x^2$ by standard differentiation rules. By the above theorem, if $f$ is differentiable, then it follows that 
> 
> $$
> D_{(1, 2)} f(x, y) = \partial_1 f(x, y) + 2\partial_2 f(x, y) = 2xy + 2x^2
> $$

## Geometry of Directional Derivatives

We want to expand out geometric intuition of directional and partial derivatives beyond real-valued functions. In general, for functions in $\mathbb{R}^m$, we cannot interpret the derivative as simply slice along a slice in a given direction. Thus, we try an alternative geometric formulation.

The directional derivative of $f$ at $a$ in the direction $v$ outputs a vector $D_v f(a)$ that is **tangent to the parametric curve** $\gamma(t) = f(a + tv)$ at $t = 0$

Informally speaking, this holds since 

$$
\gamma'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{\gamma(t) - \gamma(0)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a)}{t} = D_vf(a)
$$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Recall the polar coordinate transformation $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is defined by
> 
> $$
> f(r, \theta) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)
> $$
> 
> Assume $f$ is differentiable. Calculating its partial derivatives, you find that
> 
> $$
> \partial_1 f(r, \theta) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta), \partial_2 f(r, \theta) = (-r \sin \theta, r \cos \theta)
> $$
> 
> Then, by the above theorem, for any vector $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$
> 
> $$
> D_v f(r, \theta) = v_1 \begin{bmatrix}
> \cos \theta \\
> \sin \theta
> \end{bmatrix} + v_2 \begin{bmatrix}
> - r \sin \theta \\
> r\cos \theta
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> v_1 \cos \theta - v_2 r \sin \theta \\
> v_1 \sin \theta + v_2 r \cos \theta
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> What does this represent geometrically?
> 
> Fix a point $a \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and a vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Consider the curve $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $\gamma(t) = a+tv$ so $\gamma$ is a straight line passing through $a$. Think of this line as lying in the $(r, \theta)$-plane. This line $\ell = \{a + tv: t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in the $(r, \theta)$-plane is mapped via $f$ to the curve $f(\ell) = \{f(a + tv): t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ lying in the $(x, y)$-plane.
> 
> This is illustrated below for the choice $v = (0, 1)$.
> 
> ![[direction derivatives geometric 1.png]]
> 
> Then, the directional derivative is the tangent vector along this parameterized curve $f(\ell)$ at the point $f(a)$. This holds for any direction $v$.
> 
> ![[directional derivative geometric 2.png]]

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the vector-valued map $F: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $F(x, y) = (x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2)$. Notice $F$ is a parameterization of the graph of the real-valued function $f(x, y) = 9 - x^2 - y^2$. This graph can be written in parametric form as
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\} = F(\mathbb{R}^2)
> $$
> 
> Using the geometric viewpoint from above, we can interpret this geometrically using vectors tangent to curves on $S$.
> 
> Assume $F$ is differentiable so for any $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and any $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
> 
> $$
> D_vF(x, y) = v_1 \partial_1 F(x, y) + v_2 \partial_2 F(x, y) = (v_1, v_2, -2 v_1 x - 2 v_2 y) \in \mathbb{R}^3
> $$
> 
> Fix a point $a \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and a vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Consider the line $\ell = \{a + tv: t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in the $(x, y)$-plane is mapped via $F$ to the curve $F(\ell) = \{F(a + tv): t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ lying on the graph $S$ in $(x, y, z)$-space. The direction $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$ maps to the directional derivative $D_v F(a) \in \mathbb{R}^3$
> 
> ![[direction derivatives geometric 3.png]]

# Gradients

Before we explore the generalized definition of derivatives for a map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, it is worthwhile to formalize what we have learned with maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and directional derivatives.

Since we can write any directional derivative of a map $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable at $a$ as a linear combination of the partial derivatives of $f$ at $a$, the vector of partials $(\partial_1 f(a), \ldots, \partial_n f(a))$ captures all the necessary information about the change of $f$ in any direction. 

> [!NOTE] Definition: Gradients
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. The **gradient of $f$ at $a$** is denoted $\nabla f(a)$ and given by the $n$-dimensional vector
> 
> $$
> \nabla f(a) = (\partial_1 f(a), \ldots, \partial_n f(a))
> $$
> 
> provided the partial derivatives $\partial_1 f(a), \ldots, \partial_n f(a)$ all exist.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined by $f(x, y) = xy^2 + 2x$. Then $\partial_1f(x, y) = y^2 + 2$ and $\partial_2 f(x, y) = 2xy$, so $\nabla f(x, y) = (y^2 + 2, 2xy)$. For example, $\nabla f(-1, 2) = (6, -4)$.

Thus, we can rewrite the theorems about directional and partial derivatives in terms of the gradient.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then for all $v = (v_1, \ldots, v_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $D_vf(a)$ exists and
> 
> $$
> D_v f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot v = (\nabla f(a))^T v
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f(x, y) = xy^2 + 2x$. Assuming $f$ is differentiable at $(-1, 2)$, it follows that for any $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
> 
> $$
> D_vf(-1, 2) = (6, -4) \cdot (v_1, v_2) = 6v_1 - 4v_2
> $$
> 
> This allows you to linearly approximate values of $f$ near $(-1, 2)$. For example, since $(-0.9, 1.8)$ is equal to $(-1, 2) + (0.1, -0.2)$,
> 
> $$
> f(-0.9, 1.8) \approx f(-1, 2) + D_{0.1, -0.2} f(-2, 2) = -6 + 0.6 + 0.8 = -4.6
> $$

## Gradient Vector Field

The gradient takes as input a differentiable function $f$ and it outputs a vector field $\nabla f$. This operation is formalized in the following definition.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and let $f: U \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Assume all partial derivatives of $f$ exist on all of $U$. The **gradient of $f$** (or **gradient vector field of $f$**) is the function $\nabla f: U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ given by $\nabla f(a) = (\partial_1 f(a), \ldots, \partial_n f(a))$ for all $a \in U$.

You can plot the vector gradient fields for functions of two variables along with their contours and their graphs.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + 1$, so $\nabla f(x, y) = (2x, 2y)$. Here is a picture in $\mathbb{R}^2$ of its gradient vector field along with a few level sets.
> 
> ![[gradient 1.png]].
> 
> Notice that at a given point $a$, the gradient $\nabla f(a)$ appears to point in the direction of greatest change. This becomes more apparent when you plot the graph of $f$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and embed the gradient vector field below the graph.
> 
> ![[gradient 2.png]]
> 
> Also notice that at a given point $p$ on the level set, the direction of the gradient $\nabla f(p)$ appears orthogonal to the tangent line of the circle at the point.

## Direction of Steepest Ascent

As suggested by the above example, the gradient points in the **direction of the steepest ascent**.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Assume $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $\nabla f(a) \ne 0$. Both of the following hold:
> 
> 1. The maximum of $D_uf(a)$ over all unit vectors $u$ occurs when $u = \nabla f(a) / ||\nabla f(a)||$ and the maximum value is $||\nabla f(a)||$.
> 2. The minimum of $D_uf(a)$ over all unit vectors $u$ occurs when $u = -\nabla f(a) / ||\nabla f(a)||$ and the maximum value is $-||\nabla f(a)||$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> $D_uf(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u$ for any unit vector $u \in S^{n-1}$ From linear algebra, 
> 
> $$
> \nabla f(a) \cdot u = ||\nabla f(a)|| \cdot ||u|| \cos \theta = ||\nabla f(a)|| \cos \theta
> $$
> 
> where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f(a)$ and $u$. This quantity is maximized when $\theta = 0$ and minimized when $\theta = -\pi$. Therefore, the maximum value is $||\nabla f(a)||$ and the minimum value is $-||\nabla f(a)||$

This lemma formalizes two notions:

> First, the gradient $\nabla f(a)$ points in the direction of steepest ascent at $a$.
> Second, the norm $||\nabla f(a)||$ is the rate of change of $f$ in this direction.

## Orthogonality to Level Sets

For $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, recall that the level set of a scalar-valued function $f:A \to \mathbb{R}$ is given by

$$
S = \{x \in A, f(x) = c\} = f^{-1}(\{c\})
$$

Assuming $f$ is continuous, this set is written in implicit form. Based on previous examples, you may guess that the gradient of $f$ at a point $p \in S$ is "orthogonal" to this set $S$. 

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$. The level sets of $f$ are spheres in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
> 
> ![[gradients 3.png]]
> 
> Since $\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent of $f$, the vectors cross the level sets orthogonally. More precisely, at a given point $p$ on the level set, the direction of the gradient $\nabla f(p)$ appears orthogonal to the "tangent plane" at that point.
> 
> Consider the set 
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: f(x, y, z) = 3\} = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3\}
> $$
> 
> at the point $p = (1, 1, 1) \in S$. Notice $\nabla f(x, y, z) = (2x, 2y, 2z)$ so $\nabla f(p) = (2, 2, 2)$. To describe the "tangent plane" at $p$ such that $\partial f(p)$  is normal to the plane, we can take the following approach.
> 
> If $(x, y, z)$ is a point on the plane, then the vector $(x, y, z) - (1, 1, 1)$ lies inside the plane so $\partial f(p)$ should be orthogonal to it. That is, you may expect that
> 
> $$
> \nabla f(p) \cdot (x - 1, y - 1, z - 1) = 0
> $$
> 
> Using the normal form for a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, you might therefore guess that the "tangent plane" of $S$ at $p$ should be
> 
> $$
> \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 2(x-1) + 2(y-1) + 2(z-1) = 0\}
> $$
> 
> This conclusion is not rigorous at all; it is merely a well-founded conjecture for now.

The idea of "tangent" planes and vectors "tangent to a set" will be explored more rigorous in latter chapters. We can informally state our observations of the key properties of gradients in this chapter as:

> Every tangent vector $v$ at a point $p$ of a set $S$ written in implicit form is orthogonal to $\nabla f(p)$.
> 
> Moreover, the gradient $\nabla f(p)$ is orthogonal to the tangent plane of $S$ at $p$.

# Differentials and Jacobians

Generalization the derivative to any map poses quite a few issues in higher dimensions. Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a map and fix a point $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$. You might first guess that the derivative of $f$ at $a$ is

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

But this doesn't work since the quantity $h$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. We can try taking the norm of $h$:

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{||h||}
$$

But this limit will rarely exist. If we take $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ where $f(x) = x$ and $a = 0$, we have $\lim_{h \to 0} h/|h|$ which does not exist. We can consider the map

$$
v \mapsto D_vf(a)
$$

as the derivative of $f$ at $a$, but directional derivates don't have any natural relationship with each other, even if you assume they all exist.

## Definitions

The breakthrough comes from the concept of differentials, summarized as

> [!NOTE] Theorem, Summarized
> A map $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at the real number $a \in \mathbb{R}$ if and only if there exists a linear map $L: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\gamma(a + h) - \gamma(a) - L(h)}{h} = 0
> $$

The non-linear map $\gamma(a + h) - \gamma(a)$ is approximated by the linear map $L(h)$ **and** the error $\gamma(a + h) - \gamma(a) - L(h)$ tends to the zero vector faster than $h$ tends to the zero scalar.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Differentiability
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. A function $f$ is **differentiable at $a$** if there exists a linear map $L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{||h||} = 0
> $$
> 
> The linear map $L$ is the **differential of $f$ at $a$**, denoted $df_a$.

^00b51a

> [!TIP] Remark
> Other sources refer to $df_a$ as the “total derivative” of $f$ at $a$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Take the point $a = (1.5, 0.5)$ and define $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = \left(\dfrac{x^2 + y^2}{2}, xy\right)
> $$
> 
> As we will soon see, $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and the differential of $f$ at $a$ is given by $df_a: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ where
> 
> $$
> \forall h \in \mathbb{R}^2, df_a(h) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1.5 & 0.5 \\
> 0.5 & 1.5 \\
> \end{bmatrix}h
> $$
> 
> Thus, $df_a(h)$ approximates $f(a + h) - f(a)$ with error tending to zero faster than $||h||$ as $h \to 0$. What does this mean from a geometric perspective?
> 
> Write $h = (\Delta x, \Delta y)$ where $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are small positive real numbers. The two points $a$ and $a + h$ form opposite corners of a rectangle. You can plot how this rectangle transforms under the non-linear map $f$ compared to how it transforms under the linear map $L$.
> 
> ![[differential 1.png]]
> 
> The left grid has $a$ and $a + h$ on opposite corners of a rectangle. On the right, the blue square has been transformed by the non-linear map $f$ to the blue parallelogram and transformed by the linear map $L$ translated by $f(a)$. The top right corners are $f(a + h)$ and $f(a) +L(h)$ respectively. The difference between those two corners is the error.
> 
> The above diagram illustrates the choice $\Delta x = 1$ and $\Delta y = 0.75$. If you shrink those values to say $\Delta x = \Delta y = 0.5$, the approximation improves.
> 
> ![[differentials 2.png]]
> 
> This gives some intuition for what the differential $df_a$ represents geometrically. To prove it exists and compute it, we want to choose a linear map $L: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ such that $L(h)$ approximates $f(a + h) - f(a)$ with error tending to zero faster than $||h||$. Right now, you do not have a systematic method so you can try an ad hoc approach. Write $h = (\Delta x, \Delta y)$ where $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are small positive real numbers. Notice that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f(a + h) - f(a) &= f(1.5 + \Delta x, 0.5 + \Delta y) - f(1.5, 0.5) \\
> &= \begin{bmatrix}
> 1.5\Delta x + 0.5 \Delta y + 0.5 (\Delta x)^2 + 0.5 (\Delta y)^2 \\
> 0.5 \Delta x + 1.5 \Delta y + \Delta x \Delta y
> \end{bmatrix}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> You can separate the linear terms from the quadratic terms. As $h = (\Delta x, \Delta y) \to 0$, the quadratic terms tend to zero faster than the linear terms. Then, we get
> 
> $$
> f(a + h) - f(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1.5 & 0.5 \\
> 0.5 & 1.5 \\
> \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
> \Delta x \\
> \Delta y
> \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.5(\Delta x)^2 + 0.5 (\Delta y)^2 \\
> \Delta x \Delta y
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> This suggests defining $L: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by
> 
> $$
> L(h) =  \begin{bmatrix}
> 1.5 & 0.5 \\
> 0.5 & 1.5 \\
> \end{bmatrix}h
> $$
> 
> for all $h \in \mathbb{R}^2$. It then follows by standard limit laws that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L(h)}{||h||} = \lim_{(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0, 0)} \dfrac{1}{||(\Delta x, \Delta y)||} \begin{bmatrix}
> 0.5(\Delta x)^2 + 0.5 (\Delta y)^2 \\
> \Delta x \Delta y
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
  0 \\ 0
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> This proves that $L$ satisfies the definition of a differential, so $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and the differential $df_a$ exists and equals $L$.

Note that the definition of a differential implicit claims that if the differential exists, then it is unique. This requires proof.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Uniqueness of Differentials
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. If there exists linear maps $L_1: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and let $L_2: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L_1(h)}{||h||} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a) - L_2(h)}{||h||} = 0
> $$
> 
> then $L_1 = L_2$. In other words, the differential of $f$ at $a$, if it exists, is unique.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> By taking the difference of the two limits, it follows that
> 
> $$
> 0 = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{L_2(h) - L_1(h)}{||h||} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{T(h)}{||h||}
> $$
> 
> where $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is given by $T(v) = L_2(v) - L_1(v)$ for $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Since $T$ is the difference of two linear maps, it is linear and so $T(0) = 0$. It therefore suffices to prove that $T(v) = 0$ for all non-zero vectors $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
>  Fix $v \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$. Define the sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ by $x(k) = \tfrac{1}{k} v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ so $||x(k)|| = \tfrac{1}{k} ||v||$ and $x(k) \ne 0$.
> 
> This implies that $||x(k)|| \to 0$ and hence $x(k) \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$. By the sequential definition of the limit (as the limit exists) and the linearity of $T$, it follows that
> 
> $$
> 0 = \lim_{k \to \infty} \dfrac{T(x(k))}{||x(k)||} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \dfrac{T(\tfrac{1}{k}v)}{\tfrac{1}{k}||v||} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \dfrac{\tfrac{1}{k} T(v)}{\tfrac{1}{k}||v||} = \dfrac{T(v)}{||v||}
> $$
> 
> The last step uses a limit law for constant sequences. Thus, $T(v) = 0$ for $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ as required. $\blacksquare$

You will also need some terminology to discuss differentiability on subsets of a domain.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Differentiability on a Subset
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $S$ be a subset of the interior $A^o$. The function $f$ is **differentiable on $S$** if $f$ is differentiable at $a$ for every $a \in S$. The function $f$ is **differentiable** if $f$ is differentiable on its domain $A$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Note that the set $S$ is a subset of the interior of the domain because a function can only be differentiable at interior points. This also means the phrase "$f$ is differentiable" only makes sense if its domain $A$ is open.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $A = (0, \infty)$ and $B = [0, \infty)$. Define $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = x^2$ and $g: B \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x) = x^2$. Both $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $(0, \infty) = A^o = B^o$. Thus, the function $f$ is differentiable but $g$ is not. Notice $g$ cannot be differentiable at $0$ because $0$ is not an interior point of its domain $B$.
> 
> Also, you can still assert differentiability of non-open sets. For example, since $[1, 5) \subseteq (0, \infty) = A^o = B^o$, the maps $f$ and $g$ are both differentiable on the non-open set $[1, 5)$.

## Properties

Computing differentials can be a difficult task without more tools. At the very least, you can directly verify two special cases

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear map. For any $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$, you can prove that $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and that $df_a = f$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a parametric curve. You can check that the definition of differentiability in [[#^fe1b4f|Derivatives of a One-Variable Function]] is equivalent to the definition of [[#^00b51a|Differentiability]] above.

You can compute differentials component-by-component.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Component-wise Differentials
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Then $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if each of its component functions $f^1, f^2, \ldots, f^m$ are. If so
> 
> $$
> df_a = (df_a^1, \ldots, df_a^m)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Differentiability Implies Continuity
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Linearity of Differentials
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Fix $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. If $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ are differentiable at $a$, then the function $f + \lambda g$ is differentiable at $a$ and 
> 
> $$
> d(f + \lambda g)_a = df_a + \lambda dg_a
> $$

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Differentiability Implies Existence of Directional Derivatives
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then for $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the directional derivative $D_vf(a)$ exists and 
> 
> $$
> df_a(v) = D_vf(a)
> $$

^4b0589

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Assume $f$ is differentiable at $a$. Fix $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. By linearity, $df_a(tv) = tdf_a(v)$ for $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Thus,
> 
> $$
> D_vf(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0}\left[\dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{t} + df_a(v)\right]
> $$
> 
> By the limit addition law, it suffices to show that 
> 
> $$
> \lim_{t \to 0}\dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{t} = 0
> $$
> 
> For $i \in \mathbb{R}$, the identity $||tv|| = |t| \cdot ||v||$ implies that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \left[\left(\dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{||tv||}\right) \cdot \dfrac{|t|}{t} \cdot ||v||\right]
> $$
> 
> Since the quantity $\tfrac{|t|}{t} \cdot ||v||$ is bounded between $-||v||$ and $||v||$ as $t \to 0$, the result follows from the multivariable squeeze theorem provided
> 
> $$
> \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{||tv||} = 0
> $$
> 
> Define $F: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ by $F(a) = 0$ and, for $x \ne a$,
> 
> $$
> F(x) = \dfrac{f(x) - f(a) - df_a(x - a)}{||x - a||}
> $$
> 
> Since $f$ is differentiable at $a$, it follows that $F$ is continuous at $0$. As $a$ is an interior point of $A$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $\{a + tv: t \in (-\delta, \delta)\} \subseteq A$. Define $\gamma: (-\delta, \delta) \to \mathbb{R}^m$ by $\gamma(t) = a + tv$ so $\gamma$ is continuous and $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq A$. Since the composition of continuous functions is continuous, we have that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + tv) - f(a) - df_a(tv)}{||tv||} = \lim_{t \to 0} F(\gamma(t)) = F(\gamma(0)) = F(a) = 0
> $$
> 
> as required above. $\blacksquare$

The converse of the above theorem is false.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(0, 0) = 0$ and $f(x, y) = \dfrac{2x^2y}{x^4 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$. Let $v = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. You can check that $D_{(v_1, 0)}f(0, 0) = 0 and if $v_2 \ne 0$, then 
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> D_vf(0, 0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{f(tv_1, tv_2) - f(0, 0)}{t}
> &= \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{1}{t} \dfrac{2v_1^2v_2t^3}{v_1^4t^4+v_2^2t^2}\\ 
> &= \lim_{t \to 0} \dfrac{2v_1^2v_2}{v_1^4 t^2 + v_2^2} \\
> &= \dfrac{2v_1^2}{v_2}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> so every directional derivative exists. However, so you prove that $f$ is not continuous at $(0, 0)$ by considering the sequence $(x_k, y_k) = (1/k, 1/k^2)$ as $k \to \infty$. This implies that $f$ is not differentiable at $(0, 0)$.

## Matrix of the Differential

For a map $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and a point $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$, calculating the differential $df_a$ is equivalent to finding the matrix defining this linear map. Recall from linear algebra that the matrix of a linear map $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is defined using the standard basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. That is, the matrix of $T$ is precisely the $m \times n$ matrix.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\mid &  & \mid \\
T(e_1) & \cdots & T(e_n) \\
\mid &  & \mid \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Each partial derivative $\partial_j f(a) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ mirrors each column vector $T(e_j) \in \mathbb{R}^m$. This suggests a matrix of partial derivatives.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Jacobian
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. Assume all the partials of $f$ exist at $a$. The **Jacobian of $f$ at $a$** is the $m \times n$ matrix $Df(a)$ given by
> 
> $$
> Df(a) = [\partial_j f_i (a)]_{i, j} = \begin{bmatrix}
> \mid &  & \mid \\
> \partial_1 f(a) & \cdots & \partial_n f(a) \\
> \mid &  & \mid \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> \partial_1 f_1(a) & \cdots & \partial_n f_1(a) \\
> \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
> \partial_1 f_m(a) & \cdots & \partial_n f_m(a) \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> The Jacobian is also called the **Jacobian matrix**.

^9d5048

Since Jacobians are defined with partial derivatives, they are quick to compute.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $f(x, y, z) = (x^2y^2z, y + \sin z)$. To find its Jacobian, we begin by computing its partials
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \partial_1f_1(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} x^2y^2z = 2xy^2z \\
> \partial_2f_1(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} x^2y^2z = 2x^2yz \\
> \partial_3f_1(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} x^2y^2z = x^2y^2 \\
> \partial_1f_2(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} (y + \sin z) = 0 \\
> \partial_2f_2(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} (y + \sin z) = 1 \\
> \partial_3f_2(x, y, z) &= \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} (y + \sin z) = \cos z \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Then, at any $(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, this implies that
> 
> $$
> Df(x, y, z) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 2xy^2z & 2x^2yyz & x^2y^2 \\
> 0 & 1 & \cos z \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example: Functions of One Variable
> Let $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a parametric curve with domain $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Then, if $\gamma$ is differentiable at $a \in I$, the Jacobian of $\gamma$ at $a$ is 
> 
> $$
> D\gamma(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> \mid \\
> \partial_1 \gamma(a)\\
> \mid \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> \gamma'_1(a) \\
> \vdots \\
> \gamma'_m(a) \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \gamma'(a)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example: Real-Valued Functions
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function with $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If all the partials of $f$ exist at $a$, then the Jacobian of $1 \times n$ matrix
> 
> $$
> Df(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> \partial_1 f(a) & \cdots & \partial_n f(a)
> \end{bmatrix} = \nabla f(a)^T
> $$
> 
> In other words, the Jacobian is the transpose of the gradient in this case.

> [!NOTE] Example: Linear Maps
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a linear map, so $f(x) = Ax$ for some $m \times n$ matrix $A$. For any $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$, you can verify by direct computation that $Df(p) = A$.

The Jacobian and differential are connected.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a$ be an interior point of $A$. If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ then the matrix of the differential of $f$ at $a$ is the $m \times n$ Jacobian matrix of $f$ at $a$. In other words,
> 
> $$
> \forall v \in \mathbb{R}^n, df_a(v) = Df(a)v
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ be the standard basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then, by the [[#^9d5048|definition of the Jacobian]] and since [[#^4b0589|differentiability implies the existence of directional derivatives]],
> 
> $$
> \forall j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}, df_a(e_j) = D_{e_j} f(a) = \partial_j f(a) = Df(a) e_j
> $$
> 
> The result then follows from linear algebra. since the matrix of $df_a$ is the $m \times n$ matrix 
> 
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}
> \mid &  & \mid \\
> df_a(e_1) & \cdots & df_a(e_n) \\
> \mid &  & \mid \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> $\blacksquare$

This theorem implies the following chain of relationships.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> If $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at $a$ and $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, then
> 
> $$
> df_a(v) = Df(a) v = D_vf(a) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j \partial_j f(a)
> $$
> 
> This connects differentials, Jacobians, directional derivatives, and partial derivatives! Assuming differentiability, we can now easily compute differentials using the Jacobian.

> [!NOTE] Example
> If we assume that $f(x, y, z) = (x^2 y^2 z, y + \sin z)$ is differentiable at $(1, 2, 0)$, then
> 
> $$
> \forall v \in \mathbb{R}^3, df_{(1, 2, 0)}(v) = Df(1, 2, 0)v = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 0 & 4 \\
> 0 & 1 & 1 \\
> \end{bmatrix}v
> $$
