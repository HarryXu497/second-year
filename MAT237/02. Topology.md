# Interior, Boundary, and Closure

Our guiding question for this chapter will be

> How can you formalize the ideas of "inside", "outside", and "edge" for any set in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The solution will be to **zoom in** near a point. The rigorous formulation of this technique will be achieved with shrinking open balls (analogous to $\varepsilon$ in single-variable calculus). Illustrated below is a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ and three different points $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}^2$ pictured four times. Each time, balls with smaller and smaller radii are drawn at each point.

This picture will serve as the inspiration for nearly all the definitions that you will discover in this section.

## Interior

A point $p$ should be "inside" a region $A$ if, once you zoom in close enough on $p$, all you see is the region $A$. This idea can be formalized using a sufficiently small open ball.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Interior Points
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. A point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an **interior point** of $A$ if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(p) \subseteq A$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $p = 2$ is an interior point of the interval $A = [1, 4)$ since $B_{1/2}(2) = (1.5, 2.5)$ is a subset of $A$. On the other hand, $5$ is not an interior point of $A$. To prove this, we need to verify the negation:
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon > 0, B_{\varepsilon}(5) = (5 - \varepsilon, 5 + \varepsilon) \nsubseteq [1, 4) = A
> $$
> 
> Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Set $x = 5$. Then $x \in (5 - \varepsilon, 5 + \varepsilon)$ yet $x \notin [1, 4)$. Hence $B_\varepsilon(5) \nsubseteq [1, 4)$, so $5% is not an interior point of $A$.
> 
> You can also check that the end point $1$ is not an interior point of $[1, 4)$. Follow a similar argument as above, with $x = 1 - \varepsilon/2$. Then $x \in (1- \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$ yet $x \notin [1, 4)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$ with $c < d$. Let $p \in \mathbb{R}$. If $c < p < d$ then you can prove that $p$ is an interior point of the closed interval $[c, d]$ i.e. 
> 
> $$
> \exists \varepsilon > 0 \text{ such that } B_{\varepsilon}(p) \subseteq [c, d]
> $$
> 
> Take $\varepsilon = \min\{p - c, d - p\} > 0$. Then
> - $\varepsilon \le p - c \implies p - \varepsilon \ge  p - (p - c) = c$
> - $\varepsilon \le d - p \implies p + \varepsilon \le  p + (d - p) = d$
> 
> This means that $B_{\varepsilon}(p) = (p - \varepsilon, p + \varepsilon) \subseteq [c, d]$.

Proving an open ball is a subset of another set is trickier in higher dimensions

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $p = (1, 0)$ is an interior point of the left half-plane
> 
> $$
> A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x \le 2\}
> $$
> 
> Take $\varepsilon = 1/2$. Then you have to prove that for all points $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
> 
> $$
> (x, y) \in B_{1/2}((1, 0)) \implies (x, y) \in A
> $$
> 
> **Proof**. Let $(x, y) \in B_{1/2}((1, 0))$ so $(x-1)^2 + y^2 < (1/2)^2$. Since $y^2 \ge 0$, this implies that
> 
> $$
> (x-1)^2 < \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 \implies x- 1 < \dfrac{1}{2} \implies x < 1 + \dfrac{1}{2} \le 2
> $$
> 
> Thus, $(x, y) \in A$ as required. Therefore, $(1, 0)$ is an interior point of $A$.

The definition of interior point leads to the concept of the "inside" of a set.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Interior of a Set
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. The **interior** of $A$, denoted $A^o$ or $\mathrm{int}(A)$, is the set of interior points of $A$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The interior of $A = [1, 4)$ is the open interval $A^o = (1, 4)$. First, you can prove that $(1, 4) \subseteq A^o$ or, more formally,
> 
> $$
> \forall x \in (1, 4), \exists \varepsilon > 0 \text{ s.t. }(x - x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon) \subseteq [1, 4)
> $$
> 
> Let $x \in (1, 4)$. Set $\varepsilon = \min\{\tfrac{x-1}{2}, \tfrac{4-x}{2}\} > 0$. Then,
> 
> - Since $x > 1$ and $\varepsilon \le \tfrac{x-1}{2}$, it follows that
> 
>   $$
>   x - \varepsilon \ge x - \dfrac{x-1}{2} = \dfrac{x+1}{2} > 1 - \dfrac{1-1}{2} = 1
>   $$
> 
>  - Since $x < 4$ and $\varepsilon \le \tfrac{4-x}{2}$, it follows that
> 
>   $$
>    x + \varepsilon \le x + \dfrac{4-x}{2} = \dfrac{4+x}{2} < 4 + \dfrac{4-4}{2} = 4
>   $$
> 
> Together, this implies that $(x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon) \subseteq [1, 4)$ as required.
> 
> The other direction remains to be proven. We need to show that $A^o \subseteq (1, 4)$. Since $A^o \subseteq A = [1, 4)$ (See the below lemma on interior properties), it suffices to prove that $1 \notin A^o$. 
> 
> We want to show that $1$ is not an interior point i.e. $\forall \varepsilon > 0, B_\varepsilon(1) = (1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \nsubseteq [1, 4)$
> 
> Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be arbitrary. Take $x = 1 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$. Then, $x \in B_\varepsilon(1) = (1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$ yet $x \notin [1, 4)$. Therefore, $B_\varepsilon(1) = (1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \nsubseteq [1, 4)$, as required.
> 
> This means that $1 \notin A^o$, completing the proof. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> The interior of $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x \le 2\}$ is the set $A^o = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x < 2\}$.
> 
> **Proof**. We start by proving that $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x < 2\} \subseteq A^o$. Let $(x, y) \in \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x < 2\}$. We need to show that $(x, y)$ is an interior point of $A$:
> 
> $$
> \exists \varepsilon > 0 \text{ s.t. }B_{\varepsilon}((x, y)) \subseteq A
> $$
> 
> Take $\varepsilon = \tfrac{2 - x}{2}$. Then, 
> 
> $$
> B_{\varepsilon}((x, y)) = \{(x_1, y_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x_1-x)^2 + (y_1-y)^2 < \varepsilon^2 \}
> $$
> 
> Let $(x_1, y_1) \in B_{\varepsilon}((x, y))$. Then $(x_1-x)^2 + (y_1-y)^2 < \varepsilon^2$. Since $x < 2$ and $(y_1 - y)^2 \ge 0$, then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> (x_1-x)^2 + (y_1-y)^2 < \varepsilon^2 &\implies (x_1-x)^2  < \varepsilon^2 \\
> &\implies |x_1-x|  < \varepsilon \\
> &\implies x_1 < x + \varepsilon \\
> &\implies x_1 < x + \tfrac{2 - x}{2} \\
> &\implies x_1 < \tfrac{x + 2}{2} \\
> &\implies x_1 < \tfrac{2 + 2}{2} \\
> &\implies x_1 < 2\\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Since $x_1 < 2$, $(x_1, y_1) \in A$. Since $(x_1, y_1)$ was arbitrarily in $B_{\varepsilon}((x, y))$, $B_{\varepsilon}((x, y)) \subseteq A$.
> 
> Now, we must prove that $A^o \subseteq \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x < 2\}$. By the below lemma on properties of interiors, we know that $A^o \subseteq A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x \le 2\}$. Thus, we only need to prove that $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x = 2\} \cap A^o = \emptyset$ i.e.
> 
> $$
> \forall (x_2, y_2) \in \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x = 2\}, (x_2, y_2) \notin A^o
> $$
> 
> Let $(x_2, y_2) \in \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x = 2\}$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be arbitrary. Then
> 
> $$
> B_\varepsilon((x_2, y_2)) = \{(x_3, y_3) \in \mathbb{R}^2: (x_3 - x_2)^2 + (y_3 - y_2)^2 < \varepsilon^2 \}
> $$
> 
> Take $(x_3, y_3) = (x_2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}, y_2)$. Then, since $\varepsilon > 0$, we have
> 
> $$
> (x_3 - x_2)^2 + (y_3 - y_2)^2 = (x_2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} - x_2)^2 + (y_2 - y_2)^2 = (\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})^2 < \varepsilon^2
> $$
> 
> It then follows that $(x_3, y_3) \in B_\varepsilon((x_2, y_2))$. Yet, since $\varepsilon > 0$ and $x_2 = 2$, $x_3 = x_2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} > 2$ and so $(x_3, y_3) \notin A$.
> 
> This means that $B_\varepsilon((x_2, y_2)) \nsubseteq A$ for an arbitrary $\varepsilon$, which implies $(x_2, y_2) \notin A^o$ for an arbitrary point $(x_2, y_2) \in \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x = 2\}$. Thus, $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x = 2\} \cap A^o = \emptyset$ and $A^o \subseteq \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x < 2\}$.
> 
> This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a list of examples of interiors of sets. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$.
> 
> - The interior of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> - The interior of any finite set is empty.
> - The interior of the open ball $B_r(a)$ is the open ball itself.
> - The interior of the closed ball $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| \le r\}$ is the open ball $B_r(a)$.
> - The interior of any sphere is empty.
> - The interior of the hypercube $[c, d]^n$ is the open hypercube $(c, d)^n$, where $c < d$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The interior of the set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ is empty. A full formal proof is reserved for a course in topology or analysis, but here is a nearly complete argument.
> 
> Let $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ so $q$ is a rational number. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. The interval $B_\varepsilon(q) = (q - \varepsilon, q + \varepsilon)$ myst contain irrational numbers, so $B_\varepsilon(q)$ cannot be a subset of $\mathbb{Q}$. Thus, $q$ is not an interior point and the interior set of $\mathbb{Q}$ is empty.
> 
> Sets like $\mathbb{Q}$ are notorious counterexamples to many statements that you shall later see, so it is good to keep in your back pocket.

The interior satisfies natural properties with respect to other set operations.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> 1. $A^o \subseteq A$
> 2. $A^o \cup B^o \subseteq (A \cup B)^o$
> 3. $(A \cap B)^o = A^o \cap B^o$
> 4. $(A \times B)^o = A^o \times B^o$

With the "inside" of a set formalized as the interior, you can study the "edge" of a set.

## Boundary

A point $p$ should be at the "edge" of a region $A$ if, no matter how close you zoom into $p$, you can see points nearby inside $A$ and outside $A$. This idea can be formalized as follows.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Boundary Points
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. A point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **boundary point** of $A$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the sets $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A$ and $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A^c$ are both non-empty i.e. $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A \ne \emptyset$ and $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A^c \ne \emptyset$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $p = 1$ is a boundary point of the interval $A = [1, 4)$. Fix. $\varepsilon > 0$. Then
> 
> $$
> B_\varepsilon(p) = (1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)
> $$
> 
> First, we show that $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A$ is non-empty. Take $x = 1$. Then, $x \in A$ and $x \in B_\varepsilon(p)$.
> 
> Second, we show that $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A^c$. Take $y = 1 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$. Then, $x \notin A \implies x \in A^c$ and $x \in  B_\varepsilon(p)$.
> 
> The sets $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A$ and $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A^c$ are both non-empty. Thus, $1$ is a boundary point of $[1, 4)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $p = (2, 1)$ is the boundary point of the set $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x \le 2\}$. To prove this, fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Then,
> 
> $$
> B_\varepsilon(p) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: (x-2)^2 + (y-1)^2 < \varepsilon^2\}
> $$
> 
> First, we show that $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap A$ is non-empty. Take $(x_1, y_1) = (2, 1)$. Then, $(x_1, y_1) \in B_\varepsilon(p)$ and $(x_1, y_1) \in A$ (since $x_1 \le 1$).
> 
> Second, we show that $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap A^c$ is non-empty. Take $(x_2, y_2) = (2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}, 1)$. Then,  $(x_2, y_2) \in B_\varepsilon(p)$ and $(x_1, y_1) \notin A \implies (x_1, y_1) \in A^c$.
> 
> The sets $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A$ and $B_{\varepsilon}(p) \cap A^c$ are both non-empty.  This completes the proof.
>

Boundary points product the concept of the "edge" of a set.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Boundary of a Set
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. The **(topological) boundary** of $A$, denoted $\partial A$, is the set of boundary points of $A$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The boundary of $A = [1, 4)$ is the pair of endpoints $\partial A = \{1, 4\}$

> [!NOTE] Example
> The boundary of the left half plane $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x \le 2\}$ is the vertical line $\partial A = \{(2, y) : y \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are a list of examples of boundaries of sets. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$.
> 
> - The boundary of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is empty.
> - The boundary of a finite set $A$ is $A$ itself.
> - The boundary of the closed interval $[c, d]$ is the finite set $\{c, d\}$
> - The boundary of the open ball $B_r(a)$ is the sphere $\partial B_r(a) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| = r\}$.
> - The boundary of $B_1(0)$ is the unit sphere $\partial B_1(0) = S^{n-1}$.
> - The boundary of any sphere is the sphere itself.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The boundary of $\mathbb{Q}$ is all of $\mathbb{R}$. Again, a fully formal proof is reserved for a course in topology or analysis, but here's a nearly complete proof. 
> 
> Fix $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. The open interval $B_\varepsilon(x) = (x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon)$ contains both rational and irrational points since $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}^c = \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ are "dense" in $\mathbb{R}$.
> 
> In other words, $B_\varepsilon(x) \cap \mathbb{Q}$ and $B_\varepsilon(x) \cap \mathbb{Q}^c$ are both non-empty so $x \in \partial \mathbb{Q}$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, this proves that $\partial \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{R}$.
> 
> This means that the set of rational $\mathbb{Q}$ has an empty interior, yet its boundary is all of $\mathbb{R}$.

The boundary also satisfies natural properties with respect to many set operations. 

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then
> 
> 1. $\partial(A \cup B) \subseteq \partial A \cup \partial B$
> 2. $\partial(A \cap B) \subseteq \partial A \cup \partial B$
> 3. $\partial(A \times B) = (\partial A \times B) \cup (A \times \partial B) \cup (\partial A \times \partial B)$

Now, for a given set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, the "inside" is the interior, the "edge" is the boundary $\partial S$, and the "outside" is the interior of the complement $(\mathbb{R}^n \setminus S)^o$. This addresses the original motivation of this section, but there is a closely related concept you need to investigate.

## Closure

Defining limits on a domain in $\mathbb{R}^n$ presents a new issue. Over $\mathbb{R}$, we can take the limit of a function at a point $p$ provided an open interval centered at $p$, like $(p - \varepsilon, p + \varepsilon)$, belonged to the domain of the function. This is because there are only two ways to approach a point on the real number line.

This changes in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $n \ge 2$: you can approach a point in infinitely many ways. If the domain of our function is a complicated set in $\mathbb{R}^n$, how do you decide where you can take limits of your function? You must determine which points $p$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be approach by points only from $A$.

Intuitively, a point $p$ can be approached via a region $A$ if, no matter how close you zoom into $p$, you can see points of $A$ not including $p$ itself. This idea needs to be formalized if you want to rigorously study limits.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Limit Points
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. A point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$  is a **limit point** of $A$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\}$ contains points in $A$ i.e. $(B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\}) \cap A \ne \emptyset$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $p=2$ is a limit point of $A = [1, 4)$. To prove this, fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Set $x = \max\{1, 2 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\}$. Then
> - $x \ge 2 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \text{ and } \varepsilon > 0 \implies 2 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \le x < 2 \implies x \in B_\varepsilon(2) = (2 - \varepsilon, 2 + \varepsilon)$, and
> - $x \ge 1 \text{ and } \varepsilon > 0 \implies 1 \le x < 2 \implies x \in [1, 4)$
> 
> so the set $B_\varepsilon(2) \setminus \{2\}$ contains points in $[1, 4)$. A similar argument can also show that $1$ or $4$ is a limit point.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The point $(0, 0)$ is not a limit point of 
> 
> $$
> A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 < 2\} \cup \{(0, 0)\}
> $$
> 
> because the punctured open disk 
> 
> $$
> B_{1/2}((0, 0)) \setminus \{(0, 0)\} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 0 < x^2 + y^2 < (1/2)^2\}
> $$
> 
> does not contain any points of $A$. The set of limit points of $A$ is $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 2\}$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a list of examples of sets of limit points. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$.
> 
> - The set of limit points of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> - The set of limit points of any finite set is empty.
> - The set of limit points of the open ball $B_r(a)$ is the closed ball $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| \le r\}$.
> - The set of limit points of any sphere is the sphere itself.
> - The set of limit points of any (closed) rectangle is the rectangle itself.

Note that the set of limit points of a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ does not actually contain $A$ itself.  This caveat will be a bit of a nuisance when $A$ is the domain of a function, since you may want to evaluate a function at all points of $A$. You can resolve this issue with a simple definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Closures
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. The **closure** of $A$, denoted $\overline A$ or $\mathrm{cl}(A)$ is the union of the set $A$ and the set of limit points of $A$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> The set of limit points of $A$ is often denoted $A^*$ so $\overline A = A \cup A^*$.

> [!NOTE]
> The closure of $A = [1, 4)$ is the set $\overline A = [1, 4]$ since its limit points are $A^* = \{1, 4\}$. On the other hand, the closure of $B = [1, 4) \cup \{5\}$ is the set $\overline B = [1, 4] \cup \{5\}$ even though its set of limit points $B^* = \{1, 4\}$ is the same as $A^*$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a list of examples of closures. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$.
> 
> - The set of limit points of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R}^n$.
> - The set of limit points of any finite set is the set itself.
> - The set of limit points of the open ball $B_r(a)$ is the closed ball $\overline {B_r(a)} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| \le r\}$.
> - The set of limit points of any sphere is the sphere itself.
> - The set of limit points of any (closed) rectangle is the rectangle itself.
> - The closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R}$.

Again, closure respects natural set operations.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then
> 
> 1. $A \subseteq \overline A$
> 2. $\mathrm{cl}(A \cup B) = \overline A \cup \overline B$
> 3. $\mathrm{cl}(A \cap B) \subseteq \overline A \cap \overline B$
> 4. $\mathrm{cl}(A \times B) = \overline A \times \overline B$

Despite starting with independent definitions, the interior, boundary, and closure of a set possess all the relationships that you might guess.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> For any set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, all of the following hold:
> 
> 1. $A^o \subseteq A \subseteq \overline A$
> 2. $A^o \cap \partial A = \emptyset$
> 3. $\overline A = A^o \cup \partial A$
> 4. $\partial A = \overline A \setminus A^o$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> The first statement follows from previous lemma about the relationships of interiors and closures. The second statement follows quickly from the definitions of interior and boundary. The fourth statements follows quickly from the second and third as well as basic properties of sets. It remains to prove the third statement. We proceed by proving both set containments.
> 
> We begin by proving that $A^o \cup \partial A \subseteq \overline A$. Since $A^o \subseteq A \subseteq \overline A$, it suffices to show that $\partial A \setminus A \subseteq \overline A$.
> 
> Fix $p \in \partial A$ with $p \notin A$. This implies for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\}$ contains points of $A$. Therefore, $p$ is a limit point of $A$ and hence $p \in \overline A$ by definition. This proves that $A^o \cup \partial A \subseteq \overline A$.
> 
> We next prove that $\overline A \subseteq A^o \cup \partial A$ by showing $A \subseteq A^o \cup \partial A$ and $A^* \setminus A \subseteq \partial A$.
> 
> Fix $p \in A$. If there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(p) \subseteq A$ then $p \in A^o$ as required. Otherwise, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the ball $B_\varepsilon(p)$ is not a subset of $A$, but $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap A$ is non-empty as it contains $p$. Therefore, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, both $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap A$ and $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap A^c$ are non-empty so $p \in \partial A$ by definition. This proves $A \subseteq A^o \cup \partial A$.
> 
> Fix $q \in A^*$ with $q \notin A$. By definition of a limit point, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the punctured open ball $B_\varepsilon(q) \setminus \{q\}$ contains points in $A$ in which case $B_\varepsilon(q) \cap A$  is non-empty. Moreover, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the set $B_\varepsilon(q) \cap A^c$ is non-empty since $q \in A^c$. By definition, $q \in \partial A$ which proves that  $A^* \setminus A \subseteq \partial A$ as desired. $\blacksquare$

Now, these definitions were described in terms of open balls, but there is another equally valuable perspective in terms of sequences. These will be explored next.

# Sequences

The limit is the fundamental notion which drives calculus. So far, we've seen a description of limit points in terms of open balls. There are equivalent definitions with sequences, and these will prove to be quite useful for practical and theoretical purposes.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Sequences
> A **sequence** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a function with domain $\{k \in \mathbb{Z}: k \ge k_n\}$ for some fixed $k_0 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and codomain $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Since these are so ubiquitous, there are lots of different conventions and choices of notation. There are many ways to specify a sequence including
> 
> $$
> (x(k)) \quad (x(k))_k \quad (x(k))_{k = k_0}^\infty \quad \{x(k)\}_k \quad \{x(k)\}_{k = k_0}^\infty
> $$
> 
> It is also common to write $x_k$ instead of $x(k)$. Unless stated otherwise, you may assume the domain of a sequence $\mathbb{N}^+ = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$.

As with sequences in $\mathbb{R}$, you can specify what it means to "pick terms from a sequence".

> [!NOTE] Definition: Subsequences
> Let $x: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a sequence and let $m: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ be a strictly increasing function. The sequence $\{x(m(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ is a **subsequence** of the sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> This definition can be modified to allow for domains for $x$ and $m$ that are subsets of which are not necessarily $\mathbb{N}+$. At minimum, the domain of $x$ must equal the codomain of $m$. This version with $\mathbb{N}^+$ is stated for simplicity but you are free to define subsequences and sequences with different domains.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the sequence $x(k) = k^2$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$. The subsequence of even terms $x(2), x(4), x(6), \ldots$ is defined using the strictly increasing function $m: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ given by $m(k) = 2k$. Then, $x(m(k)) = x(2k) = 4k^2$.
> 
> Subsequences do not need to follow "nice" patterns like even numbers. We can, for example, define $p: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ so that $p(k)$ is the $k$th prime. The first few terms of the subsequence $\{x(p(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ are $4, 9, 25, 49, \ldots$.

These core definitions lead to a natural generalization of convergence.

## Convergence of Sequences

A term $x(k)$ in a sequence often represents a finite approximation to some desired value. The next term $x(k + 1)$ in a sequence is usually a refined version of the previous term. Intuitively, the limit $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (if it exists) is the result of refining these finite approximations to arbitrary accuracy.

Using this intuition and the definition of convergent sequences in $\mathbb{R}$, we can generalize the notion of convergent sequences to $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Convergence of Sequences
> Fix a point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$. A sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ **converges to** $p$ provided
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists K \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. }\forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K \implies ||x(k) - p|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> If the above holds, write $\lim_{k \to \infty}x(k) = p$ or equivalently $x(k) \to p$. You can also say that the limit of the sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ is $p$.

This definition can be visualized in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the diagram below.

![[convergence of sequences.png]]

This picture inspires a geometric interpretation with shrinking balls in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> A sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ converges to $p$ if and only if every open ball centered at $p$ contains all but finitely many points of the sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ given by $x(k) = (2 + \tfrac{1}{k}, \tfrac{\sin k}{k})$ converges to $(2, 0)$.
> 
> Proof. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $K = \lceil \sqrt{2}/\varepsilon\rceil + 1$. Let $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ satisfy $k \ge K$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||x(k) - p|| = ||(2 + \tfrac) - (2, 0)|| &= \sqrt{(2 + \tfrac{1}{k} - 2)^2 + (\tfrac{\sin k}{k})^2} \\
> &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1 + \sin^2 k}{k^2}} \\
> &\le \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{k^2}} \tag{$0 \le \sin^2 k \le 1$}\\
> &< \varepsilon \tag{$k > \sqrt{2} / \varepsilon$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> so $||x(k) - p|| < \varepsilon$. By definition, $\lim_{k \to \infty}x(k) = p$ as desired.

It will be helpful to refer to convergence without knowing the specific limiting value.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ be a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ **converges** if there exists $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\lim_{k \to \infty} x(k) = p$. Otherwise, the sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ **diverges**.

> [!TIP] Remark
> If $\{x(k)\}_k$ converges (resp. diverges), then you can equivalently say the limit of the sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ exists (resp. does not exist).

Many of the same facts about limits of sequences over $\mathbb{R}$ carry over with almost identical arguments. First and foremost, limits of sequences are unique.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Uniqueness of Limits
> Fix $p, q \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ be a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $\lim_{k \to \infty} x(k) = p$ and $\lim_{k \to \infty} x(k) = q$, then $p = q$.
> 
> Proof. Assume $\lim_{k \to \infty} x(k) = p$ and $\lim_{k \to \infty} x(k) = q$. This means:
> 
> 1. $\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists K_1 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. }\forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K_1 \implies ||x(k) - p|| < \varepsilon$.
> 2. $\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists K_2 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. }\forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K_2 \implies ||x(k) - q|| < \varepsilon$
> 
> Assume for contradiction that $p \ne q$. Then, $||p - q|| > 0$ and we can take $\varepsilon = ||p - q||/3$. Substituting this value of $\varepsilon$ into assumptions 1 and 2 above, we get the following true statements.
> 
> 1. $\exists K_1 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. }\forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K_1 \implies ||x(k) - p|| < \dfrac{||p - q||}{3}$.
> 2. $\exists K_2 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. }\forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K_2 \implies ||x(k) - q|| < \dfrac{||p - q||}{3}$
> 
> Take $k = \max\{K_1, K_2\}$. Then $k \ge K_1$ and $k \ge K_2$ and, as such,
> 
> 1. $||x(k) - p|| < \dfrac{||p - q||}{3}$
> 2. $||x(k) - q|| < \dfrac{||p - q||}{3}$
> 
> We derive the following contradiction, in part, using these two statements.
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||p - q|| &= ||p - x(k) + x(k) - q|| \\
> &\le ||x(k) - p|| + ||x(k) - q|| \tag{triangle inequality} \\
> &< \dfrac{||p - q||}{3} + \dfrac{||p - q||}{3} \\
> &< \dfrac{2}{3}||p - q||
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> We end up with
> 
> $$
> ||p - q|| < \dfrac{2}{3} ||p - q||
> $$
> 
> which is a contradiction since $||p - q|| \ge 0$. Thus, we must have $p = q$ and the statement is true. $\blacksquare$

Moreover, subsequences of convergent sequences also converge to the same value.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ be a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ converging to $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Every subsequence of $\{x(k)\}$ also converges to $p$.

There is also a standard list of limit laws for sequences in higher dimensions.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Fix $p, q \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ and $\{y(k)\}_k$ be sequences in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> 1. **Constant**. If $x(k) = p$ for all except finitely many $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, then $x(k) \to p$.
> 2. **Linearity**. If $x(k) \to p$ and $y(k) \to q$, then $x(k) + \lambda y(k)$.
> 3. **Dot Product**. If $x(k) \to p$ and $y(k) \to q$, then $x(k) \cdot y(k) \to p \cdot q$.

This correspondence between $|\cdot|$in and $||\cdot||$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ demonstrates that, with good definitions, generalizing ideas from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$ is often a matter of notation. As you shall see in the next subsection, this connection can be formalized.

## Components of Sequences

A sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ an be thought of as $n$ sequences in $\mathbb{R}$. Namely, a sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ can be written as

$$
x(k) = (x_1(k), x_2(k), \ldots, x_n(k)) \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

so the $n$ sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ are given by $\{x_1(k)\}_k, \ldots, \{x_n(k)\})k$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the sequence $\{(\cos k, \sin k)\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. In this case, $x(k) = (\cos k, \sin k)$ so $x_1(k) = \cos k$ and $x_2(k) = \sin k$. The first few terms of $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ are:
> 
> $$
> x(1) = (\cos 1, \sin 1), \quad x(2) = (\cos 2, \sin 2), \ldots
> $$

This viewpoint allows you to formally connect the notions of convergence between $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ in a groundbreaking way.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Component-Wise Convergence
> Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ be a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $x(k) = (x_1(k), \ldots, x_n(k))$. Fix $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ converges to $p$ if and only if $\{x_i(x)\}_k$ converges to $p_i$ for all $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$.

Its proof illuminates a way to relate distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ to the corresponding $n$ distances in $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> 
> **Forward direction**. Assume $\{x(k)\}_k$ converges to $a=(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Fix $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ and fix $\varepsilon > 0$. It suffices to prove $x_i(k) \to a_i$ as $k \to \infty$. Since $x(k) \to a$, there exists $K \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
> 
> $$
> k \ge K \to ||x(k) - a|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> Take this same $K$ and let $k \in \mathbb{N}$ satisfy $k \ge K$. Then
> 
> $$
> |x_i(k) - a| = \sqrt{|x_i(k) - a_i|^2} \ge \sqrt{|x_1(k) - a_1|^2 + \cdots + |x_n(k) - a_n|^2} = ||x(k) - a
> $$
> 
> Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary, this proves that $x_i(k) \to a_i$ as $k \to \infty$. Since $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ was arbitrary, this proves the desired implication.
> 
> **Reverse direction**. Assume $\{x_i(k)\}$ converges for all $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. For each $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, let $a_i \in \mathbb{R}$ be the limit of $\{x_i(k)\}_k$. Let $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. It suffices to prove that $x(k) \to a$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. For $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, $x_i(k) \to a_i$, there exists $K_i \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
> 
> $$
> k \ge K_i \implies |x_i(k) - a| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt n}
> $$
> 
> Take $K = \max\{K_1, \ldots, K_n\}$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||x(k) - a|| &= \sqrt{|x_1(k) - a_1|^2 + \cdots + |x_n(k) - a_n|^2} \\
> &\le \sqrt{n \max_{1 \le i \le n} |x_i(k) - a_i|^2} \\
> &< \sqrt{n \cdot \dfrac{\varepsilon^2}{n}} = \varepsilon
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as $k \ge K \ge K_i$ for all $i \le i \le n$. This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

It will be a recurring theme to use components to reduce theorems over $\mathbb{R}^n$ to theorems over $\mathbb{R}$.

## Interior Points, Boundary Points, and Limit Points

A sequence of points $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ often arises in optimization problems or search algorithms. In those cases, your approximations ay only naturally lie in a particular set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ such as the domain of your optimization function.

Sometimes these approximation will converge to a point in the interior of $A$. Sometimes this limit of this sequence may fall on the boundary of $A$. This perspective leads to an equivalent formulation for interior points, boundary points, and limit points.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Sequence Definition of Interior Points, Boundary Points, and Limit Points
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. Let $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a point.
> 
> 1. The point $p$ is an interior point of $A$ if and only if for every sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ of points converging to $p$, there exists $K \in \mathbb{N}^+$ such that $\{x(k)\}_{k=K}^\infty \subseteq A$.
> 2. The point $p$ is a boundary point of $A$ if and only if there exists a sequence of points in $A$ converging to $p$ and there exists a sequence of points in $A^c$ converging to $p$.
> 3. The point $p$ is a limit point of $A$ if and only if there exists a sequence of points in $A \setminus \{p\}$ which converges to $p$.

It is easy to exhibit limit points in explicit examples with this theorem.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $A \in \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 < 2\} \cup \{(0, 0)\}$. You can quickly check that $(1, 1)$ is a limit point of $A$ using the sequential definition. Define the sequence
> 
> $$
> x(k) = (1 - \tfrac{1}{4k}, 1 - \tfrac{1}{4k})
> $$
> 
> for $k \ge 1$ so $x(k) \to (1, 1)$ as $k \to \infty$ by component-wise convergence. Moreover, $x(k) \in A \setminus \{(1, 1)\}$ since for $k \ge 1$,
> 
> $$
> 2 > (1 - \tfrac{1}{4k})^2 + (1 - \tfrac{1}{4k})^2 \ge 2(\tfrac{3}{4})^2 > 1
> $$
> 
> Thus, $(1, 1)$ is a limit point of $A$ by the sequence definition.

The proof of this theorem requires a clever idea.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We only prove (3) since (1) and (2) are similar.
> 
> **Reverse direction**. Let $\{x(k)\}_n$ be a sequence of points in $A \setminus \{p\}$ which converge to $p$. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. By definition of convergence, there exists $K \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
> 
> $$
> \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K \implies ||x(k) - p|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> In particular, $x(k) \in B_{\varepsilon}(p)$ and, by assumption $x(k) \in A \setminus \{p\}$. Thus $B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\}$ contains points of $A$. This proves $p$ is a limit point of $A$.
> 
> **Forward direction**. Assume $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a limit point of $A$. It suffices to construct a sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ such that $\forall k \in \mathbb{N}^+, x(k) \in A \setminus \{p\}$ and $x(k) \to p$.
> 
> For each $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, choose a point $x(k) \in B_{1/k}(p) \cap A$ with $x(k) \ne p$. Such a point exists by assumption that $p$ is a limit point. It remains to show $x(k) \to p$ as $k \to \infty$.
> 
> Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $K = \lceil \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon} \rceil$. Let $k \in \mathbb{N}$ satisfy $k \ge K$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||x(k) - p|| &< \dfrac{1}{k} \tag{since $x(k) \in B_{1/k}(p)$} \\
> &\le \dfrac{1}{K} \tag{since $k \ge K$} \\
> &\le \varepsilon \tag{since $K \ge 1/\varepsilon$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

A picture illustrating this proof is provided below. It shows how each point $x_k = x(k) \in A$ in the constructed sequence is selected using successively smaller open balls.

# Open Sets and Closed Sets

Given a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, you will often only consider sequences converging to points inside $A$. For example, if $A$ is the domain of a map, you may want a sequence of approximations $\{x(k)\}_k$ lying inside $A$ to actually converge to a point $p$ within the domain $A$. Otherwise, you cannot necessarily evaluate the map at the point $p$.

You can ensure this feature in two different ways.

1. If a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ converges to $a \in A$, then the tail of the sequence belongs to $A$.
2. If a sequence in $A$ converges to $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $a$ must belong to $A$.

Many sets $A$ will not satisfy either property, so each of these two properties of sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ warrants their own definition. 

## Open Sets

First, we want to define a property of a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying the following.

> If a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ converges to $a \in A$, then the tail of the sequence belongs to $A$.

In other words, no matter how you approach $a \in A$, you must eventually lie inside $A$. This is precisely the definition of an interior point. Thus, we have

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **open** if every point $a \in A$ is an interior point of $A$.

If $a$ is an interior point of $A$, then you can approach $a$ however you wish; that is, the set $A$ does not restrict how the sequence approaches $a$. 

On the other hand, if $a$ is a boundary point of $A$, then you can only approach $a$ along certain sequences while lying inside $A$; that is, the set $A$ restricts how the sequence approaches $a$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are some examples of open sets:
> - The empty set $\emptyset$ is (vacuously) open.
> - The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is open.
> - For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$, the open interval $(a, b) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is open.
> - For $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$, the open ball $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x - p|| < r\}$ is open

> [!NOTE] Example
> We want to prove that the set $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: y > 1\}$ is open.
> 
> Let $(a, b) \in A$ be given. Then, by assumption $b > 1$. Let $\varepsilon = \tfrac{b-1}{2} > 0$. Then, we have
> 
> $$
> B_\varepsilon((a, b)) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: ||(x, y) - (a, b)|| < \varepsilon\}
> $$
> 
> We want to show that $B_\varepsilon((a, b)) \subseteq A$. Let $(x, y) \subseteq B_\varepsilon((a, b))$. Then,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||(x, y) - (a, b)|| < \dfrac{b-1}{2} &\implies (x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 < \left(\dfrac{b-1}{2}\right)^2 \\
> &\implies (y-b)^2 < \left(\dfrac{b-1}{2}\right)^2 \\
> &\implies y-b < \dfrac{b-1}{2} \\
> &\implies y < \dfrac{3b - 1}{2}\\
> &\implies y < \dfrac{3(1) - 1}{2} \tag{since $b < 1$} \\ 
>  &\implies y < 1\\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Thus, $(x, y) \in A$, which proves that $B_\varepsilon((a, b)) \subseteq A$, as desired. $\blacksquare$

The easiest way to produce an open set is using the interior of a set.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The interior of a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is open.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $a \in A^o$. We want to show that $a$ is an interior point of $A^o$ i.e. $\exists \varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(a) \subseteq A^o$
> 
> By definition, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(a) \subseteq A$. Fix $x \in B_\varepsilon(a)$. Now, we want to show that $x \in A^o$.
> 
> Since the open ball $B_\varepsilon(a)$ is open and $x \in B_\varepsilon(a)$, $x$ is an interior point of $B_\varepsilon(a)$. Thus, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $B_\delta(x) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(a)$. As $B_\varepsilon(a) \subseteq A$, it follows that $B_\delta(x) \subseteq A$ so $x \in A^o$.
> 
> Since $x$ was an arbitrary point of $B_\varepsilon(a)$, we have $B_\varepsilon(a) \subseteq A^o$.

This gives rise to equivalent definitions of an open set.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. All of the following definitions are equivalent:
> 
> 1. $A$ is open.
> 2. $A = A^o$.
> 3. $A \cap \partial A = \emptyset$

These equivalent definitions can be used to verify whether a set is open.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the interval $I = [137, 237)$ in $\mathbb{R}$. We know that the point $137$ is a boundary point of $I$ because the sequence
> 
> $$
> \{137 + \tfrac{1}{k}\}_{k=1}^\infty
> $$
> 
> in $I$ and the sequence
> 
> $$
> \{137 - \tfrac{1}{k}\}_{k=1}^\infty
> $$
> 
> in $I^c$ both converge to $137$. Therefore, $137 \in I \cap \partial I$ so $I \cap \partial I$. Thus, by the above lemma, $I$ is not open.

## Closed Sets

Second, we want to define a property of a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying the following.

> If a sequence in $A$ converges to a point $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then $a$ must belong to $A$.

In other words, $A$ must contain all of its limit points. This suggests a definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **closed** if every limit point of $A$ belongs to $A$.

Any convergent sequence in a closed set $A$ must converge to a point in $A$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are some examples of closed sets.
> 
> - The empty set $\emptyset$ is (vacuously) closed.
> - The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is closed.
> - For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$, the closed interval $[a, b]$ in $\mathbb{R}$.
> - For $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$, the closed ball $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: || x - p|| \le r\}$ is closed.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The set $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: y \ge 1\}$ is closed.
> 
> Let $(a, b)$ be a limit point of $A$. Then, there is a sequence $\{(x(k), y(k))\}_k$ in $A \setminus \{(a, b)\}$ converging to $(a, b)$. By component-wise convergence, we have $x(k) \to a$ and $y(k) \to b$.
> 
> Since the sequence is in $A \setminus \{(a, b)\}$, we have $y(k) \ge 1$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Then, it follows by a limit law over $\mathbb{R}$ that 
> 
> $$
> b = \lim_{k \to \infty} y(k) \ge 1
> $$
> 
> Thus, $(a, b) \in A$.

Closed sets are often produced via the closure.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The closure of a set $A$ is closed.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $p$ be a limit point of $\overline A$. Then, there exists a sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $\overline A \setminus \{p\}$ converging to $p$. For each $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, note $x(k) \in \overline A$ implies there exists a sequence $y(k) \in A$ satisfying $||x(k) - y(k)|| < 1/k$. Thus, it suffices to show that the sequence $\{y(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $A$ converges to $p$, as this implies $p \in \overline A$.
> 
> Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. As $x(k) \to p$, there exists $K \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
> 
> $$
> \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, k \ge K \implies ||x(k) - p|| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
> $$
> 
> Set $K' = \max\{K, \lceil \tfrac{2}{\varepsilon} \rceil\}$. By the triangle inequality, for $k \in \mathbb{N}$ with $k \ge K'$,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||y(k) - p|| = ||y(k) - x(k) + x(k) - p|| &\le ||y(k) - x(k)|| + ||x(k) - p|| \\
> 
> &< \dfrac{1}{k} + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
> &\le \dfrac{1}{K'} + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
> &\le \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Hence, $y(k) \to p$ as required. $\blacksquare$

> [!TIP] Remark
> Why was the sequence $\{y(k)\}_k$ above constructed in that way?
> 
> If $\{x(k)\}_k$ was a sequence in $A \setminus \{p\}$ instead of $\overline A \setminus \{p\}$ , then $x(k) \to p$ implies $p$ is a limit point of $A$ and hence $p \in \overline A$. Thus, the "worst case scenario" is that $x(k) \in \overline A \setminus A$ for every $k$, in which case $x(k) \in \partial A$ for every $k$.
> 
> By choosing $y(k)$ in shrinking balls centered at $x(k)$, this forces $y(k)$ to converge to $p$.

The above lemma gives rise to equivalent definitions of a closed set.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. All of the following are equivalent:
> 
> 1. $A$ is closed.
> 2. $A = \overline A$.
> 3. $\partial A \subseteq A$.

Again, you can use these equivalent definitions to verify whether a set $A$ is closed.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider again the interval $I = [137, 237)$ in $\mathbb{R}$. The point $237$ is a boundary point of $I$ (consider the sequence $\{237 - \tfrac{1}{k}\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $I$ and $\{237 + \tfrac{1}{k}\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $I^c$). On the other hand, $237 \notin I$ so $\partial I \subseteq I$. Hence, $I$ is not closed.

## Set Operations

As we have seen, there are similar but dual motivations for the definitions of open sets and closed sets. Surprisingly, their definitions are literally complementary.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is open if and only if its complement $A^c = \mathbb{R}^n \setminus A$ is closed.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Assume $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is an open set. Let $p$ be a limit point of the complement $A^c$, so there exists a sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $A^c \setminus \{p\}$ satisfying $x(k) \to p$. Since $A$ is open, if $p \in A$ then the tail of this sequence must lie in $A$ but this is impossible since $x(k) \in A^c$ for all $k \ge 1$. Therefore, $p$ must lie in the complement $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus A = A^c$. This shows that $A^c$ is closed.
> 
> Conversely, assume $B = A^C$ is a closed set. We must show that $B^c = A$ is open. Let $p \in B^c$ be arbitrary. Since $B$ is closed, the point $p$ cannot be a limit point of $B$. Therefore, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\}$ does not contain any points in $B$. In other words, $B_\varepsilon(p) \setminus \{p\} \subseteq B^c$. Since $p \in B^c$, it follows that $B_\varepsilon(p) \subseteq B^c$. This shows that $p$ is an interior point of $B^c$ and hence $B^c$ is open. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> The closed interval $[223, 224]$ is closed and its complement $(-\infty, 223) \cup (224, \infty)$ is open.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The empty set $\emptyset$ and the set $\mathbb{R}^n$ are both open and closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is sometimes called **clopen**. It can be proven that these are the only two clopen sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> There are examples of sets which are neither open or closed.
> 
> 1. The interval $[137, 237)$.
> 2. The set $A = \{\tfrac{1}{n}: n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. You can show it is not open because any open ball around any point is not contained in the set. You can show it is not closed because $0$ is a limit point and yet $0$ does not belong to $A$.
> 3. The set of rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ is neither open or closed since $\mathbb{Q}^0 = \emptyset$ and $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{R}$

Open sets and closed sets respect other basic set operations, too.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> All of the following are true for sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> 1. A finite intersection of open sets is open.
> 2. A finite or infinite union of open sets is open.
> 3. A finite union of closed sets is closed.
> 4. A finite or infinite intersection of closed sets is closed.
> 5. A finite Cartesian product of open sets is open.
> 6. A finite Cartesian product of closed sets is closed.

> [!NOTE] Example
> An infinite intersection of open sets may not necessarily be open, such as:
> 
> - $\bigcap_{\varepsilon > 0} (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$. This is equal to the singleton $\{0\}$, which is not open.
> - $\bigcap_{n = 1} \left(0, 1 + \dfrac{1}{n}\right)$. This is equal to $(0, 1]$, which is not open.
> 
> An infinite union of closed sets may not necessarily be closed, such as
> 
> - $\bigcup_{0 < \varepsilon < 1} [-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]$. This is equal to the interval $(-1, 1)$, which is not closed.
> - $\bigcap_{n = 1} \left[0, 1 - \dfrac{1}{n}\right]$. This is equal to $(0, 1]$, which is not closed.

# Compact Sets

Suppose a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is domain of real-valued function $f$. Suppose we want to determine the maximum value of $f$ on $A$. We can construct a sequence of points $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $A$, where each value $f(x(k))$ is attempting to approximate the maximum value of $f$. 

Ideally, as $k \to \infty$, the sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ converges to some point $p$ inside $A$. Then $f(p)$ will presumably be the maximum value of $f$.

However, there are two critical issues:

1. How can you ensure that the sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $A$ converges to a point $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$?
2. How can you ensure the limiting point $p$ belongs to $A$?

The second point can be addressed if the set $A$ is closed. However, we cannot completely resolve the first point.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the closed set $A = [-1, 1]$. The sequence $x(k) = (-1)^k$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ lying in $A$ does not converge. Yet, notice that $x(2k) = 1$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ i.e. the subsequence of even terms $x(2), x(4), \ldots$ converges to $1 \in A$.
> 
> Contrast this with the closed set $B = [0, \infty)$ and the sequence $y(k) = 2^k$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ lying in $B$. This sequence does not converge and, no matter how you pick terms from this sequence, you cannot form a new subsequence which will converge. The issue here is that the set $B$ is not bounded, so the terms of the sequence $x(k)$ can always stay far apart.

We cannot ensure every sequence in a closed set converges; we can always construct an "alternating" sequence like the example above which will not converge. On the other hand, we know that we can sometimes pick infinitely many terms from a sequence and form a new subsequence which will then converge. If this can be done for **any sequence in $A$**, then we have addressed the first question.

## Definitions of Compactness

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **compact** if every sequence of $A$ has a subsequence which converges to a point lying in $A$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are some common examples of non-compact sets.
> - The interval $A = (0, 1]$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact. The sequence $\{\tfrac{1}{k}\}^\infty_{k=1}$ converges to $0$, which does not belong to $A$.
> - The set $B = \{\tfrac{1}{n}: n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact for the same reason as $A$.
> - The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not compact, since, for example, the sequence $x(k) = (k, k, \ldots, k)$ does not converge.
> - The unit open ball $B_1(0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not compact because you can construct a sequence which converges to a point on the unit sphere $\partial B_1(0) = S^{n-1}$.

The non-examples of $(0, 1]$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ illustrate two necessary conditions for compactness. First, the set must be closed. Second, the set cannot be arbitrarily large.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is **bounded** if there is $R > 0$ such that $A \subseteq \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x|| < R\}$. If a set is not bounded, then it is unbounded.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is unbounded. The interval $[1, 4]$ is bounded since $[1, 4] \subseteq (-5, 5)$.

If a set $A$ is unbounded, then it cannot be compact because you can construct a sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ lying in $A$ where $||x(k)|| \to \infty$. As a result, the terms will never get close to each other, so no subsequence can converge.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are some common examples of compact sets
> - The empty set $\emptyset$ is (vacuously) compact
> - Any finite set $A$ is compact since every sequence must repeat at least one element $a$ infinitely often.
> - The closed interval $[a, b]$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is compact, but hard to verify from the definition

It is hard to verify if a set $A$ is compact by definition. However, there is a natural equivalent definition of compactness.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Heine-Borel Theorem
> A set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is compact if and only if it is both **closed** and **bounded**.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Both directions of the proof are tough, but it is easier to show the conditions of closed and bounded are necessary for compactness than to show that they are sufficient.
> 
> The proof begins with the easier direction.
> 
> **Proof**. ($\implies$) Assume $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is compact.
> 
> First, we show $A$ is closed. Let $p$ be a limit point of $A$. Then there exists a sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ lying in $A$ such that $x(k) \to p$. Then, any subsequence of $\{x(k)\}_k$ must therefore converge to $p$ as well. Since $A$ is compact, this implies $p \in A$ and hence $A$ is closed.
> 
> Second, we show that $A$ is bounded by contrapositive. If $A$ is not bounded, then $\forall k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, the set $A$ is not a subset of $B_k(0)$ so there exists a point $x(k) \in A$ with $||x(k)|| > k$. Let $\{x(m(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ be any subsequence of $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$, so $m: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ is a strictly increasing function. It suffices to show that $x(m(k))$ does not converge to any point in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Fix $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We must verify that
> 
> $$
> \exists \varepsilon > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall K \in \mathbb{N}^+, \exists k \in \mathbb{N}^+ \text{ s.t. }k \ge K \text{ and }||x(m(k)) - p|| \ge \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> Take $\varepsilon = 1$. Let $K \in \mathbb{N}^+$. Since $m: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ is a strictly increasing function, set $k$ to be large enough so that $m(k)$ is greater than $K$ and the norm $||p$||. By rearranging the triangle inequality, we see by our choice of the sequence $x$ that 
> 
> $$
> ||x(m(k)) - p|| \ge ||x(m(k))|| - ||p|| > m(k) - ||p|| \ge 1 = \epsilon
> $$
> 
> This shows $x(m(k))$ does not converge to $p$, as required. Since the subsequence and point $p$ were arbitrary, the set $A$ is not compact. This proves that $A$ is bounded by contrapositive. This completes the proof of this direction.
> 
> ($\Longleftarrow$) Assume $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is closed and bounded. Let $\{x(k)\}_{1}^\infty$ be a sequence lying in $A$. We claim that $\{x(k)\}_k$ has a convergent subsequence. Assuming this claim, the subsequence must converge to a point $p$ in $A$, since $A$ is closed, and this would prove $A$ is compact.
> 
> It remains to prove this claim. Express the sequence $x(k) = (x_1(k), \ldots, x_n(k))$ in terms of its components, so $x_i(k) \in \mathbb{R}$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$ and all $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$. For $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, we show that each sequence $\{x_i(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ of real numbers is bounded in $\mathbb{R}$. Since $A$ is bounded, there exists $R > 0$ such that $A \subseteq B_R(0)$ so $\forall a \in A$, we have $||a|| < R$. Writing $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$, it follows that $|a_i| \le ||a|| < R$ for all $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$. Thus, for $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, the coordinate sequence $\{x_i(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ is bounded within $(-R, R)$.
> 
> Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ has a convergent subsequence. This property of $\mathbb{R}$ is called completeness and it can be assumed. Thus, the first coordinate sequence $\{x_1(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $\mathbb{R}$ has a convergent subsequence, say $\{x_1(m_1(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ for some strictly increasing function $m_1: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$. It converges to some real number $p_1 \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 
> The second coordinate sequence $\{x_2(k)\}_{k_1}^\infty$ also has the subsequence $\{x_2(m_1(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ in $\mathbb{R}$ and this subsequence is also a bounded sequence $x_2 \circ m_1: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^n$. Thus, this **subsequence** has a convergent subsequence $\{(x_1 \circ m_1)(m_2(k))\}_{k_1}^\infty$ where $m_2: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ is a strictly increasing function. This subsequence converges to some real number $p_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, so
> 
> $$
> (x_2 \circ m_1)(m_2(k)) = x_2(m_1(m_2(k))) = x_2 ((m_1 \circ m_2)(k)) \to p_2
> $$
> 
> as $k \to \inf$. Any subsequence of a convergent sequence still converges, so the first coordinate sequence still converges to $p_1$ along $(m_1 \circ m_2)(m_2(k))$.  That is $x_1((m_1 \circ m_2)(k)) \to p_1$.
> 
> You can repeat this process for the third coordinate sequence and so on. Ultimately, this gives strictly increasing functions $m_1, \ldots, m_n$ from $\mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ and $p_1, \ldots, p_n \in \mathbb{R}$ such that
> 
> $$
> \forall i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}, x_i(m(k)) \to p_i \quad \text{as }k \to \infty
> $$
> 
> where $m = m_1 \circ m_2 \circ \cdots \circ m_n: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ is strictly increasing. By component-wise convergence, the subsequence $\{x(m(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ converges to $p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

This powerful theorem makes it dramatically easier to verify compactness.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$. The interval $[a, b]$ is closed and bounded, so it is compact by the Heine-Borel theorem. The interval $[a, \infty)$ is closed but not bounded, so it is not compact. The open interval $(a, b)$ is bounded but not closed, so it is not compact.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Any finite set of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (including the empty set) is closed and bounded. Hence it is compact. The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is unbounded and hence not compact.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Fix $r > 0$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}$. The open ball $B_r(a)$ is not closed so it is not compact. The closed ball $B_e(a) = \{x\in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| \le r\}$ and the sphere $\partial B_r(a) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: ||x - a|| = r\}$ are closed and bounded, so both sets are compact.

The Heine–Borel theorem also implies a fundamental result about $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem
> Every bounded sequence of $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a convergent subsequence.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ be a bounded sequence of $\mathbb{R}^n$. That is, 
> 
> $$
> \exists R > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, ||x(k)|| < R
> $$
> 
> Then, construct the set
> 
> $$
> A = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x|| \le R\}
> $$
> 
> such that $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty \subseteq A$. This set is closed and bounded (by $R + 1$), and thus compact by Heine-Borel.
> 
> Since $A$ is compact and $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ lies in $A$, by the definition of compactness, $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ has a subsequence which converges to a point lying in $A$.
> 
> Thus, the bounded sequence $\{x(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ has a convergent subsequence. $\blacksquare$

The theorems of Heine–Borel and Bolzano–Weierstrass are equivalent for $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## Set Operations and Subsets

Like open sets and closed sets, compact sets respect set operations in a natural way.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Compactness and Set Operations
> All of the following are true for sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$
> 1. A finite union of compact sets is compact
> 2. A finite or infinite intersection of compact sets is compact
> 3. A finite Cartesian product of compact sets is compact

The same statements are true for bounded sets. Then, since the statements are also true for closed sets, we apply the Heine-Borel theorem to yield the above lemma.

You can use this lemma to produce compact sets using simpler compact sets.

> [!NOTE] Example
> For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$, since the interval $[a, b]$ is compact the hypercube $[a,b]^n = [a, b] \times \cdots [a, b]$ is compact.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The set
> 
> $$
> A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 \le 4, (x-2)^2 + y^2 \le 4\}
> $$
> 
> is the intersection of $B = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 \le 4\}$ and $C = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: (x-2)^2 + y^2 \le 4\}$.
> 
> Both $B$ and $C$ are compact so the set $A = B \cap C$ is compact.

Subsets of compact sets also satisfy a nice property.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Compactness of Subsets
> Let $A$ be a compact set in $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $B \subseteq A$ and $B$ is closed, then $B$ is compact.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> This follows directly from the Heine-Borel theorem but it can also be proved from the definition of compactness.
> 
> Let $\{x(k)\}_k$ be a sequence in $B$. Since $B \subseteq A$, the sequence also lies in $A$. By the compactness of $A$, it has a subsequence $\{x(m(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ converging to some $p \in A$. The subsequence $\{x(m(k))\}_{k=1}^\infty$ also lies in $B$. As $B$ is closed and $x(m(k)) \to p$, this implies $p \in B$. Hence, $B$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

This allows you to produce even more compact sets from other compact sets.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the following subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$:
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 2, -1 \le z \le 1\}
> $$
> 
> This is a hollowed out cylinder in $\mathbb{R}^3$. You can prove this is compact set using the above two lemmas. First, the set
> 
> $$
> A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 2\}
> $$
> 
> is a closed subset of the closed ball $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 \le 2\}$. Thus, $A$ is compact in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The closed interval $B = [-1, 1]$ is compact in $\mathbb{R}$. Thus,
> 
> $$
> A \times B = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: 1 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 2\} \times [-1, 1] = S
> $$
> 
> is compact in $\mathbb{R}^3$, as desired.

# Limits

Limits in $\mathbb{R}$ can only approach a real number from either the left (negative) or right (positive). Furthermore, limits that can only be approached from one side are separately defined as one-sided limits. There is rarely any meaningful difference in proofs with standard limits and proofs with one-sided limits.

Limits in $\mathbb{R}^n$, on the other hand, need to be able to approach a point from any possible direction and in any way. It is therefore not reasonable to come up with a separate definition for limits at boundary points.

Thus, a good definition of a limit in $\mathbb{R}^n$ should be sensitive to the function's domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, yet be indistinguishable for boundary points versus interior points of $A$.

## Formal Definitions

The formal definition of a limit in $\mathbb{R}^n$ generalizes almost effortlessly by replacing the absolute value with the corresponding norm (of the domain or codomain).

> [!NOTE] Definition: Open Ball Definition of Limits
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a limit point of $A$ and let $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Define $b$ to be the **limit of $f$ at $a$** provided
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta \implies ||f(x) - b|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> If the above holds, then write $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b$ or write $f(x) \to b$ as $x \to a$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> If there exists $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b$ then the **limit of $f$ at $a$ exists**. Otherwise, the limit does not exist.

Since the definition is so similar to limits over $\mathbb{R}$, the proof structure with the formal definition multivariable limits is unsurprisingly similar to single variable limits.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a proof that $\lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)}(x + y) = 5$ using the formal definition of a limit.
> 
> **Proof**. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$. Let $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Assume $0 < ||(x, y) - (2, 3)|| < \delta$. This implies that 
> 
> $$
> |x-2| \le \sqrt{(x-2)^2 + (y-3)^2}  = ||(x, y) - (2, 3)|| < \delta
> $$
> 
> and similarly $|y-3| < \delta$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||(x + y) - 5|| &= |x + y - 5| \\
> &= |(x - 2) + (y - 3)| \\
> &\le |x - 2| + |y - 3| \tag{triangle inequality}\\
> &< 2\delta \tag{$|x-2| < \delta$, $|y-3| > \delta$}\\
> &= \varepsilon 
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

There are a few new ideas required to estimate the norms that arise in the proof. One idea is to estimate the distance between a fixed coordinate component of two points by the (norm) distance between the two points. This was demonstrated above. Another idea is to use intermediate approximations by bounding variables one at a time and then combining the bounds. This was demonstrated above with the triangle inequality

We can also use $\min$ to bound variables in order to avoid certain cases (e.g. a variable is $0$ or negative). The next example shows this idea

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a proof that $\lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} xy = 6$ using the formal definition of a limit.
> 
> **Proof**. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $\delta = \min\{\varepsilon/6, 1\}$. Then, $\delta \le \varepsilon/6$ and $\delta \le 1$
> 
> Let $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Assume $0 < ||(x, y) - (2, 3)|| < \delta$. Then,
> 
> $$
> |x - 2| \le ||(x, y) - (2, 3)|| < \delta \quad \text{and} \quad |y - 3| \le ||(x, y) - (2, 3)|| < \delta
> $$
> 
> Moreover, as $\delta \le 1$, this implies that $2 < y < 4$. Then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||xy - 6|| = |xy - 6| &= |xy - 2y + 2y - 6| \\ 
> &\le |xy-2y| + |2y-6| \\
> &\le |y||x-2| + 2|y-3| \\
> &< 4|x-2| + 2|y-3| \\
> &< 6\delta \\
> &< \varepsilon \tag{$\delta \le \varepsilon/6$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

Yet another idea is to actually use the existence of single variable limits as an input.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a proof that $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \cos(x + y) = 1$.
> 
> **Proof**. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. As $\cos(t)$ is continuous at $t = 0$ and $\cos(0) = 1$, from the definition of continuity at a point, we have
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon_1 > 0, \exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall t \in \mathbb{R}, |t| < \delta_1 \implies |\cos(t) - 1| < \varepsilon_1 
> $$
> 
> Thus, we have the following with $\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon$,
> 
> $$
> \exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall t \in \mathbb{R}, |t| < \delta_1 \implies |\cos(t) - 1| < \varepsilon \tag{$\star$}
> $$
> 
> Take $\delta = \delta_1 / 2$. Let $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Assume $0 < ||(x, y)|| < \delta$. This implies, by the triangle inequality, that
> 
> $$
> |x + y| \le |x| + |y| \le 2||(x, y)|| < 2\delta = \delta_1
> $$
> 
> Since $|x + y| < \delta_1$, by $(\star)$ with $t = x + y$, it follows that $|\cos(x + y) - 1| < \varepsilon$.
> 
> This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

Notice from the definition of a limit that limits are only defined at limit points of the domain. The remaining points of a function's domain have their own name.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. A point $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an **isolated point of $A$** if $a \in A$ and $a$ is not a limit point of $A$.

In particular, a limit at an isolated point of a function's domain is not defined.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $A = [1, 3) \cup \{7\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$. Define the function $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ by
> 
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases}
> x & 1 \le x < 2 \\
> 100 & 2 \le x < 3 \\
> e^\pi & x = 7
> \end{cases}
> $$
> 
> The limit of $f$ at $a$ for any $a \in [1, 3)$ is defined; it may or may not exist, but it is defined. The limit of $f$ at $7$ is **not** defined since $7$ is an isolated point of the domain $A$.

Second, you only consider points belonging to the punctured ball **and the function's domain**; you cannot evaluate the function at points outside the function's domain.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f(x, y) = \log(1 - x^2 - y^2)$. Although $f$ is only defined inside the open ball $B_1((0, 0))$, the limit of $f$ as $(x, y) \to (1, 0)$ is still defined since $(1, 0)$ is a limit point. It does not exist, but is defined.

Proving a limit does not existing using the above definition of a limit is possible but rather tedious since the negation of the formal open ball definition is complex. Instead, we can use an equivalent sequential definition of the limit.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Sequential Definition of Limits
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a limit point of $A$ and let $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
> 
> Then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) =b$ if and only if for every sequence of points $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $A \setminus \{a\}$ with $x(k) \to a$, the sequence of points $\{f(x(k))\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ converges to $b$ i.e. $f(x(k)) \to b$.
> 
> In other words, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> \forall \{x(k)\}_k \subseteq A \setminus \{a\}, x(k) \to a \implies \{f(x(k))\}_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m \to b
> $$

This equivalent definition is the main tool for proving the non-existence of limits.

> [!TIP] Remark
> The negation of the above theorem is $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) \ne b$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> \exists \{x(k)\}_k \subseteq A \setminus \{a\} \text{ s.t. } x(k) \to a \text{ and } \{f(x(k))\}_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m \not\to b
> $$
> 
> To prove that a limit does not exist, we need to show that $\forall b \in \mathbb{R}^m, \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \ne b$. Thus we need to show that
> 
> $$
> \forall b \in \mathbb{R}^m, \exists \{x(k)\}_k \subseteq A \setminus \{a\} \text{ s.t. } x(k) \to a \text{ and } \{f(x(k))\}_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m \not\to b
> $$
> 
> In practice, we can find two sequences $\{x(k)\}_k$ and $\{y(k)\}_k$ in $A \setminus \{a\}$ that both converge to $a$ yet have $\{f(x(k))\}_k$ and $\{f(y(k))\}_k$ converge to different values $b_1$ and $b_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$. Then, for all $b \ne b_2$, we can take $\{x(k)\}_k$ and for $b = b_2$, we can take $\{y(k)\}_k$. This covers all possible values of $b$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a proof that $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \tfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ does not exist.
> 
> **Proof**. Define two sequences in $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0, 0)\}$ by $x(k) = (0, 1/k)$ and $y(k) = (1/k, 1/k)$ for $k$. By component-wise convergence, these both converge to $(0, 0)$. Notice that for $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$,
> 
> $$
> f(x(k)) = \dfrac{0}{0^2 + (1/k)^2} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f(y(k)) = \dfrac{(1/k)^2}{(1/k)^2 + (1/k)^2} = \dfrac{1}{2}
> $$
> 
> Thus, $f(x(k)) \to 0$ and $f(y(k)) \to 1/2$ as $k \to \infty$. Since $\lim_{k \to \infty} f(x(k)) \ne \lim_{k \to \infty} f(y(k))$ where $\{x(k)\}_k$ and $\{y(k)\}_k$ both converge to $(0, 0)$, the desired limit does not exist. $\blacksquare$

This concludes the formal definitions of the limit of a function. Like single variable calculus, these definitions capture the same key ideas but they are not efficient for computation.

## Basic Properties

Some basic properties of limits are needed to speed up calculations, and to prove other theorems.

### Components of Limits

The first and most fundamental property allows you to reduce limits of vector-valued functions to limits of real-valued functions.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Component-wise Limits
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. let $A$ be a limit point of $A$ and let $b = (b_1, \ldots, b_m) \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $f_1, \ldots, f_m$ be the component functions of $f$ so $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m)$. Then
> 
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b
> $$
> 
> if and only if for all $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$,
> 
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a} f_i(x) = b_i
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> $(\implies)$ Assume $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = b$. Then,
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon_1 > 0, \exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 \implies ||f(x) - b|| < \varepsilon_1
> $$
> 
> Fix $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. By assumption (with $\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon$), we have 
> 
> $$
> \exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 \implies ||f(x) - b|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> Take $\delta = \delta_1$. Then, for arbitrary $x \in A$, we have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 &\implies ||f(x) - b|| < \varepsilon \\
> &\implies |f_i(x) - b_i| < \varepsilon
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as required.
> $(\Longleftarrow)$ Assume $\lim_{x \to a} f_i(x) = b_i$ for every $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$. This means that
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon_i > 0, \exists \delta_i > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_i \implies |f_i(x) - b_i| < \varepsilon_i
> $$
> 
> Let $\varepsilon > 0$. By assumption, with $\varepsilon_i = \varepsilon/\sqrt{m}$ for each $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, we have
> 
> $$
> \exists \delta_i \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_i \implies |f_i(x) - b_i| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{m}}
> $$
> 
> Take $\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \ldots, \delta_m\}$. Let $x \in A$. Assume $0 < ||x - a|| < \delta$. For each $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, we have $\delta \le \delta_i$, which implies that 
> 
> $$
> |f_i(x) - b_i| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{m}}
> $$
> 
> It follows that 
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||f(x) - b|| &= \sqrt{|f_1(x) - b_1|^2 + \cdots + |f_m(x) - b_m|^2} \\
> &\le \sqrt{m \max_{1 \le i \le m} |f_i(x) - b_i|^2} \\
> &< \sqrt{m \cdot \dfrac{\varepsilon^2}{m}} \\
> &< \varepsilon
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as required. Thus, this proof is complete. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f(x, y) = (x + y, xy)$ so $f = (f_1, f_2)$ can be written using its coordinate functions $f_1(x, y) = x + y$ and $f_2(x, y) = xy$. By the previous examples, it follows that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f_1(x, y) = 5 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f_2(x, y) = 6
> $$
> 
> Therefore, by component-wise limits, 
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f(x, y) = \left(\lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f_1(x, y), \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f_2(x, y)\right) = (5, 6)
> $$

### Uniqueness of Limits

The second property is that limits are unique

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Uniqueness of Limits
> Let $a$ be a limit point of $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and fix points $b_1, b_2 \in \mathbb{R}^m$. If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b_1$ and $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b_2$, then $b_1 = b_2$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $a$ be a limit point of $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and fix points $b_1, b_2 \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
> 
> For the purpose of contradiction, assume that $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b_1$, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b_2$, and $b_1 \ne b_2$. Then,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &\forall \varepsilon_1 > 0, \exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 \implies ||f(x) - b_1|| < \varepsilon_1 \tag{$\star 1$}\\
> &\forall \varepsilon_2 > 0, \exists \delta_2 > 0 \text{ s.t. } \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_2 \implies ||f(x) - b_2|| < \varepsilon_2 \tag{$\star 2$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Take $\varepsilon = ||b_1 - b_2||/3 > 0$ (since $b_1 \ne b_2$). Then, with $\varepsilon_ 1 = \varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon$ in $(\star 1)$ and $(\star 2)$, we have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &\exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 \implies ||f(x) - b_1|| < \varepsilon \\
> &\exists \delta_1 > 0 \text{ s.t. } 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta_1 \implies ||f(x) - b_1|| < \varepsilon
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Take $\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_2\}$. Then $\delta \le \delta_1$ and $\delta \le \delta_2$. Let $x \in A$ be such that $0 < ||x - a|| < \delta$. Then, 
> 
> $$
> ||f(x) - b_1|| < \varepsilon \quad \text{and} \quad ||f(x) - b_2||
> $$
> 
> We can finally construct our contradiction:
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||b_1 - b_2|| &= ||b_1 - f(x) + f(x) - b_2|| \\
> &\le ||f(x) - b_1|| + ||f(x) - b_2|| \\
> &< 2 \varepsilon \\
> &< \dfrac{2}{3} ||b_1 - b_2||
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> which is not possible since $||b_1 - b_2|| > 0$. Thus, we must have $b_1 = b_2$ and the proof is complete. $\blacksquare$

### Basic Limit Laws

Many standard properties also hold along with a few more since limits can be vector-valued.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Basic Limit Laws
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $a$ be a limit point of $A$. Let $f$ and $g$ be $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued functions defined on $A$. Let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ be constants. All of the following hold:
> 
> 1. **Constants**. $\lim_{x \to a} b = b$ and $\lim_{x \to a} x = a$.
> 2. **Linearity**. If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x)$ exist, then $\lim_{x \to a} (f(x) + \lambda g(x))$ exists and
> 
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a} (f(x) + \lambda g(x)) = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) + \lambda \lim_{x \to a} g(x)
> $$
> 
> 3. **Scalar products**. If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} \phi(x)$ exist, then $\lim_{x \to a} (\phi(x)f(x))$ exists and 
> 
> $$
> \lim_{x \to a}(\phi(x)f(x)) = \left(\lim_{x \to a}\phi(x)\right)\left(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right)
> $$
> 
> 4. **Dot product**. If $\lim_{x \to a}f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a}g(x)$ exist then $\lim_{x \to a} (f(x) \cdot g(x))$ exists and 
> 
>  $$
> \lim_{x \to a} (f(x) \cdot g(x)) = \left(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right)\cdot \left(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\right)
> $$   

### Squeeze Theorem

Finally, there is a multivariable squeeze theorem for real-valued functions.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Squeeze Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $a$ be a limit point of $A$. Let $f, g, h$ be real-valued functions with domain $A$. Assume there exists $p > 0$ such that
> 
> $$
> \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < p \implies f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)
> $$
> 
> If $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = \lim_{x \to a}h(x) = b$ for some $b \in \mathbb{R}$ then $\lim_{x \to a}g(x) = b$.

This collection of limit properties will form the basis for quantities defined via limits.

## Limits with Infinity

The concept of infinity changes in higher dimensions. It is still defined using a limit but, since there are countlessly many ways to diverge outward in $\mathbb{R}^n$ for $n \ge 2$, the notion of $\pm \infty$ no longer makes any sense. Instead, for $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $n \ge 2$, you only consider whether the norm of a sequence grows arbitrarily large. This motivates a definition for limits at infinity.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Limits at Infinity
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be unbounded. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and let $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Define $b$ to be the **limit of $f(x)$ as $||x|| \to \infty$** provided
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists M > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, ||x|| > M \implies ||f(x) - b|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> If the above holds, then write $\lim_{||x|| \to \infty}f(x) = b$ or write $f(x) \to b$ as $||x|| \to \infty$.
> 
> If the above does not hold, the limit $\lim_{||x|| \to \infty}f(x)$ does not exist.

Limits at infinity do not behave quite the same in higher dimensions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> In the two dimensional plane, you can check that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{||(x, y)|| \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{x^2 + y^2} = 0 \quad \text{yet} \quad \lim_{||(x, y)|| \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{x^2 } = \text{DNE}
> $$
> 
> This may seem strange at first but notice that the latter function $1/x^2$ does not tend to zero along **all possible sequences** $\{(x(k), y(k))\}_k$ with $||x(k), y(k)|| \to \infty$, which is required by the definition above.

You can similarly define limits of real-valued functions diverging to infinity.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Infinite Limits
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set. Let $a$ be a limit point of $A$. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. The **limit of $f$ at $a$ diverges to $+\infty$** provided
> 
> $$
> \forall M > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta \implies f(x) > M
> $$
> 
> If the above holds, then write $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = + \infty$ or write $f(x) \to +\infty$ as $x \to a$.
> 
> The definition for $f(x) \to -\infty$ as $x \to a$ is similar:
> 
> $$
> \forall M < 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta \implies f(x) < M
> $$

These limits also do not behave the same in higher dimensions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> In the two-dimensional plane, you can check that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \dfrac{1}{x^2 + y^2} = + \infty 
> $$
> 
> yet
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} \dfrac{x}{x^2 + y^2}
> $$
> 
> does not exist and is not $\pm \infty$. Again, this is because not **all possible sequences** $\{(x(k), y(k))\}_k$ with $||x(k), y(k)|| \to (0, 0)$ have the limit exist.

> [!NOTE] Remark
> The above definitions can be combined to create more definitions. For example, the definition of $f(x) \to \infty$ as $||x|| \to \infty$ is
> 
> $$
> \forall M > 0, \exists N > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, ||x|| > N \implies f(x) > M
> $$

Despite these differences, these two definitions of limits involving infinity are quite similar to the one-dimensional case. You can prove analogous limit laws and formulate equivalent sequential versions

# Continuity

There are many similarities with continuity in multivariable calculus and continuity for maps $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ from single variable calculus. However, we must once again grapple with the subtleties of limits in higher dimensions where you can approach a point in countlessly many ways. This leads to many possible ways in which a function can be discontinuous

## Formal Definitions

Extrapolating from single variable calculus, we may guess  that a function $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous at a point $a \in A$ whenever

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)
$$

This is nearly correct but this is only defined if $a$ is a limit point of $A$, and not if it is an isolated point. Expanding this definition yields

$$
\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta \implies ||f(x) - f(a)|| < \varepsilon
$$

We know that $f(x)$ is defined at $a$ so we can replace "$0 < ||x-a|| < \delta$" with "$||x - a|| < \delta$". This leads to the following definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Open Ball Definition of Continuity
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $a \in A$ be a point. The function $f$ is **continuous at $a$** provided
> 
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, ||x - a|| < \delta \implies ||f(x) - f(a)|| < \varepsilon
> $$

This definition includes isolated points now, since the removal of the $> 0$ bound allows us to take $x = a$ for all isolated points $a$ (remember we cannot approach $a$ otherwise). It also gives the expected criteria for limit points.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $a \in A$ be a point. Both of the following hold:
> 
> 1. If $a$ is an isolated point of $A$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.
> 2. If $a$ is a limit point of $A$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$ if and only if $\lim_{x \to a}f(x) = f(a)$.

> [!NOTE] Proof: Sketch
> (1) If $a$ is an isolated point of $A$ then there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $A \cap B_\delta(a) = \{a\}$. Then, the definition holds for any $\varepsilon > 0$ with this fixed choice of $\delta$.
> 
> (2) Can be verified from the arguments preceding the definition.

Here are some basic examples.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Set $f(x, y) = x + y$ and $f(x, y) = xy$. Then, we know that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} f(x, y) = 5 = f(2, 3) \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} g(x, y) = 6 = g(2, 3)
> $$
> 
> Thus, both $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $(2, 3)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = 1/x$. Note that $f$ is not defined at $0$; we cannot say that $f$ is discontinuous at $0$ since it is not defined there.
> 
> On the other hand, if $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $g(0) = 0$ and $g(x) = 1/x$ for $x \ne 0$, $g$ is in fact discontinuous at $0$.

Notice that there is a geometric interpretation of continuity using subsets and open balls. Notice that $f$ is continuous at $a$ is equivalent to

$$
\begin{align*}
&\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A, x \in B_{\delta}(a) \implies f(x) \in B_\varepsilon(f(a)) \\

\iff &\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. }\forall x \in A \cap B_{\delta}(a) \implies f(x) \in B_\varepsilon(f(a)) \\

\iff &\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. } f(A \cap B_{\delta}(a)) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(f(a))
\end{align*}
$$

As expected, there is also a sequential definition of continuity.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Sequential Definition of Continuity
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $a \in A$ be a point. Then $f$ is continuous at $a$ if and only if for every sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ in $A$ converging to $a$, the sequence $\{f(x(k))\}_k$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ converges to $f(a)$.

Just like the sequential definition of a limit, this theorem is particularly useful to verify a discontinuity. Next, we introduce some terminology to discuss continuity on subsets of a domain.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Continuity on a Subset
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function with domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. For a subset $S \subseteq A$, the function $f$ is **continuous on $S$** if $f$ is continuous at $a$ for every $a \in S$. The function $f$ is **continuous** if $f$ is continuous on its domain $A$.

Checking continuity on subsets is similar to checking continuity at a point.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The function $f(x, y) = x + y$ is continuous on its domain $\mathbb{R}^2$. Here is a proof.
> 
> **Proof**. Fix $(a, b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$. Let $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Assume $||(x, y) - (a, b)|| < \delta$. Then, we have $|x-a| < \delta$ and $|y-b| < \delta$.
> 
> Then,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||f(x, y) - f(a, b)|| &= |(x + y) - (a + b)| \\
> &= |(x - a) + (y - b)| \\
> &\le |x-a| + |y-b| \\
> &< 2\delta \\
> &< \varepsilon \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as required. This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

> [!TIP] Remark
> Notice that the function $f(x, y) = x + y$ is linear and therefore simple enough so $\delta$ only depends on $\varepsilon$. For other functions, the parameter $\delta$ may depend on $(a, b)$ as well as $\varepsilon$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Every function $f: \mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous because all points in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ are isolated.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The function $f: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{R}$ give by $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous, because $f$ is continuous at every point in its domain. However, the function $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $g(0) = 0$ and $g(x) = 1/x$ for $x \ne 0$ is not continuous because $g$ is not continuous at $0$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define
> 
> $$
> F(x, y) = \begin{cases}
> x + y & \text{if } (x, y) \ne (2, 3) \\
> 237 & \text{otherwise}
> \end{cases}
> $$ 
> 
> By previous examples, $F$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(2, 3)\}$, but $F$ is not continuous at $(2, 3)$ since
> 
> $$
> \lim_{(x, y) \to (2, 3)} F(x, y) = 5 \ne 237 = F(2, 3)
> $$
> 
> This discontinuity is a **removable** discontinuity. 

Recall in single variable calculus, there were 3 common categories for discontinuities: removable, jump, or infinite. Discontinuities in higher dimensions are much more diverse.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $G(x, y) = \tfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ Notice $G$ is not continuous at $(0, 0)$ because it is not defined at $(0, 0)$. You can fix this by defining
> 
> $$
> H(x, y) = \begin{cases}
> \tfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2} & \text{if }(x, y) \ne (0, 0) \\
> 0 & \text{otherwise}
> \end{cases}
> $$
> 
> so $H$ is defined at $(0, 0)$. However, $H$ is not continuous at $(0, 0)$ since $\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} H(x, y)$ does not exist. This discontinuity looks like
> 
> ![[discontinuity 1.png]]

> [!NOTE] Example
> For $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$, define
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad  g(x, y) = \dfrac{x}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad h(x, y) = \dfrac{x}{x + y}
> $$
> 
> and set $f(0, 0) = g(0, 0) = h(0, 0) = 0$. The functions $f$, $g$, and $h$ are all discontinuous since their limits at $(0, 0)$ do not exist. However, all these discontinuities look different.

## Basic Properties

The first and most fundamental property relates the continuity of vector-valued functions to the continuity of its component real-valued functions

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Component-wise Continuity
> The map $f = (f_1, \ldots, f_m): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous at $a \in A$ if and only if for each $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, the component function $f_i$ is continuous at $a$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> By component-wise continuity, the function $F(x, y) = (x + y, xy)$ is continuous at $(2, 3)$ because each of its component functions is continuous at $(2, 3)$.

This property also gives proof that some common functions are continuous.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Every linear transformation $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> By , if $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is a linear map, then there exists an $m \times n$ matrix such that $f(x) = Ax$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Each component function of $f$ is of the form $g(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = c_1 x_1 + \cdots + c_n x_n$ for fixed constants $c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 
> By component-wise continuity, it suffices to check that $g$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. This can be proven from the definition. $\blacksquare$

There is also the standard list of properties that follows from limits.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Basic Continuity Laws
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and let $a \in A$. Let $f$ and $g$ be $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued functions defined on $A$. Let $\phi$ be a real-valued function defined on $A$. Let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. All of the following hold:
> 
> 1. If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$ then the function $f + \lambda g$ is continuous at $a$.
> 2. If $g$ and $\phi$ are continuous at $a$ then their scalar product $\phi f$ is continuous at $a$.
> 3. If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$ then their dot product $f \cdot g$ is continuous at $a$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> From component-wise continuity, it suffices to check all of these properties for real-valued functions (e.g. when $m=1$). Each statement can then be shown directly by the definition of continuity.

This allows you to create more continuous functions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the map $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ by $N(x) = ||x||^2 = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2$. Notice $f$ is the dot product of the identity map $\mathrm{id}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with itself. That is, for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{id}(x) \cdot \mathrm{id}(x) = x \cdot x = ||x||^2 = f(x)
> $$
> 
> Since $\mathrm{id}$ is a linear map and hence continuous, the map $f$ is continuous by the above continuity laws.

Another fundamental property of continuity relates to composition of functions.

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Continuity of Composition
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{B}$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $g: B \to \mathbb{R}^k$. Let $a \in A$. 
> 
> If $f$ is continuous at $a$ and $g$ is continuous at $g(a)$ then $g \circ f$ is continuous at $a$.

This corollary follows immediately from the following slightly more general theorem.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $f: A \to B$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $g: B \to \mathbb{R}^k$. Let $a$ be a limit point of $A$ and let $b \in B$.
> 
> If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = b$ and $g$ is continuous at $b$ then $\lim_{x \to a} g \circ f(x) = g(b)$.
> 
> The assumption of continuity for $g$ is vital.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Fix $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $g$ is continuous at $b$, there exists $\eta > 0$
> 
> $$
> \forall y \in B, ||y - b|| < \eta \implies ||g(y) - g(b)|| < \varepsilon
> $$
> 
> Since $f(x) \to b$ as $x \to a$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that
> 
> $$
> \forall x \in A, 0 < ||x - a|| < \delta \implies ||f(x) - b|| < \eta
> $$
> 
> Fix $x \in A$. Assume $0 < ||x - a|| < \delta$. By the above, $||f(x) - b|| < \eta$ so the previous implication with $y = f(x)$ implies that $||g(f(x)) - g(b)|| < \varepsilon$, as required. $\blacksquare$

With this, we can construct even more continuous functions via composition.

Like linear maps, multivariable polynomials are another class of continuous functions.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Monomials and Polynomials
> A **monomials** in the $n$ variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ is a function of the form $x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$ for some $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{N}$. A **polynomial** in the $n$ variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ is a linear combination of monomials in $n$ variable with real coefficients.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The function $p(x, y, z) = xy + 3z^4$ is a polynomial in the $3$ variables $x, y, z$. It is a linear combination of the monomials $xy$ and $z^4$ which respectively correspond to $(a_1, a_2, a_3) = (1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 4)$ from the above definition. You can show $p$ is continuous by writing it as a composition of functions. 
> 
> Namely, define the functions
> 
> - $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x, y) = xy$ and $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x) = x^4$
> - $\pi_3: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $\pi_3(x, y, z) = z$ and $\pi_{1,2}: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $\pi_{1, 2}(x, y, z) = (x, y)$.
> 
> You can check that $f$ is continuous  by definition. From single-variable calculus, the function $g$ is continuous. Since $\pi_3$ and $\pi_{1, 2}$ are linear transformations, they are continuous. Notice that
> 
> $$
> \forall (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3, p(x, y, z) = xy + 3z^4 = (f \circ \pi_{1, 2})(x, y, z + 3(g \circ \pi_3)(x, y, z)
> $$
> 
> Then, from continuity of composition, $f \circ \pi_{1, 2}$ and $g \circ \pi_3$ are continuous. Then, since $p$ is a linear combination of $f \circ \pi_{1, 2}$ and $g \circ \pi_3$, $p$ itself is continuous.

These ideas can be extended to all multivariable polynomials.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Continuity of Polynomials
> All polynomials in $n$ variables are continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> By continuous of linear combinations, it suffices to show all monomials in $n$ variables are continuous. This can be done with induction on the degree of the monomial, combined with continuity of linear transformations, continuity of $f(x, y) = xy$, and continuity of single-variable powers $x^n$.

## Topological Properties

We want to investigate how certain functions affect the topological properties of sets (e.g. open, closed, bounded, compact). Of course, if we don't restrict functions we use, anything can happen. If we restrict to continuous functions, we get an amazing result:

> The **preimage** of a continuous map preserves the properties of open and closed.

Even more remarkably, this phenomenon is a equivalent definition.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Continuity Preserves Openness and Closedness
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a function. The following are equivalent:
> 
> 1. $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> 2. The preimage $f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$.
> 3. The preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is closed for every open set $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$

> [!TIP] Remark
> The complete proof for this theorem is difficult and out of the scope of this course.

> [!NOTE] Proof ($1$ \implies $2$)
> Assume $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$. By definition
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &\forall a \in \mathbb{R}^n, \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. } f(B_\delta(a)) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(f(a)) \\
> \iff &\forall a \in \mathbb{R}^n, \forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 \text{ s.t. } B_\delta(a) \subseteq f^{-1}(B_\varepsilon(f(a)))
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Here $f^{-1}$ denotes the preimage. To prove (2), fix an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $a \in f^{-1}(U)$ so $f(a) \in U$. Since $U$ is open, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(f(a)) \subseteq U$. Then, from above, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that 
> 
> $$
> B_\delta(a) \subseteq f^{-1}(B_\varepsilon(f(a))) \subseteq f^{-1}(U)
> $$
> 
> as required. This establishes that $f^{-1}(U)$ is open and hence (2) holds.

This theorem can be used to quickly show that many sets are open or closed.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $A = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: xy + 3z^4 \le 8\}$. The polynomial $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $f(x, y, z) = xy + 3z^4$ is continuous as proven above. Notice that
> 
> $$
> f^{-1}((-\infty, 8]) = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: f(x, y, z) \le 8\} = A
> $$
> 
> Since the interval $(-\infty, 8]$ is closed, it follows that $f^{-1}((\infty, 8]) = A$ is closed.

You can also use it prove that a function is not continuous.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function defined by $f(x, y) = 237$ for $x^2 + y^2 < 1$ and $f(x, y) = 0$ otherwise. By definition, you have that
> 
> $$
> f^{-1}(\{237\}) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^1 + y^2 < 1\} = B_1((0, 0))
> $$
> 
> which is an open ball. Thus $f^{-1}(\{237\})$ is open, yet the set $\{237\}$ is closed. Thus, the function $f$ cannot be continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Neither boundedness nor compactness are preserved under the preimage of a continuous map.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be a constant map, say $f(x) = 223$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. The set $\{223\}$ is bounded but the preimage $f^{-1}(\{223\}) = \mathbb{R}^n$ is unbounded. The same example shows that the preimage of a compact set is not necessary compact.

Furthermore, the **image** of a continuous map does not preserve the properties of open, closed, or boundedness.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be the continuous map given by $f(x) = \sin(x)$. We have $f((0, 2\pi)) = [-1, 1]$ so the image of the open set $(0, 2 \pi)$ is a closed set $[-1, 1]$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be the continuous map given by $g(x) = 1/(x^2 + 1)$. Since $g(x)$ attains a maximum at $x = 0$ and $g(x) \to 0^+$ as $x \to \pm \infty$, you can check that $g(\mathbb{R}) = (0, 1]$ so the image of the clopen set $\mathbb{R}$ is neither open nor closed.

Compactness is a different story

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Continuity Preserves Compactness
> If $A$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f$ is an $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued function that is continuous on $A$, then $f(A)$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^m$
> 
> Informally, this theorem states that the **image** of a continuous map preserves compactness

> [!NOTE] Proof
> If $A$ is empty, then $f(A)$ is empty and hence compact. Assume $A$ is non-empty. Let $\{y(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ be a sequence $f(A)$. For each $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, the set $f^{-1}(y(k))$ is non-empty since $y(k) \in f(A)$ and $A$ is non-empty.
> 
> Thus, you can choose $x(k) \in f^{-1}(y(k))$ for each $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$. The sequence $\{x(k)\}_k$ also lies in $A$ so, since $A$ is compact, it follows by definition that there exists some strictly increasing $m: \mathbb{N}^+ \to \mathbb{N}^+$ such that the subsequence $\{x(m(k))\}_k$ converges to some $a \in A$. By the continuity of $f$ and the sequential definition of continuity, you can conclude that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{k \to \infty} y(m(k)) = \lim_{k \to \infty} f(x(m(k))) = f(\lim_{k \to \infty}x(m(k))) = f(a)
> $$
> 
> Thus, the subsequence $\{y(m(k))\}_k$ converges to $f(a) \in f(A)$. Hence $f(A)$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the set
> 
> $$
> B = \{(xy, yz, xz): 0 \le x, y, z \le 1\}
> $$
> 
> The map $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ given by $f(x, y, z) = (xy, yz, xs)$ satisfies
> 
> $$
> f([0, 1]^3) = \{f(x, y, z): i \le x, y, z \le 1\} = B
> $$
> 
> As $f$ is polynomial in each component, and polynomials are continuous, by component-wise convergence, $f$ is continuous. Since $[0, 1]^3$ is compact, $B = f([0, 1]^3)$ is compact by the above theorem.

# Path-Connected Sets

The next topological property we want to pursue is the idea of "connectedness". Informally, if a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is "connected", you should be table from walk from one point $p \in S$ to any other point $q \in S$ without leaving $S$.

![[path connected sets.png]]

Continuity and parametric curves allow us to formalize this idea.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **path-connected** if for every pair of points $p, q \in S$, there exists a continuous function $\gamma: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\gamma(a) = p$ and $\gamma(b) = q$ such that $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq S$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Later, we may refer to a set $S$ as $C^k$ **path-connected** if $\gamma$ must be also continuously $k$-times differentiable on $(a, b)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Fix $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $r > 0$. The open ball $B_r(a)$ is path-connected.
> 
> **Proof**. Fix $p, q \in B_r(a)$. Define $\gamma: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by
> 
> $$
> \gamma(t) = (1 - t)p + tq
> $$
> 
> so $\gamma(0) = p$ and $\gamma(1) = q$. Note that $\gamma$ is the straight line segment from $p$ to $q$. Each component of $\gamma$ is a linear single-variable polynomial in $t$, so it is continuous.
> 
> It remains to check that $\mathrm{im}(\gamma)$ is contained in the open ball. Fix $t \in [0, 1]$. Observe that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||\gamma(t) - a|| &= ||(1-t)p + tq - a|| \\
> &= ||(1 - t)(p - a) + t(q - a)|| \\
> &\le ||(1-t)(p - a)|| + ||t(q-a)|| \\
> &\le |1-t| ||p-a|| + |t| ||q - a|| \\
> &< |1- t|r + |t|r \tag{since $p, q \in B_r(a)$}\\
> &< r \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> This implies that $\gamma(t) \in B_R(a)$ so $\mathrm{im}(\gamma)$ is in the open ball. $\blacksquare$

The open ball is a special example of a path-connected set since you can connect any two points by a line segment. This property warrants its own definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **convex** if the line segments between two points $p, q \in S$ lies inside $S$.

Convex sets are an especially nice case of path-connected sets.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Balls, cubes, planes, and regular polygons are all convex. However, anything that has an indent of a part jutting out is not convex.
> 
> All convex sets are path-connected, but the converse is not true. For example, the union of any 2 closed balls which touch at a single point $a$ is not convex, but it is path-connected. For any 2 points $p$ and $q$ in different balls, the straight line from $p$ to $a$ and then $a$ to $q$ is a path.

Proving sets are not path-connected requires careful use of the intermediate value theorem.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The set $S = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x \ne 0\}$ is the $2$-dimensional plane minus the vertical axis. It is not path-connected.
> 
> **Proof**. Take $p = (-1, 0)$ and $q = (1, 0)$. Let $\gamma: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be any continuous function such that $\gamma(a) = p$ and $\gamma(b) = q$. Write $\gamma = (\gamma_1, \gamma_2)$ in terms of component functions, each of which are continuous by component-wise continuity.
> 
> Note that $\gamma_1(a) = -1$ and $\gamma_1(b) = 1$. Then, by the intermediate value theorem, there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that $\gamma_1(c) = 0$. This implies that $\gamma(c)$ lies on the vertical axis, so $\gamma(c) \notin S$. This proves that $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \not\subseteq S$ and hence $S$ is not path-connected. $\blacksquare$

The **preimage** does not preserve path-connectedness.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be the continuous map given by $f(x) = x^2$. The set $[1, 4]$ is path-connected, but the preimage $f^{-1}([1, 4]) = [-2, -1] \cup [1, 2]$ is not path-connected.

The **image** of a continuous map does preserve path-connectedness.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Continuity Preserves Path-connectedness
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a path-connected set. Let $f$ be a $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued function defined on $S$. If $f$ is continuous on $S$ then $f(S)$ is path-connected.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $p, q \in f(S)$ be arbitrary. By definition, there exists $a, b \in S$ such that $f(a) = p$ and $f(b) = q$. Since $S$ is path-connected, there exists a continuous map $\gamma: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with $f(0) = a$ and $f(1) = b$ such that $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq S$.
> 
> Since  $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq S$, we may define the map $f \circ \gamma: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R^m}$. We now need to show that $f \circ \gamma$ is continuous, has $\mathrm{im}(f \circ \gamma) \subseteq f(S)$, $\gamma(0) = p$, and $\gamma(1) = q$.
> 
> 1. Since $f$ and $\gamma$ are continuous, $f \circ \gamma$ is continuous by composition of continuous functions.
> 2. Since $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq S$, $\mathrm{im}(f \circ \gamma) \subseteq f(S)$.
> 3. $(f \circ \gamma)(0) = f(\gamma(0)) = f(a) = p$.
> 4. $(f \circ \gamma)(1) = f(\gamma(1)) = f(b) = q$.
> 
> Thus, $f \circ \gamma$ is a valid path of $f(S)$ and $f(S)$ is path-connected. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Recall the set $B = \{(xy, yz, xz): 0 \le x, y, z, \le 1\}$ and the continuous function $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ given by $f(x, y, z) = (xy, yz, xz)$. Since the cube $[0, 1]^3$ is path-connected, it follows by the above theorem that $B = f([0, 1]^3)$ is also path-connected.

If we take $S = [a, b]$ and $m = n = 1$ in the above theorem (Continuity preserves path-connectedness), we get the intermediate value theorem over $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Intermediate Value Theorem
> Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on $[a, b]$. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ then $f([a, b])$ is path connected.

> [!NOTE] Remark
> As $[a, b]$ is compact, we know that $f([a, b])$ is a compact path-connected set. You can verify that the only compact path-connected sets in $\mathbb{R}$ are closed intervals, so $f([a, b]) = [c, d]$ for some $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$ with $c \le d$.
