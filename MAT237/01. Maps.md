# Introduction

This chapter focuses on maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$. Formally, we mean functions $f: A \to B$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$.

This chapter is more exploratory in nature and will be casual with some definitions and notation.

# Parametric Curves

This section is about maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n
$$

These are sometimes called **vector-valued functions of a real variable**, especially when $n \ge 2$. They are more commonly called **parametric curves**. The input variable is the "parameter" and is often referred to as **time**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the map $\gamma_1: [-1, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ as $\gamma_1(t) = 2t^2$. This is simply a function that maps $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$. The image of $\gamma_1$ is the set $[0, 2]$.

When we plot a map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, we typically plot both the input space ($x$-axis) and the output space ($y$-axis) simultaneously in a single graph. However, when plotting parametric curve we only care about the output. A map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is illustrated by plotting its image in $n$ dimensions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the map $\gamma_2: [0, 2 \pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as $\gamma_2(t) = (\cos(t), \sin(t))$. The image of $\gamma_2$ is the unit circle in $\mathbb{R}^2$, namely the set $\gamma_2([0, 2\pi]) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/unit circle.png]]

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the map $\gamma_3: [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}^3$ given by $\gamma_3(t) = (\cos(t), \sin(t), t)$. Its image is a helix; as time increases, the helix is traced out in an upward fashion.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/helix.png]]

> [!NOTE] Example
> Not all parametric curves can be easily illustrated. For example, the image of the map $\gamma_4(t) = (t^2, \sin(t), e^{-t}, t)$ lies in $\mathbb{R}^4$ so we cannot draw it.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $p, q \in \mathbb{R}^n$. A straight line path $\gamma: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ from point $p$ to point $q$ is defined by $\gamma(t) = (1 - t)p + tq$. You can also view this formula as $\gamma(t) = p + t(q-p)$ since $p$ is the starting point and $(q - p)$ is the difference between the end and the start.

## Motion

A parametric curve $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^n$ for some interval $I \subseteq R$ describes the motion of an object moving in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The **position** of the object in $\mathbb{R}^n$ at time $t$ is precisely $\gamma(t)$. Then, one might suppose that the velocity of the object would be: for $t \in I$, the vector

$$
\gamma'(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\gamma(t + h) - \gamma(t)}{h}
$$

which is a limit of average velocities. But here the variable $h$ is a scalar whereas the quantity $\tfrac{\gamma(t+h) - \gamma(t)}{h}$ is a vector. The above notion of a limit is not quite the same as the limits of scalar quantities with scalar limit variables.

> [!NOTE] Example
> 
> An object in $3$-dimensional space is moving along the path described by
> 
> $$
> g(t) = \left( 2t-6, 5(t-3)^2, \dfrac{1}{2} t^2 - 4\right)
> $$
> 
> We can analyze the velocity $g'(3)$ using the limit definition. Similarly to the one-dimensional derivative definition, the approximation improves by averaging over increasingly short time intervals
> 
> | $h$          | $\tfrac{g(3+h)-g(3)}{h}$ | $\|\tfrac{g(3+h)-g(3)}{h}\|$ |
> |---------|---------|---------|
> | $1$          | $(2, 5, 3.5)$|  $6.42$ |
> | $0.1$      | $(2, 0.5, 3.05)$ | $3.68$ |
> | $0.01$    | $(2, 0.05, 3.005)$ | $3.61$ |
> | $0.001$ | $(2, 0.005, 3.0005)$ | $3.61$ |
> | $0.0001$ | $(2, 0.00005, 3.00005)$ | $3.60$ |
> 
> You might guess that $g'(3) = (2, 0, 3)$ and $||g'(3)|| = 3.60$ as that is what our average velocities and speeds seem to approach

You can write

$$
\gamma(t) = (\gamma_1(t), \ldots, \gamma_n(t))
$$

for $n$ single variable functions $\gamma_i: I \to \mathbb{R}$. These are the **component functions** of $\gamma$. Since there are single variable functions, we can apply single variable calculus tools to compute their derivatives $\gamma_i(t)$. This **reduction to single variable calculus** will be a recurring theme in multivariable calculus. As we will later see, the derivative of $\gamma$ can be written as:

$$
\gamma'(t) = (\gamma'_1(t), \ldots, \gamma'_n(t))
$$

Then, the **speed** of the object at time $t$ is the magnitude of the velocity $||\gamma'(t)||$, which satisfies

$$
||\gamma'(t)|| = \sqrt{\gamma'_1(t)^2 + \cdots + \gamma'_n(t)^2}
$$

and the direction of motion is the unit tangent vector, denoted as $T = T(t)$ and defined by

$$
T(t) = \dfrac{\gamma'(t)}{||\gamma'(t)||}
$$

Similarly, the **acceleration** of the object is the second derivative $\gamma''$ so

$$
\gamma''(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{\gamma'(t+h) - \gamma'(t)}{h} = (\gamma''_1(t), \ldots, \gamma''_n(t))
$$

## Frenet Frame in Three Dimensions

Describing the motion of an object **relative to its frame** is very useful in physics and engineering. 

As we have seen, the direction of motion is given by the unit tangent vector $T$. To more accurately describe the motion of an object, you may want to know: how the direction of motion is changing. Naturally, you may compute the derivative $T'(t)$ and its normalized version, the **(principal) unit normal**,

$$
N(t) = \dfrac{T'(t)}{||T'(t)||}
$$

Surprisingly, $T$ and $N$ are **orthogonal**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as $\gamma(t) = (\cos(t), \sin(t), t)$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. You can compute $\gamma'$, $\gamma''$, $T$, and $N$. Its derivative is calculated by taking each component function's derivation:
> 
> $$
> \gamma'(t) = \left(\dfrac{d}{dt} \cos(t), \dfrac{d}{dt} \sin(t), \dfrac{d}{dt} t\right) = (-\sin(t), \cos(t), 1)
> $$
> 
> Similarly to the first derivative, to find $\gamma''(t)$, take the derivative of each component of $\gamma'(t)$:
> 
> $$
> \gamma''(t) = \left(- \dfrac{d}{dt} \sin(t), \dfrac{d}{dt} \cos(t), \dfrac{d}{dt} 1\right) = (-\cos(t), -\sin(t), 0)
> $$
> 
> Since $||\gamma'(t)|| = \sqrt{\sin^2(t) + \cos^2(t) + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, it follows that
> 
> $$
> T(t) = \dfrac{\gamma'(t)}{||\gamma'(t)||} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-\sin(t), \cos(t), 1)
> $$
> 
> in which case
> 
> $$
> T'(t) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(- \cos(t), - \sin(t), 0)
> $$
> 
> As $||T'(t)|| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, we see that $N$ is given by
> 
> $$
> N(t) = \dfrac{T'(t)}{||T'(t)||} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}}(-\cos(t), -\sin(t), 0) = (-\cos(t), -\sin(t), 0)
> $$
> 
> Below is an illustration of how the unit normal is oriented on the curve traced by $\gamma(t)$
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/unit normal.png]]
> 
> Note that it is a coincidence that $\gamma''(t)$ is a scalar multiple of $N(t)$. This is only because $||\gamma'(t)||$ was just a scalar and not a function of $t$. If it was a function of $t$, we would need to apply quotient rule to compute $T$. $\gamma'(t)$ is always a scalar multiple of $T(t)$ though, by definition.

The unit tangent $T$ and the unit normal $N$ span a two-dimensional plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Based on our linear algebra knowledge, these two vectors cannot span $\mathbb{R}^3$. Since $T$ and $N$ are already orthogonal unit vectors, we can choose one more unit vector $B$, orthogonal to $T$ and $N$, to form an orthonormal basis. We can chose both $B$ and $-B$, so to remove any ambiguity, we define the **binormal unit vector** $B$ to be the **unique** unit vector such that $\{T, BN, B\}$ form a positively-oriented ordered orthogonal basis in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Geometrically, this means $T, N, B$ satisfy the right-hand rule.

![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/right-hand rule.png]]

This ordered basis $\{T, N, B\}$ forms the **Frenet frame** (or **Frenet–Serret frame** or **TNB frame**) describing the motion of an object in three dimensions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Continuing with the previous example, to find $B$ algebraically, you can use the cross product
> 
> $$
> B = T \times N
> $$
> 
> Recall that this product is only defined for vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$. We can use a "trick" to compute this. We can write the cross product $a \times b$ of two vectors $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ as a "determinant". That is, if $\{e_1, e_2, e_3\}$ is the standard basis in $\mathbb{R}^3$, then
> 
> $$
> a \times b = \det \begin{bmatrix}
> e_1 & e_2 & e_3 \\
> a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\
> b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> Its not really a determinant since some components are vectors, but by computing this determinant, we get
> 
> $$
> B = T \times N = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sin(t) e_1 - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cos(t) e_2 + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} e_3
> $$
> 
> so $B(t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sin t, - \cos t, 1)$.

## Geometry of Curves

The **trace** of a parametric curve $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is the image of $\gamma$, that is, $\gamma(I)$. This is also referred to as the path traced out by $\gamma$.

Notice that that many different parametric curves $\gamma$ can have the same trace.

> [!NOTE] Example
> There are many ways to trace the unit circle in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
> 
> - Define $\gamma_1: [0, \pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $\gamma_1(t) = (\cos(2t), \sin(2t))$. Then $\gamma_1$ traces the unit circle twice as fast.
>  - Define $\gamma_2: [0, \pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $\gamma_2(t) = (\cos(t-\pi), \sin(t))$. Then $\gamma_2$ traces the unit circle but starts at $\pi$ instead of $0$ radians of rotation.

The trace of a parametric curve is simply a set, so you may be tempted to introduce the following definition:

> A set $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **curve** if $C$ is the trace of a continuous parametric curve $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}$.

Surprisingly, this attempted definition has many pitfalls that will be discussed later.

There are also ways to describe a curve without directly using parametric curves.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the set
> 
> $$
> C = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y = x^2, -2 \le x \le 2\}
> $$
> 
> so $C$ describes the graph of the parabola $y = x^2$ on the domain $[-2, 2]$. We can define the parametric curve $\gamma: [-2, 2] \to \mathbb{R}^2$ as $\gamma(t) = (t, t^2)$ which yields a trace $\gamma([-2, 2]) = C$.

# Real-Valued Functions

This section is about maps of the form 

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}
$$

usually for $n \ge 2$. In mathematics, the full names of these maps are usually **real-valued functions of $n$ variables** or **multivariable real-valued functions**. The common shorthand is simply **real-valued functions**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are some algebraic examples of real-valued functions.
> 
> - A multivariable polynomial such as $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy - y^2 - y^4$.
> - The norm function $N: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by
> 
>   $$
>   N(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = ||x|| = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}
>   $$

## Scalar Fields and Densities

In physics, real-valued functions are called **scalar fields**, **scalar functions**, or **potentials**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Mass is never distributed uniformly among objects because there are always more dense regions and less dense regions in a mass. For example, a block of Swiss cheese has regions where there is no mass (holes) and regions where cheese is tightly packed.
> 
> Since the density of cheese depends on where in the cheese you are, we can describe the density with a scalar field $\varphi: C \to [0, \infty)$ where $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is the set of points in the cheese. Our scalar field $\varphi(x, y, z)$ outputs the density in units $\text{kg}/\text{m}^3$ at $(x, y, z)$.
> 
> For example, suppose $p \in C$ is a point inside a hole. Then we would expect $\varphi(p) \approx 0 \ \text{kg}/\text{m}^3$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> There are forces in physics that have a special connection with potentials, namely **conservative forces**. For example, the electrostatic force for a point charge is related to the scalar field $V: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by
> 
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}
> $$
> 
> The function $V$ is often referred to as the electric potential where $\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space constant and $Q$ is the charge constant. This function can describe how the electrostatic forces of two different charges influence each other as they move nearby.

Real-valued functions arise in many other fields of study as well.

> [!NOTE] Example
> A streaming site FLYX uses very complicated algorithms to find content to recommend to you. Every movie or show you watch generate data points that FLYX stores and uses to associate a rough categorization of the type of viewer you are.
> 
> Then, it evaluates the data, represented in $n$ variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, using some function $E: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ that outputs a score $E(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$. The shows with the highest scores are recommended to you. The specific implementation for the function $E$ falls into the domain of data analysis and machine learning. It could be nearest neighbors, a neural network, etc.

Real-valued functions which are non-negative are often referred to as **densities**. Often, you can write $f \ge 0$ to denote a real-valued function $f$ as non-negative. There are many real-world application of densities like population densities.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Charge density describes the amount of charges (electrons/coulombs) per unit of measurement. The unit of measurement depends on the situation. Length is commonly used for current flowing through a wire. Area is best for current flowing through a conducting sheet. Volume is generally used for any scenario in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Not all densities can be described by counting.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Think of a block of cheese again. If the mass of the entire cheese **uniformly** distributed across the region then its density $\rho$ is 
> 
> $$
> \rho = \dfrac{m}{V}
> $$
> 
> where $m$ is the total mass of the region and $V$ is the total volume of the region. For our cheese with varying density, $\rho$ is actually the **average** mass density.
> 
> What is the density $\varphi(x, y, z)$ at a specific point $(x, y, z)$ of cheese? A point has zero volume and zero mass, so instead we need a limit. Consider the average density of a small piece centered at that given point. As we reduce the size of this piece while keeping it centered at the point, we get a closer and closer approximation. This approximation becomes exact if you take the limit as volume goes to zero.
> 
> More formally, if $C_\varepsilon(x, y, z) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ is the cube of side length $\varepsilon > 0$  centered at $(x, y, z)$, then
> 
> $$
> \varphi(x, y, z) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \dfrac{\mathrm{mass}\left(C_\varepsilon(x, y, z)\right)}{\mathrm{volume}\left(C_\varepsilon(x, y, z)\right)}
> $$
> 
> To be continued when we study integration in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## Graphs, Level Sets, and Slices

There are many ways to visualize multivariable functions. The most basic is a graph.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Graph of a Function
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. The **graph of a function $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$** is the set in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ give by
> 
> $$
> \{(x, f(x)): x \in A\}
> $$

> [!INFO] Remark
> Notice that $(x, f(x))$ belongs to $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ since $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}$.

These sets are the main tools for visualizing a multivariable function $f$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$. The graph of $f$ is
> 
> $$
> \{(x, y, x^2 + y^2: (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\}
> $$
> 
> which is plotted below. In general, the graph of a two-variable real-valued function is called a **surface plot**. This specific surface is referred to as a "paraboloid".
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/paraboloid.png]]

It is often helpful to "reduce dimension" when trying to visualize a graph or prove a lemma. There are several ways of doing so.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Level Sets
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Fix $k \in \mathbb{R}$. The **level set** of $f$ at $k$ is the set $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: f(x) = k\}$ . This is also called the **$k$-level set**.

> [!TIP] Remark
> A level set in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is also called a **contour**.

For graphs of $2$-variable functions, you can create a **contour plot** by plotting the level sets for a few different values.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ as $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$. You can visualize $f$ and its graph using only its level sets. Begin with the $1$-level set, $0$-level set, an $(-1)$-level set. For example, the $1$-level set of $f$ is the set $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$.

> By plotting a few more contours, we obtain a contour plot . Here, each contour is colored differently, but that is not always done.

The graph of $f$ lies in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and cannot be plotted, but its level sets like in $\mathbb{R}^3$ so you can plot them. Yet another way of "reducing dimension" is **slicing**, or equivalently fixing a variable. Here is the definition for $2$-variable functions.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ and $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function.
> 
> - For a fixed $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the **$x$-slice at $a$ of the graph of $f$** is the set 
> 
>   $$
>   \{(y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (a, y) \in A, z = f(a, y)\}
>   $$
> 
> - For a fixed $b \in \mathbb{R}$, the **$y$-slice at $b$ of the graph of $f$** is the set 
> 
>   $$
>   \{(x, z) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x, b) \in A, z = f(x, b)\}
>   $$

You similarly define slices for $3$-variable functions.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ and $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function.
> 
> - For fixed $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the **$x$-slice at $a$ of the graph of $f$** is the set 
> 
>   $$
>   \{(y, z, w) \in \mathbb{R}^3: (a, y, z) \in A, w = f(a, y, z)\}
>   $$
> 
> - For fixed $b \in \mathbb{R}$, the **$y$-slice at $b$ of the graph of $f$** is the set 
> 
>   $$
>   \{(x, z, w) \in \mathbb{R}^3: (x, b, z) \in A, w = f(x, b, z)\}
>   $$
> 
> - For fixed $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the **$z$-slice at $c$ of the graph of $f$** is the set 
> 
>   $$
>   \{(x, y, w) \in \mathbb{R}^3: (x, y, c) \in A, w = f(x, y, c)\}
>   $$

These slices are sets in $\mathbb{R}^3$ so, like Example 1.2.17, you can plot them. These definitions continue to generalize to higher dimensions.

# Vector Fields

This section is about maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n
$$

This is a special case since the dimension of the domain and codomain are the same. As usual, the $n = 2$ and $n = 3$ cases will be the primary focus since they can be reasonable visualized.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Vector Fields
> An **($n$-dimensional) vector field** is a function $F$ with domain and codomain lying in $\mathbb{R}^n$. You may also say that $F$ is a **vector field in $\mathbb{R}^n$**.

Heuristically, this viewpoint says that:

> $F(x) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a vector at the point $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$

It is common practice to use capital letters for vector fields. The vector $F(x)$ often represents a velocity or force at the point $x$ so it is sometimes called a **velocity field** or **force field**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The ocean is an example of a (time-dependent) vector field; at any given moment in time, each point $x$ in the ocean has a velocity $F(x)$.

Vector fields can be visualized by assigned a vector $F(x) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ to each point $x$ in the domain. As usual, this can be plotted for vector fields in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ only.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the vector field $F: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $F(x, y) = (-y, x)$. The point $(1, 2)$ has associated vector $F(1, 2) = (-2, 1)$ so at the point $(1, 2)$, you draw the vector $(-2, 1)$. By doing this process for many points on a grid, you can produce a **vector field plot** like the one below.

# Coordinate Transformations

This section continues to study maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n
$$

Here, we will view that as transformations instead of vector fields. In this text, a **transformation** will usually refer to any map with domain and codomain in $\mathbb{R}^n$. It is usually continuous.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The transformation $f: \mathbb{R}^2  \to \mathbb{R}^2$ given by $f(u, v) = (u + v, u - v)$ is a linear transformation. The transformations $g: \mathbb{R}^2$ and $h: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ given by $g(u, v) = (u^2 + v^2, v)$ and $h(u, v) = (u + 1, 0)$ are non-linear transformations. You can show that $f$ is invertible, whereas $g$ and $h$ are not.

A **coordinate transformation** $f: A \to B$ will refer to a continuous transformation that is usually (but not always) bijective. The domain $A$ and the map $f$ form a **coordinate system** for the codomain $B$. For example, if $b = f(a)$ then you might informally say any one of:

> The point $b$ can be written as the point $a$ in the coordinate system defined by $f$.

> The point $b$ in $B$-space corresponds to point $a$ in $A$-space

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the transformation $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{2}$ defined by $g(u, v) = (u^2 + v^2, v)$. The pair $(u, v)$ denotes a point in the domain $\mathbb{R}^2$ so, to distinguish points in the codomain $\mathbb{R}^2$, it is common to use another pair, usually $(x, y)$. Then you may write
> 
> $$
> (x, y) = (u^2 + v^2, v)
> $$
> 
> to equivalently describe the coordinate transformation $g$. This notation makes it easier to describe whether you are referring to the domain or codomain of $g$.
> 
> Notice that $g(1, 1) = (2, 1)$, for example. This means the point $(2, 1)$ in $(x, y)$-space corresponds to the point $(1, 1)$ in $(u, v)$-space. Notice that $g(-1, 1) = (2, 1)$ so the point $(2, 1)$ can be written as $(1, 1)$ or $(-1, 1)$ in $(u, v)$-coordinates.
> 
> Transformations in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are commonly visualized using two planes. For example, you can plot the point $(1, 1)$ in the $(u, v)$-plane and its image $g(1, 1) = (2, 1)$ in the $(x, y)$-plane.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/coordinate transform.png]]
> 
> This idea generalizes to any set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ in the $(u, v)$-plane and its image $g(S) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ in the $(x,y)$-plane. For example, you can use $S = [0, 1] \times [0.5, 1.5]$ and obtain the plot below.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/coordinate transform image.png]]

Coordinate transformations are wonderful because they allow you to describe the same set in many ways. By choosing your coordinate system wisely, you can dramatically simplify many scenarios. This text studies three fundamental examples: polar coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^2$, cylindrical coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and spherical coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## Polar Coordinates

A point in the $(x, y)$-plane can be equivalently described using its distance from the origin and the polar angle. Formally, define the **polar coordinate transformation** $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by

$$
T(r, \theta) = (r\cos \theta, r\sin \theta)
$$

The variable $r$ is the **radius** and the variable $\theta$ is the **polar angle**. Notice this allows the radius to be negative. Informally, you can write

$$
(x, y) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)
$$

and geometrically this can be viewed as follows

![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/polar coordinates.png]]

Note that the polar angle $\theta$ is not unique.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The polar coordinates transformation $T$ has a lot of symmetries. By direct calculation notice that
> 
> $$
> T(1, \tfrac{\pi}{4}) = (\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}), \quad
> T(-1, \tfrac{\pi}{4}) = (-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}), \quad
> T(2, -\tfrac{\pi}{2}) = (0, -2),
> \quad
> T(0, -\tfrac{\pi}{3}) = 0
> $$
> 
> You can plot the transformation of these points
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/polar coordinates transformation.png]]
> 
> Notice that the radius can be negative and that infinitely many points in the $(r, \theta)$-plane correspond to the same point in the $(x, y)$-plane.

If $(x, y) = T(r, \theta)$ then you may say that the point $(x, y)$ can be written as $(r, \theta)$ in **polar coordinates**. This language is convenient because it allows us to plot points in rectangular coordinates but label them in polar coordinates.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Continuing with the previous example, the point $(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$ can be written as $(-1, \tfrac{\pi}{4})$ in polar coordinates. This leads to the following plot.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/polar coordinates example.png]]
> 
> Many authors may write $(-1, \tfrac{\pi}{4})$ instead of $T(-1, \tfrac{\pi}{4})$. This can be confusing since the point is actually $(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$ but you must interpret the meaning from context

Polar coordinates allow certain shapes (e.g. circles, hyperbolas, and ellipses) to be described more simply.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the polar equation $r=2$. Informally, notice that
> 
> $$
> x^2 + y^2 = (r\cos x)^2 + (r\sin y)^2 = r^2
> $$
> 
> Thus, the equation $r = 2$ in polar coordinates represents the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$.
> 
> Formally speaking, the polar equation $r= 2$ corresponds to the image of the set
> 
> $$
> A = \{(r, \theta): r = 2, \theta \in \mathbb{R}\} = \{(2, \theta): \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> under the polar coordinate transformation $T$. Notice that $\theta$ is any real number so
> 
> $$
> T(A) = \{(2 \cos \theta, 2\sin \theta): \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> is precisely the circle of radius $2$ centered at $(0, 0)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> What does the polar equation $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$ represent. Using the informal route is tricky since you must solve the equations
> 
> $$
> x = r\cos \theta, \quad y = r\sin \theta
> $$
> 
> for $\theta$. Instead, we take the formal approach: the polar equation $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$ corresponds to the image of the set
> 
> $$
> B = \{(r, \theta): r \in \mathbb{R}, \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\} = \{(r, \tfrac{\pi}{3}): r \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> under the polar coordinate transformation $T$. Notice that $r$ is any real number. Now, observe that
> 
> $$
> T(B) = \{(r \cos \tfrac{\pi}{3}, r \sin \tfrac{\pi}{3}): r \in \mathbb{R} \} = \{(\tfrac{r}{2}, \tfrac{r \sqrt 3}{2}): r \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> which is a line through the origin.

Most polar equations can be tricky to draw without software; using a table of values is highly recommended.

Coordinate transforms typically preserve some kind of geometric properties or modify them in a predictable manner. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, a nice visual involves grid lines. The domain $\mathbb{R}^2$ is plotted with vertical and horizontal grid lines and then those lines are transformed to the codomain (keeping the same colors).

> [!NOTE] Example
> On the left-hand side, the grid lines in the $(r, \theta)$-plane are plotted. They are mapped under the polar coordinate transformation to produce the picture on the right-hand  side in the $(x, y)$-plane.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/grid lines.png]]

We know that the polar coordinate transformation is not bijective on its entire domain. However, by restricting its domain to a subset, you can obtain a bijection.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The polar coordinate transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by
> 
> $$
> T(r, \theta) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)
> $$
> 
> maps the subset $(0, \infty) \times (-\pi, \pi)$ and $B = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(x, 0): x \le 0\}$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Let $A = (0, \infty) \times (-\pi, \pi)$ and $B = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(x, 0): x \le 0\}$. First we prove that $T(A) \subseteq B$.
> 
> Fix $(r, \theta) \in A$. Assume by contradiction that $T(r, \theta) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta) \notin B$. This implies that $r \sin \theta = 0$ and $r \cos \theta \le 0$. This only happens if $\theta$ is an odd integer multiple of $\pi$ but that is impossible when $(r, \theta) \in A$. This proves the claim. Thus, we may define $f: A \to B$ by $f(r, \theta) = T(r, \theta)$ for all $(r, \theta) \in A$. We must prove that $f$ is bijective.
> 
> Next, we prove that $f$ is injective. Let $(r_1, \theta_1), (r_2, \theta_2) \in A$ be such that $f(r_1, \theta_1) = f(r_2, \theta_2)$. Then, $r_1 \cos \theta_1 = r_2 \cos \theta_2$ and $r_1 \sin \theta_1 = r_2 \sin \theta_2$. It follows that
> 
> $$
> r_1^2 = (r_1 \cos \theta_1)^2 + (r_1 \sin \theta_1)^2 = (r_2 \cos \theta_2)^2 + (r_2 \sin \theta_2)^2 = r_2^2
> $$
> 
> so $r_1 = r_2$ as both $r_1$ and $r_2$ are positive. This implies $(\cos \theta_1, \sin \theta_1) = (\cos \theta_2, \sin \theta_2)$. As $\theta_1, \theta_2 \in (- \pi, \pi)$, notice $\cos \theta_1 = \cos \theta_2$ implies $\theta_1 = \pm \theta_2$ and similarly $\sin\theta_1 = \sin\theta_2$ implies $\theta_1 = \theta_2$, $\theta_1 = \pi - \theta_2$, or $\theta_1 = -\pi - \theta_2$. These conditions are consistent only when $\theta_1 = \theta_2$ so we may conclude that $(r_1, \theta_1) = (r_2, \theta_2)$ as desired.
> 
> Finally, we must prove that $f$ is surjective. Let $(x, y) \in B$. Define $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and 
> 
> $$
> \theta = \begin{cases}
> \arccos(\tfrac{x}{r}) & \text{if } y \ge 0, \\
> -\arccos(\tfrac{x}{r}) & \text{if } y < 0,
> \end{cases}
> $$
> 
> so $r > 0$ and $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi)$. By checking cases depending on the sign of $y$, you can verify that $f(r, \theta) = (x, y)$. This proves that $f$ is surjective and hence bijective, as required.

## Cylindrical Coordinates

You can extend the ideal of polar coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to a coordinate system in $\mathbb{R}^3$ in two ways. Define the **cylindrical coordinate transform $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$** by

$$
T(r, \theta, z) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta, z)
$$

The variable $r$ is the **polar radius**, the variable $\theta$ is the **polar angle**, and the variable $z$ is the usual rectangular coordinate. Informally, you may write

$$
(x, y, z) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta, z)
$$

and say that the point $(x, y, z)$ can be written as $(r, \theta, z)$ in **cylindrical coordinates**. The geometry of this coordinate system is captured by the figure below.

![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/cylindrical coordinates.png]]

This allows us to plot points in rectangular coordinates but label them in cylindrical coordinates.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The points $(1, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, 3)$ and $(-1, -\tfrac{\pi}{2}, 3)$ in the $(r, \theta, z)$-space both correspond to the point $(0, 1, 3)$ in $(x, y, z)$-space. In other words, $(0, 1, 3)$ can be written as $(1, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, 3)$ or $(-1, -\tfrac{\pi}{2}, 3)$ in cylindrical coordinates. There are actually infinitely many ways to write $(0, 1, 3)$ in cylindrical coordinates.

Much like the name suggests, cylindrical coordinates are useful for describing objects with rotational symmetry about the $z$-axis in simpler terms.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The cylindrical equation $\theta = \pi/4$ corresponds to the plane passing through the $z$-axis. Namely, if $B = \{(r, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, z): r, z \in \mathbb{R}\}$ then its image under the cylindrical coordinate transformation $T$ is 
> 
> $$
> T(B) = \{(r \cos \tfrac{\pi}{4}, r \sin \tfrac{\pi}{4}, z): r, z \in \mathbb{R}\} = \{(r/\sqrt{2}, r/\sqrt{2}, z): r, z \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> Remember that $r$ is allowed to be negative.

The cylindrical coordinate transformation is a bijection once your restrict its domain.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The cylindrical coordinate transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ defined by 
> 
> $$
> T(r, \theta, z) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta, z)
> $$
> 
> Maps the subset $(0, \infty) \times (-\pi, \pi) \times \mathbb{R}$ bijectively to the subset $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{(x, 0, z): x \le 0, z \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

There are many other ways to restrict the domain and obtain a bijection. The key takeaway is that this coordinate system can nicely describe objects with rotational symmetry about the z-axis.

## Spherical Coordinates

The second way of extending polar coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is through spherical coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Define the **spherical coordinate transformation** $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by

$$
T(\rho, \theta, \phi) = (\rho \cos \theta \sin \phi, \rho \sin \theta \sin \phi, \rho \cos \phi)
$$

The variable $\rho$ is the (spherical) radius, the variable $\theta$ is the polar angle (or azimuthal angle), and the variable $\phi$ is the inclination angle (or zenith angle). Remember that $\rho$, $\theta$, and $\phi$ can be any real numbers, including negative ones. 

Informally, you may write

$$
(x, y, z) = (\rho \cos \theta \sin \phi, \rho \sin \theta \sin \phi, \rho \cos \phi)
$$

and say the point $(x, y, z)$ can be written as $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ in **spherical coordinates**. The geometry of this coordinate system is captured by the figure below.

![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/spherical coordinates.png]]

Spherical coordinates can be used to describe objects with rotational symmetry about the origin.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Locations on the Earth's surface are described with latitude and longitude. This geographical system is an example of spherical coordinates.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/earth spherical coordinates.png]]
> 
> Roughly speaking, longitude relates to the polar angle $\theta$ and latitude relates to the inclination angle $\phi$. Although the Earth is not a perfect sphere, its spherical radius $\rho$ is $\approx 6378$ km.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The points $(\sqrt 2, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{2})$ and $(-\sqrt 2, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{3\pi}{4})$ in $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$-space both correspond to the same point $(1, 1, 0)$ in $(x, y, z)$-space. In other words, $(1, 1, 0)$ can be written as $(\sqrt 2, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{2})$ and $(-\sqrt 2, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{3\pi}{4})$ in spherical coordinates. As usual, there are actually infinitely many ways to do so.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The spherical equation $\rho = 2$ represents a sphere of radius $2$ centered at the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$. You can verify this informally and formally. Informally, notice
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> x^2 + y^2 + z^2 &= \rho^2 \cos^2 \theta \sin^2 \phi + \rho^2 \sin^2 \theta, \sin^2 \phi + \rho^2 \cos^2 \phi \\
> &= \rho^2 \sin^2 \phi + \rho^2 \cos^2 \phi \\
> &= \rho^2 = 4
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Formally, the spherical equation $\rho = 2$ corresponds to the image of the set
> 
> $$
> A = \{(\rho, \theta, \phi): \rho = 2, \theta \in \mathbb{R}, \phi \in \mathbb{R}\} = \{(2, \theta, \phi): \theta \in \mathbb{R}, \phi \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> under the spherical coordinate transformation $T$. Then
> 
> $$
> T(A) = \{(2 \cos\theta \sin\phi, 2 \sin\theta \sin\phi, 2\cos \phi): \theta \in \mathbb{R}, \phi \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> but what is the shape of this set? From the calculation above, every point $(x, y, z) \in T(A)$ satisfies $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4$ so $T(A)$ is a subset of the sphere of radius $2$. You must show $T(A)$ is actually equal to the sphere of radius $2$, but this requires some work. This left as an exercise.

You can restrict the domain of the spherical coordinate to obtain a bijection. There are many other ways to do so as well.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The spherical coordinates transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by
> 
> $$
> T(\rho, \theta, \phi) = (\rho \cos \theta \sin \phi, \rho \sin \theta \sin \phi, \rho \cos \phi)
> $$
> 
> maps the subset $(0, \infty) \times (-\pi, \pi) \times (0, \pi)$ bijectively to the subset $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{(x, 0, z): x \le 0, z \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

# Parametric, Explicit, and Implicit Form

Linear algebra has created a language for identifying lower dimensional objects in higher dimensional spaces. Indeed you can use it answer questions of the following form.

> If a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is described by **linear equations**, then what is its dimension?

The concept of dimension in linear algebra is defined only for subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and it is calculated by finding the size of a basis of that subspace. Notice, however, that linear algebra only provides tools to analyze linear objects. Most geometric objects are not defined this way, thus spawning a golden question.

> If a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is described by **nonlinear** equations, then what is its "dimension"? How do you even define "dimension"?

This question will be a guiding question in your multivariable calculus journey. At the moment, you can speculate with some low dimensional cases to guide your intuition.

The first case is the idea of a "curve". A curve should presumably be created by bending a straight line. Since a straight $1$-dimensional (linear) object in higher dimensional space, a curve should probably be thought of as a $1$-dimensional (nonlinear) object living in higher dimensions.

The second case is the idea of a "surface". Intuitively speaking, a surface should presumable be created by bending a piece of a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Since a plane is a $2$-dimensional (linear) object living $3$-dimensional space, a surface should probably be thought of as a $2$-dimensional (nonlinear) object living in $3$-dimensional space. You can imagine this idea with some visual examples.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here are pictures of classic surfaces: a sphere, a cylinder, a cube, a hemisphere, a Klein bottle, and a torus.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/surfaces.png]]
> 
> As you can see, there is tremendous diversity in their shapes and smoothness. Formally, describing some of them can be difficult. This variation indicates how tricky it will be to formalize the concept of "$2$-dimensional object in $3$-dimensional space".

The general case is a **manifold**. Fix $k, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $k < n$. Intuitively speaking, a $k$-dimensional manifold in $\mathbb{R}^n$ should be presumable be created by bending a piece of $k$-dimensional plane in $\mathbb{R}^n$. It will take many chapters before you can study this problem and rigorously define manifolds. Right now, we can explore other fundamental questions.

> How can a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be described by nonlinear equations? Is there more than $1$ way?

There are unfathomably many ways to describe sets with nonlinear equations. In this section, you will explore the three fundamental forms for describing sets: parametric form, explicit form, and implicit form. Each form involves maps from $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ where $m \ne n$.

## Parametric Forms

One natural way to describe sets with nonlinear equation is using maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m
$$

when $n < m$. We've already seen the case $n = 1$ (parametric curves) but these are special cases. Here, we will focus on cases $n \ge 2$, with the visual focus on the special cases $n = 2$ and $m = 3$, that is, maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3
$$

This will presumably create surfaces, i.e.$2$-dimensional manifolds in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The **unit sphere** is the sphere of radius $1$ centred at the origin. Let $S$ be the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It can be described 2 "parameters". Namely, define $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by
> 
> $$
> g(\theta, \phi) = (\cos \theta \sin \phi, \sin \theta \sin \phi, \cos \phi)
> $$
> 
> This is the spherical coordinate transform with the spherical radius fixed, so
> 
> $$
> S = \{g(\theta, \phi): (\theta, \phi) \in \mathbb{R}^2\} = \mathrm{im}(g)
> $$
> 
> It seems reasonable to guess that $S$ will be a "$2$-dimensional manifold in $\mathbb{R}^3$" (i.e. a surface) because it can be described as the image of a map from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$.
> 
> Unfortunately, this calculation is not good enough for a definition of a surface. For example, you could define $h: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by
> 
> $$
> h(\theta, \phi) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta, 0)
> $$
> 
> so the image of $h$ is a unit circle lying in the $z = 0$ plane. Although $h$ has two inputs, the output in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a curve i.e. a "$1$-dimensional manifold". A formal way of distinguishing these two scnearios will come much later.

Despite the challenges presented by this example, you can extend the idea to any dimension.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Parametric Form
> Let $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $n < m$. A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ can be written in **parametric form (with $n$-variables)** if there exists a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and a continuous map $g: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> S = \{g(x): x \in A\} = \mathrm{im}(g)
> $$
> 
> Equivalently, we say that the set $S$ is **parametrized by** $g$.

This definition does not define manifolds. It simply gives one way to describe sets with nonlinear equations.

## Explicit Form

Another way to describe sets is actually a special case of parametric form. We have already encountered a large class of sets which are easy to parametrize: graphs.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The graph of the real-valued function $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ is the set
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x = f(x, y)\} = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: z = x^2 + y^2\}
> $$
> 
> We parametrize this graph by defining $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ as $g(x, y) = (x, y, x^2 + y^2)$. By convention, the graph of $f$ is always defined using the $z$-coordinate but there are other graph is produced by $f$. As long as you can express one coordinate as a function of the others, the set will be a graph. For example, the sets
> 
> $$
> S_1 = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x = y^2 + z^2\} \quad
> S_2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: y = x^2 + z^2\}
> $$
> 
> Are also graphs which can be parametrized. From left to right, the plots of $S$, $S_1$, $S_2$ are below.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/paraboloids.png]]

Graphs can be formalized to any dimension

> [!NOTE] Definition: Graphs of a Function
> Let $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $n < m$. Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^{m-n}$ be a continuous function. The set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^{m-n} = \mathbb{R}^m$ given by
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^{m-n}: x \in A, y = f(x)\}
> $$
> 
> is the **graph of $f$**. A set $\tilde S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is a **graph of** $f$ if $\tilde S$ is the same as $S$ after reordering variables. Informally, a graph is the set containing all combinations of input and output variables, permuted in any way.

> [!TIP] Remark
> "Reordering variables" can be formally expressed as $\tilde S = \pi(S)$ for a linear transformation $\pi: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^m$ given by $\pi(x) = Px$ where $P$ is a $m \times m$ permutation matrix.

As the examples demonstrate, graphs can always be written in parametric form. This suggests a new definition

> [!NOTE] Definition: Explicit Form
> Let $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $n < m$. A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ can be written in **explicit form (in $n$ variables)** if $S$ is a graph of a continuous function $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^{m - n}$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The set $\{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x = y^2 + z^2\}$ is written in explicit form since it is a graph of the function $f(y, z) = y^2 + z^2$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The unit sphere $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be written in parametric form, but not in explicit form. Since there are many possible graphs, there are many cases to consider. Here is a sketch of the proof
> 
> **Proof**. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $S$ can be written in explicit form. There are $6$ cases to consider. First, assume you can write
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: z = f(x, y)\}
> $$
> 
> for some continuous real-valued two variable function $f$. Notice that $(0, 0, \pm 1) \in S$ which implies $f(0, 0) = 1$ and $f(0, 0) = -1$, which is a contradiction. It is possible that $S$ could be written as $x= f(y, z)$, $y = f(x, z)$, or $(x, y) = g(z)$, $(x, z) = g(y)$, $(y, z) = g(x)$, but you can obtain a contraction in a similar fashion since $(0, \pm 1, 0) \in S$ and $(\pm 1, 0, 0) \in S$.
> 
> Although the unit sphere $S$ cannot be written in explicit form, it can be written as a finite union of sets in explicit form. In particular
> 
> $$
> S = \left\{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: z = \sqrt{1 - x^2 = y^2}\right\} \cup \left\{ (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x = -\sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}\right\}
> $$
> 
> so $S$ is the union of the graphs of $f(x, y) = \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}$ and of $g(x, y) = - \sqrt{1x^2 - y^2}$. These two sets in explicit form are the upper and lower hemisphere.
> 
> Informally, you can see that any vertical line crosses the lower hemisphere at most once, and the upper hemisphere at most once. But there are vertical lines that pass the entire sphere twice. Colloquially speaking, the sphere "fails the vertical line test", but this only means that $S$ cannot be written as a graph of the form $z = f(x, y)$. You also need to exclude the other 5 cases, but the argument is similar.

Thus, sets in explicit form can always be written in parametric form, but the converse is not necessarily true. Sets in explicit form are very special, because some variables can be explicitly expressed as a function of the others

## Implicit Form

Sets can also be naturally described by nonlinear equations using maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m
$$

where $n > m$. For instance, a real-valued function $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ can create a surface via its level sets.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The unit sphere $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is defined to be the set of points that are distance $1$ away from the origin. That is,
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}
> $$
> 
> which is the $1$-level set of the function $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$. The **implicit** equation
> 
> $$
> x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1
> $$
> 
> does not explicitly express one variable in terms of the others, like $z = g(x, y)$.

Other sets can also be created by looking at maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ when $n > m \ge 2$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $g(x, y, z) = (z - x^2 - y^2, x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$. Consider the set $C$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ defined by
> 
> $$
> C = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: g(x, y, z) = (0, 9)\}
> $$
> 
> This is the set of points satisfying two linear equations
> 
> $$
> z - x^2 - y^2 = 0, \quad x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9
> $$
> 
> The intersection of these non-linear equations implicitly define the curve $C$.

These examples suggest another way to describe sets by nonlinear equations.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Implicit Form
> Let $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $n > m$. A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ can be written in **implicit form (with $m$ equations)** if there exists a constant $c \in \mathbb{R}^m$, a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, and a continuous function $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that
> 
> $$
> S = f^{-1}(\{c\}) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: f(x) = c\}
> $$
> 
> where $f^{-1}$ denotes the **preimage** under the function $f$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The paraboloid $\{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: z - x^2 - y^2 = 0\}$ is written in implicit form since it is the $0$-level set of $f(x, y, z) = z -x^2 - y^2$. The same trick can be used for any graph, so sets in explicit form can always be written in implicit form.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The unit sphere $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is written in implicit form with $1$ nonlinear equation $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$ in $3$ variables $x, y, z$. This means that the sphere should presumably be, if we "carry over" the principles of linear algebra, $3 - 1 = 2$ dimensional, which it is. This acts as evidence that the principles of linear algebra may carry over to nonlinear systems.

This investigation is the beginning of something much greater, namely the **implicit function theorem**. You will explore that in depth later. For now, the key takeaway is that sets can have three different descriptions: parametric, explicit, and implicit. Each has its own advantages and disadvantages, and you will need to discover their connections to truly understand manifolds.

# Dimension Reductions

This section is about maps of the form

$$
\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m
$$

when $n > m$. These can be used to write sets in implicit form, but they have other uses. These maps are sometimes referred to as **dimension reductions**, because they push higher dimensional objects into lower dimensional space.

It has widespread scientific applications and some classical mathematical examples. As usual, the special case $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ has visual examples.

Linear projections are classic mathematical examples of dimension reductions

> [!NOTE] Example
> For $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, the map $\pi_i: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ given by
> 
> $$
> \pi_i: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}
> $$
> 
> is the **$i$th coordinate map**. These are convenient for proofs when you are trying to express some quantities in terms of continuous maps. Notice $\pi_i$ is a linear transformation.

> [!TIP] Remark
> The Greek letter $\pi$ is often used for projections. This is technically abuse of notation but it is permissible provided the context makes the notation unambiguous.

> [!NOTE] Example
> For $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, the map $\Pi_i: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ given by
> 
> $$
> \Pi_i(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = (x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}, x_{x+1}, \ldots, x_n)
> $$
> 
> is the **$i$th coordinate plane projection**. These are also linear transformations which are convenient for proofs.
> 
> In particular, for $n=3$, the map $\Pi_3(x, y, z) = (x, y)$ is also referred to as the **projection into the $xy$-plane**. The image of the paraboloid $P = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: z = x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}$ under $\Pi_3$ is the unit disk $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Informally speaking, $\Pi_3$ produces the "shadow" of an object in $\mathbb{R}^3$ when viewed from above.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The unit sphere $S^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be projected onto the plane in many ways. A famous way is **stereographic projection**. Each point on the sphere except $(0, 0, 1)$ is mapped onto the plane $z = -1$ by drawing a line through the north pole $(0, 0, 1)$ and the point $(\pi(x, y, z), -1)$ for some function $\pi: S^2 \setminus \{(0, 0, 1\} \to \mathbb{R}^2$. This process is illustrated below
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/stereographic projection.png]]
> 
> We can find an algebraic expression for this projection $\pi$:
> 
> We have two points in a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$: $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(x, y, z)$. Constructing a line with these points, we get the following line, parametrized by $t$.
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \ell(t) &= t[(x, y, z) - (0, 0, 1)] + (0, 0, 1) \\
> &= (tx, ty, t(z-1) + 1)
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> We want to find the values of $(tx, ty)$ when $t(z-1) + 1 = -1$. Isolating for $t$, we get
> 
> $$
> t = \dfrac{-2}{z-1}
> $$
> 
> and substituting, we get
> 
> $$
> (tx, ty) = \left(\dfrac{-2x}{z-1}, \dfrac{-2y}{z-1}\right) = \pi(x, y, z)
> $$
> 
> Stereographic projection is used in some fisheye lenses for wide-angle views.

This concludes our study of maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$.
