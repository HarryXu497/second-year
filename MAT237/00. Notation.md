# Sets

Sets are a fundamental building block of mathematics. Informally,

> A **set** $A$ is a collection of **elements**. We write $x \in A$ if the **element** $x$ is a **member of** $A$. The symbol "$\in$" is the set membership symbol.

Below are some canonical sets that have a fixed notation throughout this text:

- $\mathbb{N}$ is the set of natural numbers $\{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$.
- $\mathbb{N}^+$ is the set of positive natural numbers $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$.
- $\mathbb{Z}$ is the set of integers $\{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ is the set of rational numbers $\{\tfrac{m}{n}: m \in \mathbb{Z}, n \in \mathbb{Z}, n \ne 0\}$.
- $\mathbb{R}$ is the set of real numbers.

A rigorous definition of these canonical sets takes substantial effort. In this course, we can take them for granted.

## Logic

Valid logical statements in this text will either be "true" or "false". In other words, they will have a **binary truth value**. There are many standard operators to combine or compare logical statements. Below is a truth table for standard logical operations with valid logical statements $P$ and $Q$.

| $P$ | $Q$ | $\neg P$ | $P \land Q$ | $P \lor Q$ | $P \implies Q$ | $P \iff Q$ |
| :-: | :-: | :------: | :---------: | :--------: | :------------: | :--------: |
|  T  |  T  |    F     |      T      |     T      |       T        |     T      |
|  T  |  F  |    F     |      F      |     T      |       F        |     F      |
|  F  |  T  |    T     |      F      |     T      |       T        |     F      |
|  F  |  F  |    T     |      F      |     F      |       T        |     T      |

Each logical operation has a colloquial name and pronunciation.

- $\neg P$ is **negation**, pronounced "not $P$".
- $P \lor Q$ is **logical and**, pronounced "$P$ and $Q$".
- $P \lor Q$ is **logical or**, pronounced "$P$ or $Q$".
- $P \implies Q$ is **implication**, pronounced "$P$ implies $Q$", "if $P$ then $Q$", "$Q$ if $P$", or "$P$ only if $Q$".
- $P \iff Q$ is **equivalence**, pronounced "$P$ if and only $Q$" or "$P$ is equivalent to $Q$".

> Mathematical definitions **do not require** the use of "if and only if". It is common to write a sentence like "define $Q$ to hold if $P$ holds". After this sentence, the phrase $Q$ is a placeholder for the statement $P$ so, by definition, $Q$ holds if and only if $P$ holds. This convention is used throughout the text.

Quantifiers are used to construct more complex logical statements. Let $A$ be a set, finite or infinite. For each $x \in A$, let $P(x)$ be a logical statement

The symbol $\forall$ is the **universal quantifier**, denoting "for all" or "for every". For instance, the statement

$$
\forall x \in A, P(x)
$$

can be read as "for all elements $x$ in the set $A$, the condition $P(x)$ holds." A well-written proof of the above statement should look something like this

> Fix $x \in A$.
> …
> Thus, $P(x)$ holds.

We can also write "Let $x \in A$ be arbitrary" instead of "Fix $x \in A$".  Note that $x$ must be arbitrary and may not depend on other quantities.

The symbol $\exists$ is the **existential quantifier**, denoting "there exists", "there is", or "for some". For instance, the statement

$$
\exists x \in A \text{ s.t. } P(x)
$$

can be read as "there exists an element $x$ in the set $A$ such that the condition $P(x)$ holds". A well-written proof of the above statement should look something like this

> Take $x = \ldots$ so $x \in A$.
> …
> Thus, $P(x)$ holds.

The phrase "Take" can be replaced by "Define", "Let", "Choose", etc. Note that $x$ may depend on previously defined quantities.

The empty set, denoted $\emptyset$, is the set with no elements. It has a special role with these two quantifiers. If $A = \emptyset$, then $\forall x \in A, P(x)$ is **vacuously true** and $\exists x \in A, P(x)$ is **vacuously false**. You should always consider the possibility of whether $A = \emptyset$ when proving quantificational statements.

In statements with multiple quantifiers, two consecutive $\forall$ quantifiers or two consecutive $\exists$ quantifiers can be used in either order. That is, for sets $A$ and $B$ and logical statement $P(x, y)$ for each $x \in A$ and $y \in B$,

$$
\forall x \in A, \forall y \in B, P(x, y) \iff \forall y \in B, \forall x \in A, P(x, y)
$$

and

$$
\exists x \in A, \exists y \in B, P(x, y) \iff \exists y \in B, \exists x \in A, P(x, y)
$$

The order of the two quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$ matters. That is, in most cases

$$
\forall x \in A, \exists y \in B, P(x, y) \centernot\iff \exists y \in B, \forall x \in A, P(x, y)
$$

## Set Relationships

There are three common ways to compare sets.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A$ is a **subset** of a set $B$ if
> 
> $$
> \forall x \in A, x \in B
> $$
> 
> If so, we write $A \subseteq B$.

A well-written proof by definition that $A \subseteq B$ looks something like

> Fix $x \in A$
> …
> Hence, $x \in B$.
> As $x \in A$ was arbitrary, this proves $A \subseteq B$.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A$ is **equal** to a set $B$ if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$. If so, we write $A = B$.

A well-written proof by definition that $A = B$ looks something like

> First we show $A \subseteq B$.
> …
> This proves, $A \subseteq B$.
> It remains to show $B \subseteq A$.
> …
> This proves $B \subseteq A$ and hence $A = B$.

Sometimes you may want to distinguish subset and equality

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A set $A$ is a **proper subset** of a set $B$ if $A \subseteq B$ and $A \ne B$. If so, we write $A \subsetneq B$ 

A well-written proof by definition that $A \subsetneq B$ looks something like

> First we show $A \subseteq B$.
> …
> This proves, $A \subseteq B$.
> It remains to show $B \nsubseteq A$. Take $x = \ldots$
> …
> Thus $x \in B$ but $x \notin A$.
> This proves $B \nsubseteq A$ and hence $A \subsetneq B$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Some texts write $A \subset B$ to mean $A \subseteq B$ while other texts mean $A \subsetneq B$. These notes will never use the $\subset$ symbol.

## Set Builder Notation

Set builder notation is designed to define a set by picking out elements from another set that satisfy certain conditions. For each element $x$ in a set $A$, let $P(x)$ be a logical statement. You can define a subset of $A$ as

$$
 \{x \in A : P(x) \text{ holds}\}
$$

which is read as "the set of elements $x$ in $A$ such that $P(x)$ holds".

> [!EXAMPLE] Example
> The set of even integers is given by the set
> 
> $$
> \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : \exists m \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ s.t. } n = 2m\}
> $$

Note that set builder notation always uses a "dummy variable" for the elements. You can replace it by an entirely different letter and the set remains the same. To avoid abuse of notation, this dummy variable should **never** be previously defined before the set, and should **not** be used after the set is defined.

Multiple conditions in set builder notation can be specified with a common or "and"

> [!EXAMPLE] Example
> The following sets are equivalent:
> 
> $$
> \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0, x < 9\} = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0 \text{ and } x < 9\}
> $$

It is also acceptable to write

$$
\{x : x \in A \text{ and } P(x) \text{ holds}\}
$$

## Set Operations

Set operations are another common way to define sets. This method requires other sets to be already defined. The most basic operations are union and intersection.

> [!DEFINITION] Definition
> Let $I$ be a set, and for each $i \in I$, let $A_i$ be a set.
> - $\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i := \{x: \exists i \in I \text{ s.t. } x \in A_i\}$ is the **(arbitrary) union** of sets $A_i$ over $i \in I$.
> - $\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i := \{x: \forall i \in I, x \in A_i\}$ is the **(arbitrary) intersection** of sets $A_i$ over $i \in I$.

The set $I$ is often referred to as an **index set**

There are several special cases that warrant their own notation: two sets, a finite list of sets, and a countably infinite list of sets.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example
> For two sets $A$ and $B$, their union and intersection are respectively
> 
> $$
>  A \cup B = \{x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\} \quad \text{and} \quad A \cap B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \in B\} 
> $$
> 
> Fix $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. For a finite list of sets $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n$, you may write the finite union as
> 
> $$
>  \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_I = A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n = \{x : x \in A_i \text{ for some } 1 \le i \le n\}
> $$
> 
> and the finite intersection as
> 
> $$
>  \bigcap_{i=1}^n A_I = A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n = \{x : x \in A_i \text{ for all } 1 \le i \le n\}
> $$
> 
> For the countable infinite list of sets $\{A_i : i \in \mathbb{N}^+\}$, you may write the infinite union as
> 
> $$
>  \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_I = \{x : x \in A_i \text{ for some } i \in \mathbb{N}^+\}
> $$
> 
> and the infinite intersection as
> 
> $$
>  \bigcap_{i=1}^\infty A_I = \{x : x \in A_i \text{ for all } i \in \mathbb{N}^+\}
> $$

Another basic operation is subtraction.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> $A \setminus B = \{x : x \in A \text{ and } x \notin B\}$ is the **subtraction** of the set $B$ from the set $A$.

For multivariable calculus, there is another fundamental set operation.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> $A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A, b \in B\}$ is the Cartesian product of sets $A$ and $B$.

By definition, if $z \in A \times B$ then there exists $a \in A$ and $b \in B$ such that $z = (a, b)$. Thus, the following two phrases are equivalent:

1. Let $(a, b) \in A \times B$
2. Let $x \in A \times B$ so there exists $a \in A$ and $b \in B$ such that $x = (a, b)$.

We define finite Cartesian products as

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Fix $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. The $n$-**fold Cartesian product** of sets $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ is given by
> 
> $$
> A_1 \times \dots \times A_n := \{(a_1, \ldots, a_n): a_1 \in A_1, \ldots, a_n \in A_n\}
> $$
> 
> Moreover, the $n$-**fold Cartesian power** of a set $A$ is given by
> 
> $$
> A^n := A \times \dots \times A = \{(a_1, \ldots, a_n): a_1 \in A, \dots, a_n \in A\}
> $$

Again, we can write "Let $(a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in A_1 \times \dots \times A_n$".

# Euclidean Space

We define

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Fix $n \times \mathbb{N}^+$. The set
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^n = \{(x_1, \ldots x_n): x_1 \in \mathbb{R}, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> is $n$-**dimensional (Euclidean) space**.

## Elements, Subsets, and Cartesian Products

Members of $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be written in two forms: **element-wise** or **component-wise**. 

$$
x \in \mathbb{R}^n \text{ or } (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

Formally, these forms are related by the following paragraph

> Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. By definition of the set $\mathbb{R}^n$, for $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, there exists $x_i \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $x_i$ is the $i$th component of $x$. Thus, $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

This formally correct argument allows you to switch between elements and components. The choice of which form to use depends on the usage. If an argument does not require components or becomes harder to read with components, then avoid them to keep things concise.

The components for $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $\mathbb{R}^3$ have distinguished variables by convention.

- An arbitrary element of $\mathbb{R}$ is written as $x$.
- An arbitrary element of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is written as $(x, y)$.
- An arbitrary element of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is written as $(x, y, z)$.
- An arbitrary element of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is often written as $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the equation $x + y = 0$ corresponds to the line
> 
> $$
> \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x +y = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2
> $$
> 
> In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the equation $x + y = 0$ corresponds to the line
> 
> $$
> \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3: x + y = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2
> $$
> 
> This distinction demonstrates that the context matters when you write down an informal equation with variables. If in doubt, write the formal set.

It is a common misconception that a lower-dimensional Euclidean space is a subset of a high-dimensional Euclidean space. This statement is false i.e.

$$
\mathbb{R} \nsubseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \nsubseteq \mathbb{R}^3 \nsubseteq \dots
$$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Notice that 
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^2 \times \{0\} = \{(x, y, z): x \in \mathbb{R}, y \in \mathbb{R}, z = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3
> $$
> 
> yet
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^2= \{(x, y): x \in \mathbb{R}, y \in \mathbb{R}\} \nsubseteq \mathbb{R}^3
> $$

This example highlights set another concern about the difference between $\mathbb{R}^3$ and $\mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Technically speaking,
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^3 = \{(x, y, z): x \in \mathbb{R}, y \in \mathbb{R}, z \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> so an element of $\mathbb{R}^3$ has $3$ components. Yet,
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R} = \{((x, y), z): (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2, z \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> so an element $\mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}$ has $2$ components. Similarly,
> 
> $$
> \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x, (y, z)): x \in \mathbb{R}, (y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^2\}
> $$
> 
> This creates a weird situation since technically speaking, $(2, 3, 7) \ne ((2, 3), 7) \ne (2, (3, 7))$.

To avoid this nuisance, we adopt a general convention:

> [!TIP] Remark
> For any $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$, consider the sets $\mathbb{R}^{m + n}$ and $\mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n$ as equal. Thus
> 
> $(x_1, \ldots, x_m, y_1, \ldots, y_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{m + n}$ is the same as $((x_1, \ldots, x_m), (y_1, \ldots, y_n) \in \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n$

This choice is also referred to as a **canonical isomorphism** between $\mathbb{R}^{m + n}$ and $\mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n$ since it is a standard bijective mapping between the two sets.

## Matrices, Vectors, and Scalars

Euclidean space is often thought of as a space of vectors in linear algebra. Before discussing this perspective in detail, some linear algebra notation must first be fixed.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Fix $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. An $m \times n$ **matrix** $A$ is a rectangular array of real numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. It is written as
> 
> $$
> A = \begin{bmatrix}
> A_{11} & A_{12} \dots A_{1n} \\
> A_{21} & A_{22} \dots A_{2n} \\
> \vdots & \vdots ^ \ddots $ \vdots \\
> A_{m1} & A_{m2} \dots A_{mn} \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> A_{ij}
> \end{bmatrix}_{i, j}
> $$
> 
> where the $(i, j)$-**entry of** $A$ is denoted $A_{ij}$ for $i \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$, $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Fix $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{ij}\end{bmatrix}_{i, j}$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. The **transpose** of $A$, denoted $A^\top$, is the $n \times m$ matrix whose $(i, j)$-entry is $A_{ji}$ for $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, $j \in \{1, \ldots, m\}$. That is,
> 
> $$
> A = \begin{bmatrix}
> A_{11} & A_{21} \dots A_{m1} \\
> A_{12} & A_{22} \dots A_{m2} \\
> \vdots & \vdots ^ \ddots $ \vdots \\
> A_{1n} & A_{2n} \dots A_{mn} \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> A_{ji}
> \end{bmatrix}_{i, j}
> $$

Column and row vectors are defined using matrices

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Fix $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$. An **($m$-dimensional) column vector** $v$ is an $m \times 1$ matrix, written as
> 
> $$
> v = \begin{bmatrix}
> v_1 \\
> \vdots \\
> v_m \\
> \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{where }v_1, \ldots, v_m \in \mathbb{R}
> $$
> 
> An **($n$-dimensional) row vector** $v$ is an $1 \times n$ matrix, written as
> 
> $$
> w = \begin{bmatrix}
> w_1 & \dots & w_n
> \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{where }w_1, \ldots, w_n \in \mathbb{R}
> $$
> 
> A **scalar** $\lambda$ is a real number, so $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> By default, a "vector" will refer to a column vector. When possible, vectors will be denoted with Roman letters like $u, v, w$ whereas scalars will usually be denoted with Greek letters like $\alpha, \beta, \lambda$

Now, the space $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be viewed as a set of $n \times 1$ column vectors, or $1 \times n$ row vectors, or even as a set of $a \times b$ matrices provided that $n = ab$. This creates ambiguity if you want to perform some matrix operations, like multiplication or transpose. To remove all ambiguity, we fix a permanent convention:

> **Elements of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are always $n \times 1$ column vectors**

Notationally speaking, this implies that if $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then

$$
x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \begin{bmatrix}
x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The leftmost and center notation will be used most often, since they allow for more compact typesetting. Note that the center notation **does not** denote a row vector.

The **zero vector** is denoted as $0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
0 = (0, \ldots, 0) = \begin{bmatrix}
0 \\ \vdots \\ 0
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

The symbol $0$ is used to denote the scalar $0 \in \mathbb{R}$ and the vector $0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$. This is technically abuse of notation but it is acceptable when the context allows for only one plausible interpretation

Finally, you can express matrices in terms of vectors. Fix $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$.

- If $v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$, then the $m \times n$ matrix with columns $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ can be written as

$$
\left[

  \begin{array}{cccc}

   \rule[-1ex]{0.5pt}{2.5ex} & & \rule[-1ex]{0.5pt}{2.5ex} \\

	v_1 & \ldots & v_n \\

	\rule[-1ex]{0.5pt}{2.5ex} & & \rule[-1ex]{0.5pt}{2.5ex}

  \end{array}

\right] = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)^\top

   
$$

   - If $w_1, \ldots, w_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then the $m \times n$ matrix with rows $w_1, \ldots, w_n$ can be written as

$$
\left[

  \begin{array}{ccc}

	\rule[.5ex]{2.5ex}{0.5pt} & w_1 & \rule[.5ex]{2.5ex}{0.5pt} \\

			 & \vdots    &          \\

	\rule[.5ex]{2.5ex}{0.5pt} & w_m & \rule[.5ex]{2.5ex}{0.5pt}

  \end{array}

\right] = (w_1, \ldots, w_m)
$$

## Dot Product and Norm

The geometry of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is often described using the dot product

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The **dot product** of $x = (x_1, \ldots, w_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $y=(y_1, \ldots, y_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is defined to be
> 
> $$
> x \cdot y := \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i y_i = x_1y_1 + \cdots + x_ny_n \in \mathbb{R}
> $$
> 
> The vector $x$ is **orthogonal** to $y$ if $x \dot y = 0$

Below is a standard list of algebraic properties that the dot product satisfies

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$
> 
> 1. Non-negativity: $x \cdot x \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $x = 0$.
> 2. Symmetry: $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$.
> 3. Linearity: $x \cdot (y + \lambda z) = x \cdot y + \lambda (x \cdot z)$

These properties will be applied without mention throughout this text, along with other standard theorems of linear algebra.

The notion of distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by the norm:

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The **norm** of a point $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is given by
> 
> $$
> ||x|| = \sqrt{x \cdot x} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}
> $$

> [!TIP] Remark
> Many other sources will only use one vertical bar instead of two for the norm. In this text, the expression $|x|$ will only be defined as the absolute value of a real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

The norm has some standard properties which will be used without mention.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$
> 
> 1. Non-negativity: $||x|| \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $x = 0$
> 2. $||\lambda x|| = |\lambda| \cdot ||x||$
> 3. Law of cosines: $||x + y||^2 = ||x||^2 + ||y||^2 + 2(x \cdot y)$
> 4. Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: $|x \cdot y| \le ||x|| \cdot ||y||$

One property of the norm will be fundamental.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Fix $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$. For $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$
> 
> $$
> ||x_1 + \cdots + x_k|| \le ||x_1|| + \cdots + ||x_k||
> $$

This can be proved using induction on $k$ and the law of cosines:

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We proceed by mathematical induction.
> - **Base Case**. Fix $k = 1$ in the above lemma. Then, we get the trivially true statement
> 
> > for $x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $||x_1|| \le ||x_1||$
> 
>  - **Induction Step**. Let $n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ be fixed. Assume that the $k = n$ case in the above lemma is true:
> 
> > For $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $||x_1 + \cdots + x_n|| \le ||x_1|| + \cdots + ||x_n||$
> 
> Now, we want to show that the $k = n + 1$ case in the above lemma is true:
> 
> > For $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n, x_{n+1} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n} + x_{n+1}|| \le ||x_1|| + \cdots + ||x_n|| + ||x_{n+1}||$
> 
> We apply the law of cosines and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
> 
>    $$
>    \begin{align*}
>    ||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n} + x_{n+1}||^2 &= ||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}||^2 + ||x_{n+1}||^2 + 2((x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}) \cdot x_{n+1}) \\
>    
>    &\le ||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}||^2 + ||x_{n+1}||^2 + 2||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}|| \cdot ||x_{n+1}|| \\
>    
>    &\le (||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}|| + ||x_{n+1}||)^2 \\
>    
>    \end{align*}
>    $$
> 
> Then, since both the quantities being squared are non-negative, we take the square root of each side to yield
> 
>    $$
>    \begin{align*}
>    ||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n} + x_{n+1}|| &\le ||x_1 + \cdots + x_{n}|| + ||x_{n+1}|| \\
>    
>    &\le ||x_1|| + \cdots + ||x_{n}|| + ||x_{n+1}|| \\
>    \end{align*}
>    $$
> 
> as required.
> 
> Thus, by mathematical induction, this lemma is true.

## Balls and Spheres

There are infinitely many subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and some are especially important. Here you will see the different ways to generalize the idea of "intervals" in $\mathbb{R}$: one way is the norm and another via Cartesian products.

First, we define balls and spheres

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $r > 0$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
> - The **open ball of radius $r$ centered at $a$** is the set
> 
>   $$
>   B_r(a) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x - a|| < r\}
>   $$
> 
>  - The **closed ball of radius $r$ centered at $a$** is the set
> 
>   $$
>  \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x - a|| \le r\}
>   $$
> 
>  - The **sphere of radius $r$ centered at $a$** is the set
> 
>   $$
>  \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x - a|| = r\}
>   $$

> [!TIP] Remark
> A open or closed ball is **punctured** if it excludes the center. For example, $B_r(a) \setminus \{a\}$ is a punctured open ball.

> [!NOTE] Example
> One dimensional "balls" devolve to intervals. Let $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and let $r > 0$. Then the open ball $B_r(a) = (a-r, a+r)$ is the open interval of length $2r$ centered at $a$. The closed ball with radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the closed interval $[a-r, a+r]$. The sphere of radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the finite set ${a-r, a+r}$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Two dimensional "balls" are **disks** and "spheres" are **circles**. Let $(a, b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and let $r > 0$. Then the open ball
> 
> $$
> B_r((a, b)) = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 < r^2\}
> $$
> 
> is an open disk and the sphere of radius $r$ centered at $a$ is the circle
> 
> $$
> \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : (x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2\}
> $$
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/two_dimensional_balls_and_spheres.png]]

We distinguish a particular sphere that is common used:

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The **$(n-1)$-dimensional unit sphere** in $\mathbb{R}^n$, denoted $S^{n-1}$, is the sphere of radius $1$ centered at the origin. In other words
> 
> $$
> S^{n-1} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : ||x|| = 1\}
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Note that $S^1$ is the unit circle
> 
> $$
> S^1 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}
> $$
> 
> and $S^2$ is the unit sphere
> 
> $$
> S^2 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}
> $$

## Rectangles and Cubes

The other way to generalize intervals in $\mathbb{R}$ is by using Cartesian products.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A **(closed) rectangle** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a set $R$ of the form
> 
> $$
> R = [a_1, b_1] \times \cdots \times [a_n, b_n] = \{(x_1, \ldots, x_n): x_i \in [a_i, b_i] \text{ for } 1 \le i \le n\}
> $$
> 
> where $a_1, b_1, \ldots, a_n, b_n \in \mathbb{R}$ and $a_i < b_i$ for all $1 \le i \le n$.
> 
> The set $(a_1, b_1) \times \cdots \times (a_n, b_n)$ is an open rectangle. In general, a "rectangle" refers to a closed rectangle unless specified otherwise.

> [!NOTE] Example
> One-dimensional rectangles are closed intervals, like $[a, b]$ where $a < b$. Open rectangles are open intervals like $(a, b)$. The set $\{a\}$ is not a rectangle.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Two-dimensional rectangles are "rectangles" in the usual colloquial sense.
> 
> ![[Second Year/MAT237/Images/rectangles.png]]

Rectangles where all sides have the same length have a special name

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A **($n$-dimensional) hypercube** is a set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ of the form
> 
> $$
> [a, b]^n = \underbrace{[a, b] \times \cdots \times [a, b]}_{n \text{ times}}
> $$
> 
> The **($n$-dimensional) unit hypercube** is the set $[0, 1]^n$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> A 2-dimensional hypercube is a **square** and $[0, 1]^2$ is the **unit square**.

# Functions

Below is a general but somewhat information definition of functions

> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. A **function** (or **map**) $f: A \to B$ assigns to each element $a \in A$ exactly one element $f(a) \in B$. The sets $A$ and $B$ are respectively the **domain** and **codomain** of $f$.

The domain consists of all possible inputs and the codomain consists of all possible outputs.

This text focuses on functions whose domain and codomain are subsets of some higher dimensional Euclidean space. These are referred to as maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, or equivalently,

- "$f$ is a map from $n$ dimensions to $m$ dimensions"
- "$f$ is a $\mathbb{R}^m$-valued function of $n$ real variables"
- "$f$ is a function of $n$ real variables with $m$-dimensional output"
- "$f$ is a vector-valued multivariable function"

Formally, all of these statements mean that $f$ is a function $f: A \to B$ where $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ for some $m, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$.

## Domain and Codomain

An unambiguously defined function must specify its domain and codomain, and then the rule to assign elements. For example,

> Define the sets $A = \ldots$ and $B = \ldots$
> Define the map $f: A \to B$ by $f(x) = \ldots$ for $x \in A$.

This order is important. The domain and codomain must be defined first. It is also recommended to write the phrase "for $x \in A$" so that the dummy variable $x$ is necessarily defined.

For maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, there are some special conventions. First, functions can be defined element-wise or component-wise.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The norm function can be defined element-wise by
> 
> > Define $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = ||x||$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> It can also be defined component-wise as
> 
> > Define $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}$ for $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

If you do not need to restrict your codomain, then it should be all of $\mathbb{R}^m$. One case where you may restrict the codomain is when studying inverse functions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The norm function can be defined in two different ways
> 
> > Define $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) = ||x||$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
> > 
> > Define $g: \mathbb{R}^n \to [0, \infty)$ by $g(x) = ||x||$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
> 
> Both are valid, but unless you have a good reason, it is better to define $f$. Note that $f \ne g$ since they have different codomains.

If you do not specify the domain, then it assumed to be the set of inputs where the function rule is defined.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a completely unambiguous definition of a f
> 
> > Define $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : xy > 0\}$
> > Define $h: A \to \mathbb{R}$ by $h(x, y) = \log(xy)$ for $(x, y) \in A$
> 
> Since the expression $\log(xy)$ is defined if and only if $(x, y) \in A$, this can be equivalently defined in a simplified form
> 
> > Define $h(x, y) = \log(xy)$
> 
> Be careful not to overly rely on the simplified definitions for functions.

## Image and Preimage

There are two different ways to build sets using functions. The first method is via the image. Given a function $f: A \to B$ and a subset $S \subseteq A$, you can define a subset of $B$ by

$$
\{y \in B : \exists x \in S \text{ s.t. }y= f(x)\}
$$

This definition is a bit clunky so mathematicians often prefer a more compact variant. Instead, we can rewrite the set as

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. For any set $S \subseteq A$, the **image of $S$ under $f$**, denoted $f(S)$, is the set defined as
> 
> $$
> f(S) = \{f(x): x \in S\}
> $$
> 
> In particular, the **image of $f$**, denoted $\mathrm{im}(f)$, is the image of $A$ under $f$, that is
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{im}(f) = f(A)
> $$

> [!TIP] Remark
> The image of $f$ is also commonly called the **range of $f$**.

Images under $f$ satisfy some standard properties.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. Let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of $A$. Then, the following are true.
> 
> 1. $f(\emptyset) = \emptyset$.
> 2. $f(S) \subseteq B$.
> 3. If $S \subseteq T$, then $f(S) \subseteq f(T)$
> 4. $f(S \cup T) = f(S) \cup f(T)$
> 5. $f(S \cap T) \subseteq f(S) \cap f(T)$

The second method for building sets using functions is via the preimage.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. For any set $U \subseteq B$, the **preimage of $U$ under $f$**, denoted $f^{-1}(U)$ is the set defined as
> 
> $$
> f^{-1}(U) = \{x \in A : f(x) \in U\}
> $$

> [!TIP] Remark
> The preimage is only defined on **subsets** of the codomain $B$. For example, if $b \in B$, then $f^{-1}(\{b\})$ is defined and is a subset of $A$, but $f^{-1}(b)$ is not defined.
> 
> The notation $f^{-1}$ is commonly used for the inverse function of $f$, but that may necessarily exist. Preimages under $f$ always exist, so $f^{-1}$ will be used with this meaning by default.

Preimages under $f$ also satisfy some standard properties

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. Let $U$ and $V$ be subsets of $B$. Then, the following are true.
> 
> 1. $f^{-1}(\emptyset) = \emptyset$ and $f^{-1}(B) = A$.
> 2. $f^{-1}(U) \subseteq A$.
> 3. If $U \subseteq V$, then $f^{-1}(U) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$
> 4. $f^{-1}(U \cup V) = f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$
> 5. $f^{-1}(U \cap V) = f^{-1}(U) \cap f^{-1}(V)$

Composing image and preimage yield different results depending on the order of composition.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A$ and $B$ be sets. Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. Let $S \subseteq A$ and let $U \subseteq B$. Then
> 
> $$
> S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S)) \quad \text{and} \quad f(f^{-1}(U)) \subseteq U
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> **Proof 1**
> 
> We want to show that $S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S))$.
> Let $s \in S$. We want to show that $s \in f^{-1}(f(S))$.
> 
> By the definitions of $f^{-1}$, we have
> 
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(f(S)) = \{x \in A : f(x) \in f(S)\}
>    $$
> 
> Since $S \subseteq A$, we have that $s \in S \implies s \in A$. Also, $s \in S$, $f(s) \in f(S)$ by the definition of $f(S)$.
> 
> Since $s \in A$ and $f(s) \in f(S)$, we have $s \in f^{-1}(f(S))$ and therefore $S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S))$.
> 
> **Proof 2**
> 
> We want to show that $f(f^{-1}(U)) \subseteq U$.
> Let $u \in f(f^{-1}(U))$. We want to show that $u \in U$.
> 
> By the definition of $f$, we have
> 
> $$
> f(f^{-1}(U)) = \{f(x): x \in f^{-1}(U)\}
>   $$
> 
> Since $u \in f(f^{-1}(U))$, there must exist some $u' \in f^{-1}(U)$ such that $u = f(u')$. By the definition of $f^{-1}$,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> u' \in f^{-1}(U) &\implies u' \in \{x \in A : f(x) \in U\} \\
> &\implies f(u') \in U
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Since $u = f(u')$, we have $u \in U$, as required. Therefore, $f(f^{-1}(U)) \subseteq U$.

## Inverse Functions

The existence of inverse functions will be a major point of exploration later in this text.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to B$ be a function. A function $g: B \to A$ is an **inverse** of $f$ provided both of the following hold:
> - $\forall x \in A, g(f(x)) = x$
> - $\forall y \in B, f(g(y)) = y$

> [!TIP] Remark
> If an inverse of $f$ exists, then you can prove it is unique and it is usually denoted $f^{-1}: B \to A$. Moreover, for $b \in B$, the quantity $f^{-1}(b)$ is defined when the inverse of $f$ exists. Do not confuse this notation with the preimage.

There are three standard properties of functions that relate to the existence of an inverse

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A function $f: A \to B$ is **injective** (or **one-to-one**) provided for every $a_1, a_2 \in A$, if $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$ then $a_1 = a_2$.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A function $f: A \to B$ is **surjective** (or **onto**) provided for every $b \in B$, there exists $a \in A$ such that $f(a) = b$.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A function $f: A \to B$ is **bijective** if $f$ is both injective and surjective.

These definitions give an equivalent characterization for the existence of the inverse.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> The inverse of a function $f: A \to B$ exists if and only if $f$ is bijective.

Most functions are not usually bijective but, by restricting their domains and codomains, you can produce a new bijective function.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $f(x) = x^2$ is neither surjective nor injective. However, the function $g: (-\infty, 0) \to (0, \infty)$ given by $g(x) = x^2$ is bijective.

## Function Operations

There are many ways to construct new functions from old functions using **function operations**. Many of these are only designed when the domain and codomain are subsets of Euclidean space. Thus, for the remainder of this section, all functions will be vector-valued maps with vector variables. Pay attention to the domains and codomains here. 

First, the most basic function operation is composition.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be functions. The **composition of $g$ with $f$** is the function $(g \circ f): A \to C$ given by
> 
> $$
> (g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) \text{ for } x \in A
> $$
> 
> Note that $g \circ f$ is the name of the function. This means that the statement $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$ is a definition; it is not simply notation.

Second, you can take linear combinations of functions provided their domains are the same and their codomains are both equal to the same Euclidean space.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be functions. Fix a scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. A **linear combination of $f$ and $g$** is a function $(f+ \lambda g): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ given by
> 
> $$
> (f+ \lambda g)(x) = f(x) + \lambda g(x) \text{ for }x \in A
> $$

Third, you can define the dot product of two functions provided their domains are the same and their codomains are both equal to the same Euclidean space.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be functions. The **dot product of $f$ and $g$** is the real-valued function $f \cdot g): A \to \mathbb{R}$ given by
> 
> $$
> (f \cdot g)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x) \text{ for }x \in A
> $$

Fourth, you can define the product of a real-valued function with a vector valued map.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a vector-valued function. Let $\phi: A \to \mathbb{R}$. The **scalar product of $\phi$ and $f$** is the function $(\phi f): A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ given by
> 
> $$
> (\phi f)(x) = \phi(x)f(x) = \begin{bmatrix}
> \phi(x)f_1(x) \\
> \vdots \\
> \phi(x)f_m(x)
> \end{bmatrix} \text{ for }x \in A
> $$

Fifth and finally, you can define the quotient of real-valued maps.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ and $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be real-valued functions. Define $S \subseteq A$ by $S = \{x \in A: g(x) \ne 0\}$. The **quotient** of $f$ over $g$ is the function $(\tfrac{f}{g}): S \to \mathbb{R}$ given by
> 
> $$
> \left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} \text{ for }x \in S
> $$

These operations will suffice for now, but there are certainly more.

## Supremum and Infimum

Recall the definition for a supremum of a set.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Supremum of a set
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a subset of real numbers.
> - An element $b \in \mathbb{R}$ is an **upper bound for $S$** if $\forall x \in S, x \le b$.
> - An element $s \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **least upper bound of $S$** (or **supremum of $S$**) if $s$ is an upper bound for $S$ and, for every upper bound $b$ of $S$, $s \le b$. Denote $s = \sup(S)$.
> - An element $m \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **maximum of $S$** if $m = \sup(S)$ and $m \in S$.
> 
> If the supremum of $S$ exists, then $\sup(S) \in \mathbb{R}$ and, if not, write $\sup(S) = \infty$.

The infimum of a set is defined similarly

> [!NOTE] Definition: Infimum of a set
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a subset of real numbers.
> - An element $b \in \mathbb{R}$ is a **lower bound for $S$** if $\forall x \in S, x \ge b$.
> - An element $i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **greatest lower bound of $S$** (or **infimum of $S$**) if $i$ is an lower bound for $S$ and, for every lower bound $b$ of $S$, $i \ge b$. Denote $i = \inf(S)$.
> - An element $m \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **minimum of $S$** if $m = \sup(S)$ inf $m \in S$.
> 
> If the infimum of $S$ exists, then $\inf(S) \in \mathbb{R}$ and, if not, write $\inf(S) = -\infty$.

Two fundamental principles describe precisely when these quantities exist.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Least Upper Bound and Greatest Lower Bound Principles
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a subset of real numbers. Both of the following hold.
> 1. The supremum of $S$ exists if and only if there exists an upper bound for $S$.
> 2. The infimum of $S$ exists if and only if there exists a lower bound for $S$.

There are several equivalent definitions for the supremum of a set.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a set. Fix $s \in \mathbb{R}$. All of the following are equivalent.
> 
> 1. $s$ is the supremum of $S$
> 2. $-s$ is the infimum of $\{-x : x \in S\}$
> 3. $\left(\forall x \in S, x \le s\right)$ and $\left(\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists x \in S \text{ such that }x > s - \varepsilon\right)$

The supremum (and infimum) of a real-valued function on a subset of its domain is a shorthand for the supremum (and infimum) of its image on that subset of the domain.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Supremum and Infimum of a function
> Let $f: F \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. Let $A$ be a subset of its domain $D$. The **supremum of $f$ on $A$** and the **infimum of $f$ on $A$** are given by
> 
> $$
> \sup_{x \in A} f(x) := \sup\{f(x) : x \in A\}, \quad \inf_{x \in A} f(x) := \inf \{f(x) : x \in A\}
> $$

There are some standard properties of suprema and infima of functions.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ and $g: D \to \mathbb{R}$ be real-valued functions. Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of their domain $D$. All of the following hold
> 
> 1. If $A \subseteq B$ then $\sup_{x \in A}f(x) \le \sup_{x \in B}f(x)$ and $\inf_{x \in A}f(x) \ge \inf_{x \in B}f(x)$
> 2. $\sup_{x \in A}(f + g)(x) \le \sup_{x \in A}f(x) + \sup_{x \in A}g(x)$ and $\inf_{x \in A}(f + g)(x) \ge \inf_{x \in A}f(x) + \inf_{x \in A}g(x)$
> 3. If $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\lambda > 0$ then $\sup_{x \in A}(\lambda f)(x) = \lambda \sup_{x \in A}f(x)$ and $\inf_{x \in A}(\lambda f)(x) = \lambda \inf_{x \in A}f(x)$
> 4. If $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\lambda < 0$ then $\sup_{x \in A}(\lambda f)(x) = \lambda \inf_{x \in A}f(x)$ and $\inf_{x \in A}(\lambda f)(x) = \lambda \sup_{x \in A}f(x)$

> [!TIP] Remark
> If the suprema/infima do not exist, then the above statements can be suitably interpreted with $\pm \infty$ but you should be careful when writing proofs in such cases.
