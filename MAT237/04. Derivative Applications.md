# Chain Rule

We want to answer the following question.

> If a map is a composition of several or more maps, then how are the differentials of these maps related?

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Chain Rule
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. If the mappings $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to \mathbb{R}^k$ are differentiable at $a \in A^o$ and $f(a) \in B^o$ respectively, then their composition $h = g \circ f$ is differentiable at $a$. Moreover, its differential is a composition of linear maps,
> 
> $$
> dh_a = dg_{f(a)} \circ df_a
> $$
> 
> and its $k \times n$ Jacobian matrix is a product of a $k \times m$ Jacobian matrix with a $m \times n$ Jacobian matrix, namely,
> 
> $$
> Dh(a) = Dg(f(a)) Df(a)
> $$

> [!TIP] Remark
> The statement above requires that $a$ and $f(a)$ are interior points of $A$ and $B$ respectively. Be careful to check this assumption in practice since $a \in A^o$ does not necessarily imply $f(a) \in B^o$.

Informally, the chain rule says that:

> The differential of a composition is the composition of differentials.

## Basic Examples

The chain rule is algebraically elegant but it is quite messy to apply in practice.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ and $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = (x^2y, x^2 + y^2, \cos y) \quad g(x, y, z) = xz^3 + \sin y
> $$
> 
> Is $g \circ f$ differentiable at the point $(1, 0)$ and, if so, what is its differential?
> 
> Both $f$ and $g$ are $C^1$ and hence differentiable. By the chain rule, their composition $h = g \circ f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable. To calculate the Jacobian of $h$ at $(1, 0)$, we first calculate the Jacobians of $f$ at $(1, 0)$ and the Jacobian of $g$ of $f(1, 0) = (0, 1, 1)$. Note
> 
> $$
> Df(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 2xy & x^2 \\
> 2x & 2y \\
> 0 & -\sin y
> \end{bmatrix} \text{ so } Df(1, 0) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 1 \\
> 2 & 0 \\
> 0 & 0 \\
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> and similarly, as $f(1, 0) = (0, 1, 1)$,
> 
> $$
> Dg(x, y, z) = \begin{bmatrix}
> z^3 & \cos y & 3xz^2 
> \end{bmatrix} \text{ so } Dg(f(1, 0)) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & \cos 1 & 0
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> By the chain rule, the $1 \times 2$ Jacobian of $h$ at $(1, 0)$ is given by
> 
> $$
> Dh(1, 0) = Dg(f(1, 0))Df(1, 0) = \begin{bmatrix}
> 1 & \cos 1 & 0
> \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 & 1 \\
> 2 & 0 \\
> 0 & 0 \\
> \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
> 2\cos 1 & 1
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> Thus, the differential satisfies
> 
> $$
> dh_{(1, 0)}(v) = \begin{bmatrix}2\cos 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} v = 2(\cos 1)v_1 + v_2
> $$
> 
> for $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$.
> 
> Note that you can also express $h = g \circ f$ as 
> 
> $$
> h(x, y) = x^2y \cos^3 y + \sin(x^2 + y^2)
> $$
> 
> and compute the Jacobian $Dh(x, y)$ directly.

Unlike single variable calculus, the purpose of the multivariable chain rule is not to perform computations for explicit choices of functions. **Its primary goal is to express algebraic relationships between derivatives** and it is often used in proofs or algorithm.

> [!NOTE] Example
> The chain rule is commonly used to calculate derivatives of parametric curves, such as a composition of the form $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$. Let $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and let $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$. If $\gamma$ is differentiable at $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and $f$ is differentiable at $\gamma(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, then by the chain rule $f \circ \gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable at $t$ and
> 
> $$
> D(f \circ \gamma)(t) = (Df)(\gamma(t)) D\gamma(t)
> $$
> 
> Since $f$ is real-valued, the Jacobian of $f$ is the transpose of the gradient, namely $Df(x) = \nabla f(x)^T$ if $f$ is differentiable at $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Since $f \circ \gamma$ and $\gamma$ are single variable functions, their Jacobians are the $n \times 1$ derivative, namely, $D\gamma(t) = \gamma'(t)$ and $D(f\circ \gamma)(t) = (f \circ \gamma)'(t)$. Thus, the above formula may be written as 
> 
> $$
> (f \circ \gamma)'(t) = \nabla f(\gamma(t))^T \gamma'(t) = \nabla f(\gamma(t)) \cdot \gamma'(t)
> $$
> 
> This identity will be critical when we study implicit surfaces and tangent planes.

There is also a convenient consequence of the chain rule with $C^1$ functions.

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Composition of $C^1$ Functions
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ be open sets. If the function $f: U \to V$ is $C^1$ and the function $g: V \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is $C^1$, then their composition $g \circ f: U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is $C^1$.

## Variables, Leibniz Notation, and Chain Rule Trees

Computationally speaking, the chain rule is extensively used to express partial derivatives in other coordinate systems. This poses a serious notational challenge to the current approach with Jacobians and function notation.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider a composition of the form $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$. So assume $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ and $g: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ are both differentiable everywhere. By the chain rule, $h = g \circ f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable and you want to construct a general formula for its Jacobian in terms of partial derivatives of $f$ and $g$.
> 
> The Jacobian of $g$ at $f(a)$ and the Jacobian of $f = (f_1, f_2, f_3)$ at $a$ are
> 
> $$
> Dg(f(a)) = \begin{bmatrix}
> \partial_1 g(f(a)) & \partial_2 g(f(a)) & \partial_3 g(f(a))
> \end{bmatrix} \quad Df(a) = \begin{bmatrix}
> \partial_1 f_1(a) & \partial_2 f_1(a) \\ 
> \partial_1 f_2(a) & \partial_2 f_2(a) \\ 
> \partial_1 f_3(a) & \partial_2 f_3(a) \\ 
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> Multiplying this together, you get
> 
> $$
> Dh(a) = \nabla h(a)^T = \begin{bmatrix}
> \partial_1 g(f(a)) \partial_1 f_1(a) + \partial_2 g(f(a)) \partial_1 f_2(a) + \partial_3 g(f(a)) \partial_1 f_3(a) \\
> \partial_1 g(f(a)) \partial_2 f_1(a) + \partial_2 g(f(a)) \partial_2 f_2(a) + \partial_3 g(f(a)) \partial_2 f_3(a)
> \end{bmatrix}
> $$
> 
> which is valid but hard to parse.

This issue is alleviated by using a variable letter in place of a function, and by using the Leibniz notation for partial derivatives. To write the partial derivative of $g$ with respect to $x$ in **Leibniz notation**, we can write

$$
\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}
$$

This relies on the fact that $x$ denotes the first coordinate.

Often you may write

$$
u = g(x, y, z) \quad \text{and} \quad \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}
$$

The letter $u$ is technical a variable but we abuse notation to treat it as the function $g$. When using the chain rule, replacing functions with variables is helpful, because a variable can denote several different functions at once.

> [!NOTE] Example
> You can rewrite the previous example with Leibniz notation. Define the variables
> 
> $$
> u = g(x, y, z) \quad \text{and} \quad (x, y, z) = f(s, t)
> $$
> 
> where $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ and $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ are differentiable functions. The second equations implies that $x = f_1(s, t)$, $y = f_2(s, t)$, and $z = f_3(s, t)$. Note the letters $x$, $y$, and $z$ are being abused as both inputs of functions and as functions themselves.
> 
> We can abuse notation even further and write
> 
> $$
> u = g(x(s, t), y(s, t), z(s, t))
> $$
> 
> Then, we can write
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial s} &= \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial s} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} \\
> \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t} \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> which is much more elegant.

You may already notice the ease of calculating with Leibniz notation. Another great advantage is that you can remember the chain rule with trees.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the previous example with
> 
> $$
> u = g(x, y, z) \quad \text{and} \quad (x, y, z) = f(s, t)
> $$
> 
> where $g: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ and $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ are differentiable functions. The dependencies between the variables can be expressed as a tree
> 
> ![[chain rule tree 1.png]]
> 
> If you want to calculate $\partial u/ \partial s$, you collect the contribution for each branch ending with $s$:
> 
> ![[chain rule tree 2.png]]
> 
> which yields
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial s} = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial s} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} 
> $$
> 
> as above.

## Proof of the Chain Rule

The formal proof of the chain rule is difficult, but uses some intuitive elements.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Since $a \in A$ and $f(a) \in B$ are interior points of $A$ and $B$ respectively, there exists $\delta_f > 0$ and $\delta_g > 0$ such that $B_{\delta_f}(a) \subseteq A$ and $B_{\delta_g}(f(a)) \subseteq B$. Since $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g$ is differentiable at $f(a)$, the differentials $df_a$ and $dg_{f(a)}$ both exist. Define the functions $\varepsilon_f: B_{\delta_f}(0) \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $\varepsilon_g: B_{\delta_g}(0) \to \mathbb{R}^k$ by
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \forall v \ne 0, \varepsilon_f &= \dfrac{f(a + v) - f(a) - df_a(v)}{||v||} \text{ and }\varepsilon_f(0) = 0 \\
> \forall w \ne 0, \varepsilon_f &= \dfrac{g(f(a) + w) - g(f(a)) - dg_{f(a)}(w)}{||w||} \text{ and }\varepsilon_g(0) = 0
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Since $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g$ is differentiable at $f(a)$, it follows that $\varepsilon_f$ and $\varepsilon_g$ are continuous at $0$; that is,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \lim_{v \to 0} \varepsilon_f(v) = 0 \text{ and } \lim_{w \to 0} \varepsilon_g(w) = 0 \tag{$\triangle$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Now, we show that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \lim_{v \to 0} \dfrac{(g \circ f)(a + v) - g(f(a)) - dg_{f(a)}\circ df_a(v)}{||v||} = 0 \tag{$\star$}
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> By the definition of $\varepsilon_f$, observe that
> 
> $$
> (g \circ f)(a + v) = g(f(a + v)) = g(f(a) + df_a(v) + ||v||\varepsilon_f(v))
> $$
> 
> By the definition of $\varepsilon_g$, with $w = \eta(v) = df_a(v) + ||v||\varepsilon_f(v)$, it follows by linearity of $dg_{f(a)}$ that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> (g \circ f)(a + v) &= g(f(a)) + dg_{f(a)}(\eta(v)) + ||\eta(v)||\varepsilon_g(\eta(v)) \\
> &= g(f(a)) + dg_{f(a)} \circ df_a(v) + ||v||dg_{f(a)}(\varepsilon_f(v)) + ||\eta(v)||\varepsilon_g(\eta(v)) \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> To show $(\star)$, it therefore suffices to show that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{v \to 0} dg_{f(a)} (\varepsilon_f(v)) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{v \to 0} \dfrac{||\eta(v)||}{||v||} \varepsilon_g(\eta(v)) = 0
> $$
> 
> The first limit follows from $(\triangle)$ and since $dg_{f(a)}$ is a linear map and hence continuous at $0$ with $dg_{f(a)}(0) = 0$. For the second limit, notice $\eta(v) = df_a(v) + ||v|| \varepsilon_f(v) \to 0$ as $v \to 0$ by  $(\triangle)$ and continuity of the linear map $df_a$. Since $\varepsilon_g$ is continuous at $0$. It follows that
> 
> $$
> \lim_{v \to 0} \varepsilon_g (\eta(v)) = \varepsilon_g(\eta(0)) = \varepsilon_g(0) = 0
> $$
> 
> Thus, by a version of the squeeze theorem, the second limit is equal to zero, provided the function $||\eta(v)||/||v||$ is bounded for $b$ in a small punctured ball centered at $0$.
> 
> Notice that
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{\eta(v)}{||v||} = \dfrac{1}{||v||} df_a(v) + \varepsilon_f(v) = df_a\left(\dfrac{v}{||v||}\right) + \varepsilon_f(v)
> $$
> 
> By $(\triangle)$, $\varepsilon_f(v)$ is bounded in $\mathbb{R}^m$ for $v$ in a small open ball near $0$. The unit sphere $S^{n-1} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is compact so its image under the continuous linear map $u \mapsto df_a(u)$ is compact and hence bounded in $\mathbb{R}^m$. Thus, the image of the map $df_a(v / ||v||)$ for $v \ne 0$ is also bounded in $\mathbb{R}^m$. This implies that the image of $\eta(v)/ ||v||$ inside a small punctured ball centred at $0$ is bounded so the scalar quantity $||\eta(v)|| / ||v||$ is bounded, as desired. This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

# Mean Value Theorem

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Mean Value Theorem
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and let $a, b \in U$. Let $f$ be a differentiable real-valued function on $U$. If $U$ contains the line segment $L$ from $a$ to $b$ then there exists $c \in L$ such that
> 
> $$
> f(b) - f(a) = \nabla f(c) \cdot (b - a)
> $$

^0042dc

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Define $\gamma: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by $\gamma(t) = (1 - t)a + tb$ for $0 \le t \le 1$, so $\gamma([0, 1]) = L$ by definition.
> 
> Since $f$ is defined on $U$ which contains $L$, we can define the single variable function $g: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(t) = f(\gamma(t))$ for $0 \le t \le 1$.
> 
> Since $\gamma$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$ and $f$ is differentiable on $U$ which contains $L$ and hence $\gamma((0, 1))$, it follows by the chain rule that **$g$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$**. Since $\gamma$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ and $f$ is continuous on $L = \gamma([0, 1])$, it follows that **$g$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$**.
> 
> By the single variable mean value theorem, there exists $s \in (0, 1)$ such that
> 
> $$
> g'(s) = \dfrac{g(1) - g(0)}{1 - 0} = f(\gamma(1)) - f(\gamma(0)) = f(b) - f(a)
> $$
> 
> By the chain rule,
> 
> $$
> g'(s) = (f \circ \gamma)'(s) = \nabla f(\gamma(s))\cdot  \gamma'(s) = \nabla f(c) \cdot (b - a)
> $$
> 
> where $c = \gamma(s) \in L$. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the set $U = B_3(0, 0)$ and the function $f: U \to \mathbb{R}$ as $f(x, y) = xy^2$ for all $(x, y) \in U$. Consider the points $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$ contained in $U$ connected by the line segment $L = \{(t, 2t): t \in [0, 1]\}$.
> 
> Since polynomials are always differentiable, then $f$ is differentiable. Furthermore, the open ball $U$ is open. Lastly, $L \subseteq U$ since 
> 
> $$
> \sqrt{t^2 + (2t)^2} = \sqrt{5} |t| \le \sqrt{5} < 3
> $$
> 
> for all $t \in [0, 1]$. Then, by the [[#^0042dc|mean value theorem]], there exists some $c \in L$ such that
> 
> $$
> f(1, 2) - f(0, 0) = \nabla f(c) \cdot ((1, 2) - (0, 0))
> $$

Note, that the mean value theorem does not hold for vector-valued functions.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ by $f(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$. Note that $f(0) = (1, 0)$ and $f(\pi) = (-1, 0)$ but there does not exist $c \in (0, \pi)$ such that
> 
> $$
> f(\pi) - f(0) = Df(c) \cdot (\pi - 0)
> $$
> 
> We can verify this directly since the equation states that $(-2, 0) = (-\pi \sin c \pi \cos c)$ If $\cos c = 0$, then $\sin c = \pm 1$ which shows that no $c$ can satisfy the equation.

A couple consequences of mean value theorem are listened below.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and $C^1$ path-connected. Let $F: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be differentiable. The Jacobian $DF(x)$ is the $m \times n$ zero matrix for all $x \in U$ if and only if $F$ is a constant map.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> It suffices to prove this for each component of $F$, so we assume that $F$ is real-valued.
> 
> If $F$ is constant, then it is immediate that $\nabla F(c) = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for all $x \in U$.
> 
> Conversely, assume $\nabla F(x) = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for all $x \in U$. Fix $a, b \in U$. There exists a path $\gamma: [0, 1] \to U$ such that $\gamma$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$, $\gamma$ is $C^1$ on $(0, 1)$, $\gamma(0) = a$, and $\gamma(1) = b$.
> 
> Define the map $f: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(t) = F(\gamma(t))$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$. Since $\gamma$ and $F$ are continuous, $f$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$. Since $\gamma$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$ and $F$ is differentiable, it follows that $f$ is differentiable on $(0, 1)$ by the chain rule. By the single variable mean value theorem, there exists $c \in (0, 1)$ such that
> 
> $$
> f'(c) = f(1) - f(0) = F(\gamma(1)) - F(\gamma(0)) = F(b) - F(a)
> $$
> 
> On the other hand, by the chain rule
> 
> $$
> f'(c) = Df(c) = D(F \circ \gamma)(c) = \nabla F(\gamma(c)) \cdot \gamma'(c) = 0
> $$
> 
> so $F(b) = F(a)$. Since $a, b$ were arbitrary, we conclude that $F$ is constant. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Corollary
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and $C^1$ path-connected. Let $F: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $G: U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be differentiable. If $DF(x) = DG(x)$ for all $x \in U$, then there exists a constant $C \in \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $F(x) = G(x) + C$ for all $x \in U$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> This follows quickly from the previous result with $H(x) = F(x) - G(x)$.

There is a recurring pattern in the above proofs.

> Create a path between points in the set. Produce a single variable function by composing the real-valued function with the path. Apply single variable calculus to this function.

Notice how the chain rule is critical here, as it translated change in one variable to chain in many variables.

# Local Extrema and Critical Points

## Local Extreme Value Theorem

The notion of local extrema in single variable calculus generalizes nicely.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ is a real-valued function. Let $a \in A$. The map $f$ has a **local maximum** at $a$ if there exists $\delta >0$ such that $f(a) \ge f(x)$ for all $x \in A \cap B_\delta(a)$. If so, the value $f(a)$ is a local maximum value of $f$ on $A$.
> 
> A **local minimum** and **local minimum value** are defined similarly. A **local extremum** is a local maximum or local minimum.

> [!TIP] Remark
> If you drop the "local", then the meaning of the word is global. For example, a maximum is a global maximum.

Local extrema can exist on the interior or boundary of a domain. Moreover, every global extremum is a local extremum but the converse is not true.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Local extrema can be difficult to identify. Consider the function
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = \sin(x) \sin(y) e^{-x^2 - y^2}(x + y)
> $$
> 
> which is shown below
> 
> ![[local extrema 1.png]]
> 
> The gradient vector field of $f$ hints at how you might use derivatives to locate these extrema
> 
> ![[local extrema 1 graph.png]]
> 
> Notice that the gradient appears to vanish at local extrema. This is reasonable since the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent. However, the gradient also vanishes at the origin, yet the origin is not a local extrema.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the graph of the function $g(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$ on $A = [-2, 2]^2$ shown below.
> 
> ![[local extrema 2.png]]
> 
> Notice that $g$ has no local extrema on the interior of $A$. Moreover, $g$ attains a local maximum at $(2, 0)$ and $(-2, 0)$ and local minima at $(0, 2)$ and $(0, -2)$. All of these occur on the boundary of $A$.
> 
> ![[local extrema 2 graph.png]]
> 
> Unlike the previous example, the gradient vector field does not give any information about these local extrema because they are not interior points. They lie on the boundary of the domain so the gradient does not need to vanish there.
> 
> The gradient also vanishes at the origin $(0, 0)$, that is $\nabla f(0, 0) = 0$, but this is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. If you compare with the graph the origin corresponds to a "saddle point".

This leads to the discovery of a crucial result.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Local Extreme Value Theorem
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. If $a$ is an interior point of $A$ that $f$ has a local extremum at $a$, then either $\nabla f(a) = 0$ or $\nabla f(a)$ does not exist.
> 
> Note that this only holds for the interior of $A$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> It suffices to prove that if $\nabla f(a)$ exists then $\nabla f(a) = 0$. Fix $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$ and let $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ denote the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. There exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that the line segment $\{a + he_j: h \in (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\}$ lies inside $A$. Thus, we may define $g: (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(h) = f(a + he_j)$. As $\nabla f(a)$ exists, the partial derivative $\partial_j f(a)$ exists so
> 
> $$
> g'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{g(h) - g(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + he_j) - f(a)}{h} = \partial_j f(a)
> $$
> 
> and hence $g$ is differentiable at $0$. Since $f$ has a local extremum at $a \in A$, it follows that $g$ has a local extremum at $0 \in \mathbb{R}$.  By the single variable local extreme value theorem, we have that $\partial_j f(a) = g'(0) = 0$. Since $j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $\nabla f(a) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

## Critical Points

The local extreme value theorem suggests a generalization of critical points.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Critical Points
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. A point $a \in A$ is a **critical point** of $f$ if $a$ is an interior point of $A$ and either $\nabla f(a) = 0$ or $\nabla f(a)$ does not exist.

This, combined with the local extreme value theorem, yields the following.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. If $a \in A$ is a local extremum of $f$, then either $a$ is a boundary point of $A$ or $a$ is a critical point of $f$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Saddle Points
> Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f: A \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. An interior point $a \in A$ is a **saddle point** of $f$ if $a$ is an interior point of $A$, $\nabla f(a) = 0$, and $f$ does not have a local extremum at $a$.

Remember a critical point may occur also when the gradient is not defined.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the following function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$:
> 
> $$
> f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = ||(x_1, \ldots, x_n)|| = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \ldots + x_n^2}
> $$
> 
> To find its critical points, start by finding its partial. For $1 \le j \le n$ and $x \ne 0$,
> 
> $$
> \partial_jf(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \dfrac{x_j}{\sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}}
> $$
> 
> so the $j$th partial exists and is continuous on $\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : x \ne 0\} = \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$. The calculations above imply that
> 
> $$
> \nabla f(x) = \dfrac{x}{||x||}
> $$
> 
> Since the gradient is not defined at the origin, the point $x = 0$ is a critical point of $f$. Moreover, $\nabla f(x) \ne 0$ for any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ so $f$ does not have any critical points on the domain of $\nabla f$. By the local extreme value theorem, $f$ does not have any local extrema on $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$. The only possible local extrema of $f$ is therefore at the critical point $0$.
> 
> Indeed, the origin is a local minimum of $f$.

# Optimization

We've established two tools so far: the **extreme value theorem** and the **local extreme value theorem**. With these tools, we develop the following general strategy

> Determine whether global extrema must exist. Identify the critical points on the interior of the domain. Check the boundary for possible extrema. 

First, we will consider a common scenario: **a differentiable function on a compact domain**.

> [!NOTE] Example
> You can optimize the function
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 6y
> $$
> 
> on the closed disc $A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 \le 16\}$. Since $f$ is continuous and $A$ is compact, the extreme value theorem implies that $f$ attains a global maximum and global minimum on $A$. A global extremum of $f$ will either belong to 
> 
> $$
> A^o = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 < 16\} \quad \text{or} \quad \partial A = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 = 16\}
> $$
> 
> So you must divide into cases.
> 
> If $p$ is a global extremum (and hence a local extremum) in the interior of $A$, then since $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}^2$ it follows by the local extreme value theorem that $\nabla f(p) = (0, 0)$. In other words, you must locate the critical points of $f$ belonging to $A$. If a point $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfies $\nabla f(x, y) = (0, 0)$, then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &(\partial_1f(x, y), \partial_2 f(x, y)) = (0, 0) \\
> \implies& (2x + 4, 2y - 6) = (0, 0) \\
> \implies& x = -2, y = 3
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Therefore, the only critical point is $(-2, 3)$ and $(-2)^2 + 3^2 < 164 so this point lies inside $A^o$.
> 
> Next, you must locale possible extrema of $f$ on the boundary $\partial A$. This circle of radius $4$ can be parameterized by
> 
> $$
> \gamma(t) = (4 \cos t, 4 \sin t), 0 \le t \le 2\pi
> $$
> 
> so you must optimize the single variable function $g: [0, 2\pi] \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by
> 
> $$
> g(t) = f \circ \gamma(t) = (4\cos t)^2 + (4 \sin t)^2 + 4(4 \cos t) - 6(4 \sin t) = 16 + 16 \cos t - 24 \sin t
> $$
> 
> For $t \in (0, 2 \pi)$, since $g'(t) = -16 \sin t - 24 \cos t$, you can see that $g'(t) = 0$ implies that 
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &-16 \sin t - 24 \cos t = 0
> \implies & \cos t = 0 \text{ or } \tan t = -\dfrac{3}{2} \\
> \implies & t = \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2} \text{ or }t = \pi + \arctan(-\tfrac{3}{2}), 2\pi + \arctan(\tfrac{-3/2})
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> It is also possible that the endpoints $t = 0$ and $t = 2\pi$ are extrema of $g$. By standard trigonometric identities, you can verify that all 6 of these values of $t$ corresponds to the points $\gamma(t)$ given by
> 
> $$
> (0, 4), (0, -4), \left(\dfrac{-8}{\sqrt{13}}, \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{13}}\right), \left(\dfrac{8}{\sqrt{13}}, \dfrac{-12}{\sqrt{13}}\right), (4, 0)
> $$
> 
> These correspond to all candidates for possible extrema for $f$ on the boundary of $A$.
> 
> All that remains is to compare the values of $f$ at all possible extrema on the interior and boundary of $A$. You can verify that
> 
> $$
> f(-2, 3) = -13, f(0, 4) = -8, f(0, -4) = 40, f\left(\dfrac{-8}{\sqrt{13}}, \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{13}}\right) \approx -12.8, f\left(\dfrac{8}{\sqrt{13}}, \dfrac{-12}{\sqrt{13}}\right) \approx 44.8, f(4, 0) = 32
> $$
> 
> This implies that the global minimum of $f$ on $A$ is $f(-2, 3) = -13$ an the global maximum is $f\left(\tfrac{8}{\sqrt{13}}, \tfrac{-12}{\sqrt{13}}\right) \approx 44.8$.

The example above highlights the general strategy:

1. Find the critical points on the interior
2. Parameterize the boundary and find the critical points on the boundary
3. Check the endpoints of the parameterized boundary
4. Compare values for all candidates to find global extremum

The boundary above was fairly simple and could be parametrized with a single curve. The boundary can quickly get more complicated and you will need to divide-and-conquer.

> [!NOTE] Example
> You can find the global extremum of 
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = x^2 - 3xy + 4y^2 -4x - 2y + 24
> $$
> 
> on the rectangle $A = [0, 4] \times [0, 2]$. Since $f$ is continuous and $A$ is compact, the extreme value theorem implies that $f$ attains a global maximum and global minimum. A global extremum of $f$ will belong either to the interior or the boundary of $A$, so we do casework.
> 
> If $p$ is a global extremum in the interior of $A$, then since $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}^2$, it follows that $\partial f(p) = (0, 0)$. If a point $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfies $\nabla f(x, y) = (0, 0)$, then
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> & (\partial_1 f(x, y), \partial_2 f(x, y)) = (0, 0) \\
> \implies & (2x - 2y - 3, -2x + 8y - 2) = (0, 0) \\
> \implies & x = 3, y = 1
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Since $(3, 1) \in A$, we accept it as a possible global extremum of $f$ on $A$.
> 
> Now, assume $p \in \partial A$. We must determine the extrema of $f$ on the boundary of its domain, which consists of $4$ line segments labelled below.
> 
> ![[optimization 1.png]]
> 
> The strategy is to parametrize each piece of the boundary and optimize $f$ along these pieces.
> 
> **Segment 1**. The line segment $\ell_1$ can be parameterized as $\gamma_1(t) = (t, 0)$ for $0 \le t \le 4$. You want to find the global extrema of the single-variable function.
> 
> $$
> g_1(t) = f(\gamma_1(t)) = f(t, 0) = t^2 - 4t + 24, 0 \le t \le 4 
> $$
> 
> As $g_1'(t) = 2t - 4$, the only critical point is $t = 2$. Thus, the possible extrema of $f$ on $\ell_1$ occur at $\gamma_1(2) = (2, 0)$ and the endpoints $\gamma_1(0) = (0, 0)$ and $\gamma_1(4) = \gamma_1(4) = (4, 0)$.
> 
> **Segment 2** The line segment $\ell_2$ can be parameterized as $\gamma_2(t) = (4, t)$ for $0 \le t \le 2$. The function
> 
> $$
> g_2(t) = f \circ \gamma_2(t) = 4t^2 - 10t + 24, 0 \le t \le 2
> $$
> 
> satisfies $g_2'(t) = 8t - 10$ so $t = 5/4$ is the only critical point. Thus, the possible extrema of $f$ occur at $\gamma_2(5/4) = (4, 5/4)$ as well as the endpoints $(4, 0)$ and $(4, 2)$.
> 
> You can do something similar for the other two line segments. This gives us the following points to check:
> 
> $$
> (3, 1), (2, 0), (4, 5/4), (4, 2), (0, 1/4), (0, 0), (4, 0), (4, 2), (0, 2)
> $$
> 
> We have the global maximum of $f$ on $A$ as $f(0, 2) = 36$ and the global minimum of $f(3, 1) = 17$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> You can also optimize the function
> 
> $$
> f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 6y
> $$
> 
> on the **unbounded** closed domain $A = \mathbb{R}^2$. We must first prove that $f$ possesses global extrema. To do this, we need to understand the limiting behaviour of $f(x, y)$ as $||(x, y)|| \to \infty$.
> 
> Here, we have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \lim_{||(x, y)|| \to \infty} f(x, y) &= \lim_{||(x, y)|| \to \infty} (x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 6y) \\
> &= \lim_{||(x, y)|| \to \infty} ||(x, y)||^2 \left(1 + \dfrac{4x}{||(x, y)||^2} - \dfrac{6y}{||(x, y)||^2}\right)
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> You can verify that $x/||(x, y)||^2 \to 0$ and $y/||(x, y)||^2 \to 0$ as $||(x, y)|| \to \infty$. By a product limit law for infinite limits, the above is equal to $\infty$. This automatically implies that $f$ does not have a global maximum. This does imply that $f$ has a global minimum on $A$.
> 
> Then, we can find it. The domain $A = \mathbb{R}^2$ has empty boundary, so the global minimum must occur on the interior. Let $p \in \mathbb{R}^2$ be a minimum point of $f$. Since $p$ is a global minimum point lying inside the interior of $A$ and $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}^2$, it follows that $\nabla f(p) = 0$ by the local extreme value theorem. The only critical point we have where $\nabla f(p) = 0$ is the point $(-2, 3)$. Since this is the only critical point and $f$ must have a global minimum, this point is necessarily the global minimum of $f$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

There are still many problems which we cannot immediately solve.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ given by
> 
> $$
> f(x) = x_1^2 - x_2^2 + \cdots + (-1)^{n+1} x_n^2
> $$
> 
> on the closed unit ball $B = \overline{B_1(0)}$. Since $f$ is continuous and $B$ is compact, you can proceed by the strategies from before. Note
> 
> $$
> \nabla f(x) = (2x_1, -2x_2, \ldots, (-1)^{n+1} 2x_n)
> $$
> 
> so $\nabla f(x) = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$ As $f$ is differentiable, $x = 0$ is the only critical point. 
> 
> Now, you must find the possible extrema of $f$ on the boundary 
> 
> $$
> \partial B = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n: x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 = 1\}
> $$
> 
> the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Here, we cannot easily parameterize the boundary, which is a major issue.

# Tangent Spaces

This chapter formalizes the idea of the "tangent plane to a set".

## Tangent Vectors, Spaces, and Planes

Recall from Chapter 3 that if $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at $t \in \mathbb{R}$, then its derivative $\gamma'(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ defines a direction vector defining the tangent line of $\gamma$ at $t$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> You are in a vehicle traveling for 12 hours on the Earth's surface, which is modelled as a sphere $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$. Your position is given by a parametric curve $\gamma: (0, 12) \to \mathbb{R}^3$. Halfway through the trip, you are passing through Saskatoon. Your position is $\gamma(6) \in S$ and your velocity is $\gamma'(6) \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
> 
> We want to intuitively define the tangent plane at $\gamma(6)$. It should be the plane of all possible velocity vectors from similarly defined $\gamma$ at this point e.g. people travelling through Saskatoon.
> 
> We must enforce that the path $\gamma$ must stay on the Earth's surface. If you are travelling on the Earth's surface, it must be true that $\gamma(t) \in S$ for all $t \in (0, 12)$ or equivalently, $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) \subseteq S$. This condition is necessary because it restricts the possible velocities.
> 
>  You can drive in at least two different directions through Saskatoon, e.g. north-south and east-west. You may therefore guess that the set of possible velocities is a 2-dimensional subspace of 3 spanned by these two directions.

Thus, we have the following definition

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $p$ be a point in $S$. A vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **tangent vector of $S$ at $p$** if there exists an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ containing $0$ and a map $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\gamma$ is $C^1$, $\gamma(I) \subseteq S$, $\gamma(0) = p$, and $\gamma'(0) = v$.

You can interpret this physically as

> There exists a particle moving along $S$ through $p$ with velocity $v$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Define the set
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> and the point $p = (1, 2, 4) \in S$. What are you tangent vectors of $S$ at $p$. There are an infinite number of differentiable curves on $S$ that pass through $p$. For example
> 
> 1. $\gamma_1: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by $\gamma_1(t) = (1 + t, 2 - 3t, 9 - (1 + t)^2 - (2 - 3t)^2)$
> 2. $\gamma_2: (-6, 0.3) \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by $\gamma_2(t) = (\cos t, 2e^t, 9 - (\cos t)^2 - (2e^t)^2)$
> 3. $\gamma_2: (-1.5, 1.5) \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by $\gamma_3(t) = (1 + t^2, 2 + t^3, 9 - (1 + t^2)^2 - (2 + t^3)^2)$
> 
> You can verify that these are all $C^1$ and their range is a subset of the set $S$. Notice that $\gamma_1(0) = \gamma_2(0) = \gamma_3(0) = p$ and, by direct calculation,
> 
> 1. $\gamma_1(0) = (1, -3, 10)$
> 2. $\gamma_2(0) = (0, 2, -8)$
> 3. $\gamma_3(0) = (0, 0, 0)$
> 
> Thus, all three of those vectors are all tangent vectors of $S$ at $p$ by definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Tangent Spaces
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $p$ be a point in $S$. The **tangent space of $S$ at $p$**, denoted $T_pS$ is the set of tangent vectors to $S$ at $p$. In other words,
> 
> $$
> T_p S = \{v \in \mathbb{R}^n: \text{$v$ is a tangent vector of $S$ at $p$}\}
> $$

We cannot verify that this set forms a vector subspace just yet, but we can verify that the zero vector will always belong to the tangent space.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> For any set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and any point $p \in S$. Take the $C^1$ map $\gamma: (-1, 1) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\gamma(t) = p$. Note that, $(-1, 1)$ is open with $0 \in (-1, 1)$, $\gamma(0) = p$, and $\mathrm{im}(\gamma) = \{p\} \subseteq S$.
> 
> Then, $\gamma'(0) = 0$ shows that $0 \in T_p S$. $\blacksquare$ 

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider $S = \{(x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and $p = (1, 2, 4)$. We can verified that
> 
> 1. $v_1 = (1, -3, 10)$
> 2. $v_2 = (0, 2, -8)$
> 3. $v_3 = (0, 0, 0)$
> 4. $v_4 = (-2, -1, 8)$
> 
> are all tangent vectors of $S$ at $p$ and hence they are elements of $T_p S$. Producing all tangent vectors seems impossible since you would have to consider all possible parametric curves passing through $p$. Luckily, $S$ is a graph defined by $f(x, y) = 9 - x^2 - y^2$:
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, f(x, y)): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> This allows the tangent vectors $v_1$ and $v_4$ to be created with straight lines passing through $(1, 2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ in the domain of $f$. The line $(1 + t, 2 - 3t)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ corresponds to the curve $\gamma_1(t)$ on the graph and the line $(1, - 2t, 2 - t)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ corresponds to the curve $\gamma_4(t)$ on the graph.
> 
> These are inspired by the directional derivative of $f$. In particular, you can check that
> 
> $$
> \gamma_1'(0) = (1, -3, D_{(-1, 3)} f(1, 2)) \quad \text{and} \quad \gamma_4'(0) = (-2, -1, D_{(-2, -1)} f(1, 2))
> $$
> 
> This relation holds for generally.
> 
> Fix $w = (w_1, w_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and $a = (1, 2)$ so $p = (a, f(a)) = (1, 2, 4)$. Define $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ to be the straight line defined by $g(t) = a + tw$. Then define $\gamma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ by
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \gamma(t) = (g(t), f \circ g(t)) &= (a + tw, f (a + tw)) \\
> &= (1 + tw_1, 2 + tw_2, f(1 + tw_1, 2 + tw_2))
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> So $\gamma(0) = (a, f(a)) = p$ and $\gamma(t) \in S$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. In other words, $\gamma(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq S$. You can verify that both $g$ and $f$ are $C^1$ and hence differentiable on their domains. Thus, $\gamma$ is $C^1$ and 
> 
> $$
> \gamma'(t) = (g'(t), \nabla f(g(t)) \cdot g'(t))
> $$
> 
> Overall, this implies that
> 
> $$
> \gamma'(0) = (g'(0), \nabla f(g(0)) \cdot g'(0)) = (w, \nabla f(a) \cdot w)
> $$
> 
> is a tangent vector of $S$ at $p = \gamma(0) = (1, 2, 4)$ By calculation, you can check that $\nabla f(1, 2) = (-2 ,-4)$, sp the above becomes
> 
> $$
> \gamma'(0) = (w_1, w_2, -2w_1 - 4w_2) = w_1 (1, 0, -2) + w_1 (0, 1, 4)
> $$
> 
> Thus, any linear combination of $(1, 0, -2)$ and $(0, 1, -4)$ is a tangent vector of $S$ at $p$. This argument proves that
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}
> 1 \\ 0 \\ -2
> \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}
> 0 \\ 1 \\ -4
> \end{bmatrix}\right\} \subseteq T_p S
> $$

> [!NOTE] Definition: Tangent Planes
> Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and $p \in S$. The **tangent plane of $S$ at $p$**, denoted $p + T_p S$ is the tangent plane translated to $p$. That is,
> 
> $$
> p + T_p S = \{p + v: v \in T_p S\}
> $$

## Tangent Space of a Graph

Unfortunately, there is a major drawback to the definition of the tangent space.

> How do you explicitly describe all differentiable parametric curves **lying inside $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$**.

While resolving this problem for sets written in implicit form or parametric form will be much harder, you can luckily resolve it for graphs.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> Let $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ be open. Let $F: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n - k}$ be $C^1$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be the graph of $F$ so 
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, F(x)): x \in V\}
> $$
> 
> Let $I$ be open and let $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^n$. The function $\gamma$ is $C^1$ and $\gamma(I) \subseteq S$ if and only if there exists a $C^1$ function $g: I \to \mathbb{R}^k$ such that $g(I) \subseteq V$ and $\gamma(t) = (g(t), F(g(t)))$ for $t \in I$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider 
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2): x, y \in \mathbb{R}^2\}
> $$
> 
> at the point $p = (1, 2, 4)$. Notice that $S$ is the graph of the function $f(x, y) = 9 - x^2 - y^2$. Let $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be a differentiable curve such that $\gamma(I) \subseteq S$ with $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ open. By the above Lemma, if $\gamma = (\gamma_1, \gamma_2, \gamma_3)$, then for all $t \in I$,
> 
> $$
> \gamma_3(t) = f(\gamma_1(t), \gamma_2(t)) = 9 - (\gamma_1(t))^2 - (\gamma_2(t))^2
> $$

With the above lemma, we can determine the tangent space of a graph.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Tangent Space of Graphs
> Let $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ be open. Let $F: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ be $C^1$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be the graph of $F$ so 
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, F(x)): x \in V\}
> $$
> 
> For $a \in V$ and $p = (a, F(a))$, all of the following hold:
> 
> 1. $T_p S = \{(w, dF_a(w)) \in \mathbb{R}^k \times \mathbb{R}^{n - k}: w \in \mathbb{R}^k\}$
> 2. $T_p S = \mathrm{span}\left\{(e_1, \partial_1 F(a)), \ldots, (e_k, \partial_k F(a))\right\}$ where $\{e_1, \ldots, e_k\}$ is the standard $\mathbb{R}^k$ basis.
> 3. $T_p S$ is a $k$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

This allows you to quickly find the tangent plane to a graph.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the set
> 
> $$
> S = \{(x, y, 9 - x^2 - y^2): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}
> $$
> 
> at the point $p = (1, 2, 4)$. We have $p = (a, f(a))$ where $a = (1, 2)$.
> 
> $$
> T_p S = \{(w, df_a(w)): w \in \mathbb{R}^2\}
> $$
> 
> We have
> 
> $$
> df_a(w) = f(a) \cdot w = \nabla f(1, 2) \cdot w = (-2, -4)  \cdot w = -2w_1 - 4w_2
> $$
> 
> so, you may conclude that 
> 
> $$
> T_p S = \{(w_1, w_2, -2w_1 - 4w_2): w_1, w_2 \in \mathbb{R}\} = \mathrm{span}\{(1, 0, 2), (0, 1, -4)\}
> $$

Thus, we have formalized the idea of tangent planes.  For sets in explicit form, you can compute them and they are naturally the translation of subspace in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

# Smooth Manifolds

> If a set is described by nonlinear equations, then what is its "dimension"? How do you define this concept of dimension"?

The above inquiry generated the heuristic idea of a manifold, which we will now define.

A good definition for whether a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a manifold should have three key features

1. It should ensure that $S$ is "lower dimensional"; an open ball should not be a manifold, but the unit sphere and a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$ should presumably be $2$-dimensional manifolds.
2. The definition should characterize if $S$ is "smooth"; a sphere should be considered smooth but a cone should not be.
3. It should not depend on how the set $S$ is written; a hemisphere should be considered a manifold regardless of whether you describe it in explicit form, implicit form, or parametric form.

We start with out definitions of the tangent space and tangent plane. First, a tangent plane can have a formal notion of dimension from linear algebra (if the tangent space is indeed a subspace). Second, a tangent plane is "flat", which is as smooth as you can get. Third, the tangent space does not depend on how the set is described.

We can build on the concept of differentiability: a smooth manifold should presumably be locally well-approximated by the tangent plane.

> A set $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold if you zoom in close enough and it always looks like a $k$-dimensional plane in $\mathbb{R}^n$

Or formally,

> A set $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold if the tangent space $T_p S$ is a $k$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ at every point $p$ in $S$.

The problem with this definition is that it is very hard to compute tangent spaces from the definition, so it is hard to verify this in any example. We only have one theorem to do this for tangent spaces when $S$ is the graph of a differentiable function.

## Issues with Defining Manifolds

First, we explore some informal examples.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Three examples of curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$ include a circle given by $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, a cusp given by $x^2 = y^3$, and a figure eight given by $x^4 = x^2 - y^2$.
> 
> ![[manifold curve 1.png]]
> 
> For almost every point $p$ on each curve $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ , you can see that the tangent space $T_pS$ is usually a one-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$. For the circle, every point satisfies this property, so it should presumably be a smooth curve. The cusp and figure 8, however, should not be.
> 
> For the cusp, there is an issue at the origin. If a particle on the cusp $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ moves through the origin, then it must always "stop" and turn around which suggests the tangent space at the origin is only the zero vector $T_{(0, 0)}S = \{(0, 0)\}$. The dimension of the tangent space is $0$, not $1$.
> 
> For the figure eight, the tangent space at the origin appears to be the union of two subspaces, namely $T_{(0, 0)}S = \{(u, v) \in \mathbb{R}^2: u = \pm v\}$. This is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Three examples of surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^2$ include a sphere given by $x^2 + y^z + z^2 = 16$, a cube given by $\partial [-3, 3]^3$, and a two sided cone given by $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$.
> 
> ![[manifold curve 2.png]]
> 
> Similarly, at almost every point $p$ on one of these sets $S \subseteq \mathbb{B}^3$, you can see that the tangent space $T_pS$ is usually a $2$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$. For the sphere, every point satisfies this property, so the sphere should presumably be a smooth surface. 
> 
> For the cube, the edges and corners have tangent space of either $1$ (edge) or $0$ (corner) dimensional, so the dimension drops again.
> 
> For the cone, the origin has a problem. A particle can pass straight through the origin along the cone, so every straight line lying on the cone should belong to the tangent plane. This means that the tangent space of the cone $S$ at the origin is the entire cone itself, which is not a subspace.

Our first informal guess for smooth manifolds was flawed because tangent spaces for many reasonable sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ do not need to have constant dimension (when they are a subspace) and, even worse they do not need to look anything like subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

However, if the set $S$ is a graph of a differentiable function, then we know that the tangent space at eery point is always a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the same definition. The ingenious idea is to instead define smooth manifolds using **graphs**.

## Definition of a Smooth Manifold

We have the following informal definition:

> A set $S$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold if you zoom in close enough and it always looks like the graph of a function with $k$-variables in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Smooth Manifolds at a Point
> Fix $k ,n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $k < n$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $p$ be a point in $S$. The set $S$ is a **($k$-dimensional) smooth manifold at $p$** if there exists an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ containing $p$ such that $S \cap U$ is a graph of a $C^1$ function $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n - k}$ where $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ is open.

> [!TIP] Remark
> Note the following. First, the function $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ may depend on the point $p$. Second, the choice of $f$ is not necessarily unique. Third, the set $S \cap U$ is **a** graph of $f$ and not **the** graph of $f$.

One aspect of this definition is perhaps not immediately intuitive.

> How does the set $S \cap U$ for an open set $U$ containing $p$ correspond to "zooming into" the set $S$ near $p$. Why not replace $U$ with a small open ball instead.

This suggestion is actually equivalent.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Open Ball Definition of Smooth Manifolds at a Point
> Fix $k, n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $p$ be a point in $S$. The set $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p$ if and only if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $S \cap B_\varepsilon(p)$ is a graph of a $C^1$ function $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ where $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ is open.

> [!NOTE] Proof (Sketch)
> The "if" direction is immediate since an open ball is an open set. The "only if" direction requires more work. Assume $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p$. By definition, there exists an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ containing $p$ such that $S \cap U$ is a graph of $C^1$ function $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ where $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ is open. Without loss of generality, you can permute the coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and assume that $S \cap U$ is the graph of $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ so
> 
> $$
> S \cap U = \{(x, f(x)): x \in V\}
> $$
> 
> Since $U$ is open and contains $p$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(p) \subseteq U$. Define
> 
> $$
> V' = \{x \in V: (x, f(x)) \in B_\varepsilon(p)\}
> $$
> 
> and the restriction $g:= f|_{V'}: V' \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ so $g(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in V'$. With some additional effort, you can prove that $V'$ is open and $S \cap B_\varepsilon(p)$ is the graph of $g: V' \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$. $\blacksquare$

This equivalent definition of smooth manifolds with small open balls is helpful for building your geometric intuition.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Consider the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ a three different points $p$ lying in
> 
> $$
> \left\{\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right), (0, 1), (1, 0)\right\}
> $$
> 
> with a ball $B_\varepsilon(p)$ for a small fixed radius $\varepsilon > 0$.
> 
> ![[manifold curve 3.png]]
> 
> The radius $\varepsilon > 0$ is small enough so that $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ is a graph of a $C^1$ function for each $p$.
> 
> At $p = (1/\sqrt{2}, 1/\sqrt{2})$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ is a piece of the upper half of the circle. Hence, for some open interval $I$ with $1/\sqrt{2} \in I$, this is a graph $y \in f(x)$ where $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ is $C^1$ given by $f(x) = \sqrt{1- x^2}$. Also $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ is a piece of the right half of the circle. Hence, for some open interval $I'$ with $1/sqrt{2} \in I'$, this is also a graph $x = g(y)$ where $g: I' \to \mathbb{R}$ is $C^1$ given by $g(y) = \sqrt{1- y^2}$. This shows the choice of graph is not unique.
> 
> At $p = (0, 1)$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ is a piece of the upper half of the circle, so for some open interval $I$ with $0 \in I$, it is a graph $y = \sqrt{1- x^2}$ for $x \in I$. However, no matter how small you choose $\varepsilon$, you can prove that the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ cannot be written as a graph of the form $x = g(y)$.
> 
> At $p = (1, 0)$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ is the graph $x = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$ for $y \in I$ and some open interval $I$. Again, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, this cannot be written as the graph of the form $y = f(x)$.
> 
> Overall, this circle is a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold at each of these three points. You can prove these observations formally but the focus is currently on intuition.

While the open ball definition provides good intuition, it can be difficult to precisely determine the intersection of the set with an open ball. Our original definition is more flexible in a formal proof.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Here is a formal proof that the circle $S = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$ is a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p = (1, 0)$.
> 
> **Proof**. Let $U = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x > 0\}$ so $U$ is open and $p = (1, 0) \in U$. By definition,
> 
> $$
> S \cap U = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 + y^2 = 1, x > 0\}
> $$
> 
> Define the open set $V = (-1, 1) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $f: V \to \mathbb{R}$ by
> 
> $$
> f(y) = \sqrt{1 - y^2} \text{ for }y \in V
> $$
> 
> Note $f$ is $C^1$ on its domain. It follows that for $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> (x, y) \in S \cap U &\iff x^2 + y^2 = 1, x > 0 \\
> &\iff x = \sqrt{1 - y^2} \text{ and }-1 < y < 1 \\
> &\iff x = f(y) \text{ and }y \in V
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Thus, $S \cap U = \{(f(y), y): y \in V\}$ is a graph of the $1$-variable $C^1$ real-value function $f$. By definition, $S$ is a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p = (1, 0)$.

> [!NOTE] Example
> For the cusp from earlier, for any $\varepsilon > 0$, the set $B_\varepsilon(p) \cap S$ cannot be written as a $C^1$ graph of the form $y = f(x)$ because $f'(0)$ cannot exist, and it cannot be written as a $C^1$ graph of the form $x = g(y)$ because it will fail the horizontal line test.
> 
> Formally, let $S = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2: x^2 = y^3\}$ so $S$ is the cusp. Here, you will prove that $S$ is not a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold at the origin $p = (0, 0)$.
> 
> **Proof**. Suppose, for contradiction, that $S$ is a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold at $(0, 0)$. There exists an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ containing $(0, 0)$ such that $S \cap U$ is a graph of a $C^1$ function $f: V \to \mathbb{R}$ where $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is open. This implies that one of the following holds:
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \forall (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2, (x, y) \in S \cap U &\iff y = f(x), x \in V \\
> \forall (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2, (x, y) \in S \cap U &\iff x = f(y), y \in V
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Assume the first statement holds. We claim it suffices to show that there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ for $x \in (-\delta, \delta)$. Assuming the claim, it follows that
> 
> $$
> f'(0) = \lim_{x \to 0} \dfrac{x^{2/3} - 0}{x - 0} = \lim_{x \to 0} x^{-1/3}
> $$
> 
> Does not exist, so $f$ is not $C^1$, which is a contradiction. It remains to prove the claim. Since $U$ is open and contains $(0, 0)$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_{2\varepsilon}((0, 0)) \subseteq U$. Take $\delta = \min{\varepsilon, \varepsilon^{3/2}}$ and let $x \in (\delta, \delta)$. Note that $(x, x^{2/3} \in S$ by definition of $S$. As $|x| < \delta$,
> 
> $$
> ||(x, x^{2/3})|| = \sqrt{x^2 + x^{4/3}} \le \sqrt{\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon^2} < 2\varepsilon
> $$
> 
> in which case $(x, x^{2/3}) \in U$. Then, it follows that $f(x) = x^{2/3}$.
> 
> Assume the second statement holds. Since $U$ is open and contains $(0, 0)$, there exists $0 < \varepsilon < 1$ such that $B_{2 \varepsilon}(0, 0) \subseteq U$. The points $(\pm \varepsilon^3, \varepsilon^2)$ belong to the set by definition and, since $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, you can verify that $||(\pm \varepsilon^3, \varepsilon^2)|| = \sqrt{2\varepsilon^6} \le 2\varepsilon$. This means that the two points $(\pm \varepsilon^3, \varepsilon^2)$ belong to $S \cap U$. It then follows that $f(\varepsilon^2) = \varepsilon^3$ and $f(\varepsilon^2) = -\varepsilon^3$. This contradicts that $f$ is a function. $\blacksquare$

These outcomes show that our definition is effective, since it is precisely identifying the troublesome points.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Smooth Manifolds
> Fix $k, \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $k < n$. A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **($k$-dimensional) smooth manifold** if $S$ is a ($k$-dimensional) smooth manifold at every point in $S$.

> [!TIP] Remark
> A $2$-dimensional smooth manifold $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ is also called a **smooth surface** and a $1$-dimensional smooth manifold $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is also called a **smooth curve**.

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Graphs are Smooth Manifolds
> Fix $k, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $k < n$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a graph of a $C^1$ function $F: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n -k}$ with $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$. The set $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> By permuting coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^n$, you may assume without loss of generality that $S$ in the graph of $S$, so $S = \{(x, F(x)): f \in B\}$ by definition. Then, for all points $p \in S$, we take $U = V \times \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$.
> 
> Then, $U$ is open since it is the Cartesian product of open sets. Also $U$ contains $p$ since $p = (a, f(a)) \in V \times \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ for some $a \in V$.
> 
> Lastly, $S \cap U = S$ so $S \cap U$ is the graph of the $C^1$ function $F: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$. $\blacksquare$

## Tangent Space of a Smooth Manifold

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Tangent Spaces ae Local
> Let $S$ and $S'$ be subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $p \in S \cap S'$. If there exists an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ containing $p$ such that $S \cap U = S' \cap U$, then $T_p S = T_p S'$.

Informally, this theorem says that

> If two sets are locally the same near $p$, then their tangent spaces at $p$ are the same.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> By symmetry, it is sufficient to show that $T_pS = T_pS'$. Fix a tangent vector $v \in T_pS$ so there exists a differentiable $\gamma: I \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ open, $\gamma(I) \subseteq S$, and $\gamma(0) = p$, and $\gamma'(0) = v$. 
> 
> Since $\gamma$ is differentiable and hence continuous at $0$, $I$ is open, $U$ is open and contains $p$, and $\gamma(0) = p$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $I' = (-\delta, \delta) \subseteq I$ and $\gamma((-\delta, \delta)) \subseteq U$.
> 
> Set $g = \gamma\mid_{I'}$ so $g: I' \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is given by $g(t) = \gamma(t)$ for $t \in I'$. We will show $v$ is a tangent vector of the set $S'$ at $p$ using the parametric curve $g$. By definition,
> 
> $$
> g(0) = \gamma(0) = p, g'(0) = \gamma'(0) = v, g(I') = \gamma(I') \subseteq \gamma(I) \subseteq S
> $$
> 
> Also, $g(I') = \gamma(I') \subseteq U$ so overall $g(I') \subseteq S \cap U = S' \cap U \subseteq S'$. Thus, by definition, $v \in T_p S'$. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Corollary
> Fix $k, n \in \mathbb{N}^+$ with $k < n$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a set and let $p$ be a point in $S$. If $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p$, then the tangent space $T_p S$ is a $k$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Since $S$ is a $k$-dimensional smooth manifold at $p$, there exists an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $S \cap $U$ is a graph $S'$ of a $C^1$ function $F: V \to \mathbb{R}^{n-k}$ with $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ open. Since $S' = S' \cap U$, it follows that $S \cap U = S' \cap U$, so $T_pS = T_pS$. Thus, we know that $T_pS'$ is a $k$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. $\blacksquare$

In other words, at every point of a smooth manifold, the tangent space is always a subspace of the same dimension.

But how do you check if a set is a smooth manifold? If the set is a graph, then the answer is immediate by an above Lemma. If the set is written in implicit form, then the example with the circle show that you can directly find the appropriate graph. However, solving for variables in terms of others is difficult at times and impossible at other times.
