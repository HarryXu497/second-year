# Introduction

Recall from Chapter 2 that we can define a general random process as

$$
W \sim P \text{ on }\Omega
$$

that generates sequences of trial outcome $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$ so that for various $\mathbb{R}$-valued functions $g$, $h$, etc on $\Omega$, we have the corresponding $\mathbb{R}$-valued "projections" of $W$: $X = g(W)$, $Y = h(W)$, $\ldots$, each with its own particular corresponding data streams of outputs in $\mathbb{R}$ e.g. $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n, \ldots$.

For each particular stream of outputs, the **law of large numbers** provides a limiting average referred to as the **expected value**

$$
\hat E_n X = \dfrac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n} \to EX
$$

for which, in particular, for each of the various $A \subseteq \Omega$ that we refer to as **events**, we have a **Bernoulli trial** $Z = I_A(W)$ that generates a sequences consisting of $0$ and $1$,

$$
z_1 = I_A(w_1), z_2 = I_A(w_2), \ldots
$$

for whom the **expected value** is called a **probability** (a limiting **relative frequency**).

$$
\begin{align*}
\hat P_n(W \in A) &= \dfrac{z_1 + \cdots + z_n}{n} \\
&= \dfrac{I_A(w_1) + \cdots + I_A(w_n)}{n} \\
&\to EI_A(W) = P(W \in A) = EZ
\end{align*}
$$

We now restrict our attention to the collection $L$ of all $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variables $X$ for which the expected values are **finite**. Then, $E$ is a **linear transformation**/**functional** on $L$. It maps each variable variable to its real expected value.

$$
E: L \overset{\text{linear}}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{R} \text{ by } X \mapsto EX
$$

Applying this linearity to indicator functions yields the elementary property of **finite additivity** for the measure of probability

$$
\begin{align*}
P(A + B) &= EI_{A+B}(W) \\
&= E(I_A(W) + I_B(W)) \\
&= EI_A(W) + EI_B(W) \\
&= P(A) + P(B)
\end{align*}
$$

> [!NOTE] Remark
> We write $A + B$ to mean $A \cup B$ where $A$ and $B$ are disjoint (i.e. $A \cap B = \emptyset$).

We also have that $E$ is **normed** (equipped with some sort of **norm**),

$$
\forall c \in \mathbb{R}, Ec = c
$$

Applying this property to the measure of probability yields 

$$
P(\Omega) = EI_\Omega(W) = E1 = 1
$$

and also 

$$
P(\emptyset) = EI_\emptyset(W) = E0 = 0
$$

Thus, we have, for any event $A \subseteq \Omega$,

$$
0 \le P(A) = EI_A(W) \le 1
$$

We also have the property

$$
X \ge 0 \implies EX \ge 0
$$

Now with this basic knowledge, we now introduce Andrey Kolmogorov's reduction of probability to an axiomatic system.

# The Kolmogorov Axioms

> [!NOTE] Definition: Probability Spaces
> A **probability space/distribution** is a triple of objects $(\Omega, L, E)$ consisting of
> 
> 1. $\Omega$: any set, referred to as the **sample space**.
> 2. $L$: any vector space of $\mathbb{R}$-valued functions on $\Omega$ that contains the constants, and is closed under the taking of absolute values (i.e. $X \in L \implies |X| \in L$), the elements of which are referred to as **random variables**. Recall that a vector space is any set of elements that is non-empty and closed under scalar multiplication and addition.
> 3. $E: L \to \mathbb{R}$: any functional that is
> 
> 	1. **Normed**: $E1 = 1$
> 	2. **Non-negative**: $X \ge 0 \implies EX \ge 0$
> 	3. **Linear**: $E \sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_i = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i EX_i$
> 	4. **Continuous**: $0 \le X_n \uparrow X \implies 0 \le EX_n \uparrow EX$.
> 	
> 	referred to as an **expectation operator**, while its value $EX$ at any $X \in L$ is called the **expected value** of that $X$.

Note that the property of continuity above has not been proven. This property must be assumed.

There is a useful variant of the property of continuity: Given the three other properties exactly as presented, one may verify that it is exactly equivalent to replace the fourth property (continuity) as stated by

$$
n = 1, 2, \ldots, Z_n \ge 0 \implies E \sum_{n=1}^\infty Z_n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty EZ_n
$$

For some this manner of expressing the continuity requirement (as an "extension of linearity") may seem the more natural.

## Reducing the Reduction

As we can view probability as a special case of **expected valuations**, $E$, the above definition of probability spaces is often abstracted into the much reduced form as follows:

> [!NOTE] Definition: Probability Space, Reduced Definition
> A **probability space/distribution** is a triple of objects $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ consisting of
> 
> 1. $\Omega$: any set, called the **sample space**.
> 2. $\mathcal{F}$: any $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$ that is non-empty and closed under the taking of countable unions and complements, the elements of which are referred to as **events**.
> 3. $P: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}$ any set function that is
> 
> 	1. **Normed**. $P(\Omega) = 1$
> 	2. **Non-negative**: $A \in \mathcal{F} \implies P(A) \ge 0$
> 	3. **$\sigma$-additive**: $P(\sum_{i=1}^\infty A_i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i)$. Recall that the use of $\sum$ over sets imply that $A_i$ are mutually disjoint
> 	
> 	referred to as a **probability measure** while its value $P(A)$ at any $A \in \mathcal{F}$ is called the **probability** of that $A$.

From this reduced formulation, we have the barebones structure to prove many properties and propositions about probability.

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Nullity
> For a probability space, we have $P(\emptyset) = 0$
> 
> **Proof**. Define the sequence of mutually disjoint events
> 
> $$
> A_1 = \Omega \quad \text{and} \quad A_n = \emptyset, n \ge 2
> $$
> 
> Then we have $\Omega = \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ and thus obtain
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(\Omega) = P(\sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n) &\implies 1 = \sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n) \\
> &\implies 1 = 1 + P(\emptyset) \\
> &\implies P(\emptyset) = 0
> \end{align*}
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Finite-Additivity
> $$
> P(A + B) = P(A) + P(B)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Complementarity
> $$
> P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Negative Additivity
> $$
> P(A - B) = P(A) - P(AB)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Monotonicity
> $$
> A \subseteq B \implies P(A) \le P(B)
> $$
