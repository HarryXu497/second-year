# Introduction

For any fixed sample size $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the output data $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ from any real-valued random variable $X$ can be thought of as the $n$ components of a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Now that $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we can impose a geometric meaning onto the output of a random variable.

# Geometry of $\mathbb{R}^n$

On $\mathbb{R}^n$ we define three standard algebraic/geometric operations.

1. **Inner product**: $x \cdot y = x ' y = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$
2. **Length/norm**: $|x| = \sqrt{x \cdot x}$ 
3. **Distance**: $d(x, y) = |y - x|$

## Orthogonality

Two non-zero vectors $\boldsymbol x$ and $\boldsymbol y$ are mutually **orthogonal** when

$$
x \perp y \iff |y - x| = |y + x|
$$

This can be derived using congruent triangles

![[congruent.png]]

Squaring both sides of $|y - x| = |y + x|$ and expanding yields

$$
\begin{align*}
|y - x| = |y + x| &\iff |y-x|^2 = |y+x|^2 \\
 &\iff (|y|^2 - 2x \cdot y + |y^2|) = (|y|^2 + 2x \cdot y + |y^2|) \\
&\iff - 2x \cdot y = 2x \cdot y \\
&\iff x \cdot y = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, we have the equivalent condition for orthogonality:

$$
x \perp y \iff  x \cdot y = 0
$$

The **orthogonal projection** $\hat y$ of $y$ on any non-zero $x$ is defined by the following requirements:

1. The projection $\hat y$ must be a scalar multiple of $x$. **That is, $\hat y = tx$ for some $t \in \mathbb{R}$.**
2. The residual vector $y - \hat y$ must be at a right angle to $x$. **That is, $y - \hat y \perp x$.**

As long as $x \ne 0$, these requirements defined a unique solution for the scalar $t$ and thus for the vector $\hat y$ as well. An explicit formula for the scalar $t$ is derived as

$$
x \cdot (y - tx) = 0 \implies t = \dfrac{x \cdot y}{|x|^2}
$$

Thus, we have

$$
\hat y =\mathrm{op}(y \mid x) = \dfrac{x \cdot y}{|x|^2} x
$$

## Angles

In general, two non-zero vectors $x$ and $y$ may be said to describe an **angle** $\theta = \theta(x, y)$ with a unique numerical value $0 \le \theta \le \pi$. This is because any two such vectors span a unique two-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that intersects the **unit sphere** in a unique **unit circle**. By definition, $\theta$ is then the unique value of the **shortest arc length** along that unit circle between the two unit vectors $u = x/|x|$ and $v = y/|y|$.

We say that the angle $\theta$ is either acute, perpendicular, or obtuse based on if the sign of the scalar $t$ for the **orthogonal projection** is respectively $+1$, $0$, or $-1$. The sign of this scalar is determined solely to the dot product $x \cdot y$.

Therefore, whether the sign of the dot product $x \cdot y$  is $+1$, $0$, or $-1$ determines if the angle is acute, perpendicular, or obtuse respectively.

Now, we define cosine as the ratio "adjacent"/"hypotenuse" i.e.

$$
\cos \theta = \mathrm{sign}(x \cdot y) \dfrac{|\hat y|}{|y|}
$$

Substituting $\hat y = tx$ yields

$$
\cos \theta = \mathrm{sign}(x \cdot y) \dfrac{1}{|y|}\left |\dfrac{x \cdot y}{|x|^2} x\right| = \mathrm{sign}(x \cdot y) \dfrac{1}{|y|}\dfrac{|x \cdot y|}{|x|^2} |x|
= \dfrac{x \cdot y}{|x||y|}
$$

The Pythagorean theorem is trivial from here

$$
|y|^2 = |\hat y + (y - \hat y)|^2 = |\hat y|^2 + |y - \hat y|^2
$$

and it provides three variations on the **Cauchy-Schwartz** inequality:

1. **CS1**. $|\hat y| \le |y|$ with equality if and only if $y = \hat y$.
2. **CS2**. $|x \cdot y| \le |x||y|$ with equality if and only if $y = tx$ for a unique $t \in \mathbb{R}$. This can be derived by substituting the expression for $\hat y$ into the above inequality.
3. **CS3**. $|\cos(\theta (x, y))| \le 1$ with equality if and only if $y = tx$ for a unique $t \in \mathbb{R}$. This can be derived by applying the above inequality to the definition of $\cos \theta$.

We can derive the **triangle inequality**

$$
|x + y| \le |x| + |y| \text{ with equality iff } y = tx \text{ for unique }t > 0
$$

by squaring the left hand side of the inequality and substituting **CS2**. Note that for equality, the dot product $x \cdot y$ must be positive.

# Geometry of Random Variables

Suppose you have two real-valued random processes $X$ and $Y$ who, for a certain sample size $n$ generate data $x$ and $y$. Let $n$ be sufficiently large enough so that on the basis of the **law of large numbers** (**LLN**), we are guaranteed that the following limiting averages have been achieved.

In that case, we can express the basic constructs of Euclidean geometry through random variables:

1. **Inner product**

$$
\dfrac{x \cdot y}{n} = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i}{n} \overset{LLN}{=} EXY
$$

2. **Length (norm)**

$$
\dfrac{|x|}{\sqrt n} = \sqrt{\dfrac{x \cdot x}{n}} \overset{LLN}{=} \sqrt{EX^2}
$$

3. **Distance (metric)**

$$
\dfrac{d(x, y)}{\sqrt n} = \dfrac{|y - x|}{\sqrt n} \overset{LLN}{=} \sqrt{E(Y - X)^2}
$$

We define $L$ as the vector space of $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variables $X$, $Y$, etc where the expectation operator $E$ may be applied to produce the real numbers $EX$, $EY$, etc, Recall that $E$ is **normed**, **non-negative**, **linear**, and **continuous**. 

We consider the following particular subspace of $L$

$$
L_2 = \{X \in L \mid EX^2 < \infty\}
$$

whose members have finite second moments $EX^2$ and are thus guaranteed to have **finite mean and variance**.

> [!TIP] Remark
> The $L_2$ space is a Hilbert space and is thus equipped with an inner product. Other spaces like $L$ do not have a dot product.

> [!TIP] Remark
> The vector space $L$ does not have a defined norm; a length cannot be defined for a random variable using expectations.  
> 
> There also exists $L_1$, the vector subspace of $L$ of random variables with finite first moments $EX$. These have defined norms via expectations but do not have a defined inner product.

In $L_2$, we simply define

1. **Inner product**

$$
\langle X, Y \rangle = EXY
$$

2. **Length (norm)**

$$
||X|| = \sqrt{\langle X, X \rangle} = \sqrt{EX^2}
$$

3. **Distance (metric)**

$$
d(X, Y) = ||Y - X|| = \sqrt{E(Y - X)^2}
$$

We verify that $L_2$ is indeed a vector subspace of $L$

> [!NOTE] Lemma: $X, Y \in L_2 \implies X, Y, XY \in L$
> 
> **Proof**. We have
> 
> $$
> |XY| \le \dfrac{X^2 + Y^2}{2}
> $$
> 
> and letting $Y = 1$ without loss of generality, we have
> 
> $$
> |X| \le \dfrac{X^2 + 1}{2}
> $$

> [!NOTE] Corollary: $X, Y \in L_2 \implies X + Y \in L_2$
> **Proof**. We have
> 
> $$
> (X + Y)^2 \le 2(X^2 + Y^2)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Lemma: Verifying the Inner Product
> We need to ensure that $\langle X, Y \rangle = EXY$ satisfies the three abstract properties of being an inner product.
> 
> **Bilinear**.
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \langle a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2, Y\rangle &= E(a_1X_1 + a_2X_2) Y \\
> &= a_1 \langle X_1, Y \rangle + a_2 \langle X_2, Y \rangle
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> and likewise for $Y$
> 
> **Symmetric.**
> 
> $$
> \langle X, Y \rangle = EXY = EYX = \langle Y, X \rangle
> $$
> 
> **Positive (semi)-definite**.
> 
> $$
> \langle X, X \rangle = EX^2 \ge 0 \text{ with equality iff } X = 0
> $$

All but the **claim of equality** in the last property are relatively straightforward in their verification. So for the sake of completeness, we provide two additional results that will fully explain the matter. 

## Markov and Chebyshev

Take any non-negative random variable $Z \ge 0$, any non-negative number $t \ge 0$, and any non-decreasing function $g: [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)$. Then, we have

$$
g(t) I(Z \ge t) \le g(Z)
$$

by casework. If $Z \le t$, then $g(Z) \le g(t)$ since $g$ is non-decreasing. If $Z > t$, then the left hand side is $0$ and the right hand side is non-negative.

Applying $E$ to both sides yields

$$
g(t) P(Z \ge t) = Eg(Z)
$$

which gives us **Markov's Inequality**:

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Markov's Inequality
> For any $Z \ge 0$, $t \ge 0$ and non-decreasing $g: [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)$, we have
> 
> $$
> \forall t \ge 0 \text{ s.t. } g(t) > 0, P(Z \ge t) \le \dfrac{Eg(Z)}{g(t)}
> $$

There are some immediate corollaries. Consider an arbitrary random variable $X$ in $L_2$ with $\mu = EX$ and $\sigma^2 = E(X - \mu)^2$, and then let $Z = |X - \mu|$ and $g(t) = t^2$ to obtain

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Chebyshev I
> For any $X \in L_2$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$, we have
> 
> $$
> P(|X - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}
> $$

or take $Z = |X - \mu|/\sigma$ and again $g(t) = t^2$ to find

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Chebyshev II
> For any $X \in L_2$ and any $k > 0$, we have
> 
> $$
> P\left(\dfrac{|X - \mu|}{\sigma} \ge k\right) \le \dfrac{1}{k^2}
> $$

But the most important corollary is as follows.

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Markov's Equality
> 
> We have
> 
> $$
> E|X| = 0 \iff X = 0
> $$
> 
> **Proof**. ($\implies$) We have the following from Markov's Inequality
> 
> $$
> \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(|X| \ge 1/n) \le \dfrac{E|X|}{1/n} = 0 
> $$
> 
> which implies
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(|X| \ge 1/n) \le \dfrac{E|X|}{1/n} = 0 &\implies \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(|X| < 1/n) = 1 \\
> &\implies P(|X| = 0) = 1  \tag{Continuity}\\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> ( $\Longleftarrow$ ) We have
> 
> $$
> X = 0 \implies |X| = 0 \implies E|X| = 0
> $$

This equality has an immediate application to the semi-definiteness of the inner product.

$$
\langle X, Y\rangle = EXY
$$

We can now see that

$$
\begin{align*}
&||X||^2 = \langle X, X \rangle = EX^2 = 0 \\
\iff & X^2 = 0 \\
\iff & X = 0
\end{align*}
$$

# The Geometry of $L_2$

In $L_2$, just as in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we may characterize orthogonality as

$$
X \perp Y \iff ||Y - X|| = ||Y + X|| \iff \langle X, Y \rangle = EXY = 0
$$

We again define the orthogonal projection $\hat Y$ of a random variable $Y$ on another variable $X$.

$$
\exists t \in \mathbb{R} \text{ s.t. } \hat Y = tX \quad \text{and} \quad Y - \hat Y \perp X
$$

and again this provides a unique solution for $t$

$$
\begin{align*}
Y - \hat Y \perp X &\implies E(Y - \hat Y)(X) = 0 \\
&\implies E(Y - tX)(X) = 0 \\
&\implies E(XY - tX^2) = 0 \\
&\implies EXY - tEX^2 = 0 \\
&\implies t = \dfrac{EXY}{EX^2}
\end{align*}
$$

provided $EX^2 > 0$. So 

$$
\hat Y = \dfrac{EXY}{EX^2} X
$$

We define the angle $\theta(X, Y)$ between two non-zero random variables using the cosine. We define the cosine as the "adjacent" over the "hypotenuse".

$$
\begin{align*}
\cos \theta(X, Y) &= \mathrm{sign}(EXY) \dfrac{||\hat Y||}{||Y||} \\
&= \dfrac{EXY}{|EXY|} \dfrac{\sqrt{t^2EX^2}}{\sqrt{EY^2}} \\
&= \dfrac{EXY}{|EXY|} \left|\dfrac{EXY}{EX^2} \right|\dfrac{\sqrt{EX^2}}{\sqrt{EY^2}} \\
&= \dfrac{EXY}{|EXY|} \dfrac{|EXY|}{EX^2} \dfrac{\sqrt{EX^2}}{\sqrt{EY^2}} \\
&= \dfrac{EXY}{\sqrt{EX^2EY^2}} \\
\end{align*}
$$

The Pythagorean Theorem follows

$$
||Y||^2 = ||\hat Y  + (Y- \hat Y)||^2 = ||\hat Y||^2 + ||Y - \hat Y||^2
$$

and thus we obtain the same three variations on Cauchy-Schwarz:

1. **CS1**. $||\hat Y|| \le ||Y||$ with equality if and only if $Y = \hat Y$.
2. **CS2**. $(EXY)^2 \le EX^2 EY^2$ with equality if and only if $Y = tX$ for a unique $t \in \mathbb{R}$. This can be derived by substituting the expression for $\hat Y$ into the above inequality.
3. **CS3**. $|\cos\theta(X, Y)| \le 1$ with equality if and only if $Y = tX$ for a unique $t \in \mathbb{R}$. This can be derived by applying the above inequality to the definition of $\cos \theta$.

And the triangle inequality follows as well.

$$
||X + Y|| \le ||X|| + ||Y|| 
$$

with equality if and only if $Y = tX$ for a unique $t > 0$.

# The Descriptive Geometry of Data

## Means, Standard Deviations, and Variances

In $L_2$, we measure the distance between any two variables $X$ and $Y$ as

$$
d(X, Y) = ||X - Y|| = \sqrt{E(X - Y)^2}
$$

In particular, we can measure how distant any given $X$ from any specified constant $t \in \mathbb{R}$ as

$$
d(X, t) = ||X - t|| = \sqrt{E(X - t)^2}
$$

We want to find the value of $t$ that minimizes the quantity $d(X, t)$. By completing the quadratic, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
f(t) &= d(X, t)^2 = E(X - t)^2 \\
&= t^2 - 2tEX + EX^2 \\
&= (t-EX)^2 + EX^2  - (EX)^2
\end{align*}
$$

and we see that $d(X, t)$ is minimized exactly at the **mean value** of $X$: $t = \mu = EX$.

The minimum distance itself (between $X$ and its closest constant $EX$) is referred to as the **standard deviation** of $X$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Standard Deviation
> For a random variable $X \in L_2$, we define the **standard deviation of $X$** as the distance between $X$ and $EX$:
> 
> $$
> \sigma = \sigma(X) = d(X, EX) = \sqrt{E(X - EX)^2}
> $$

The standard deviation measures the amount of **variability** in that random variable. 

There are three standard properties of random variables:

> [!NOTE] Property 1: Invariance under Relocation
> For a constant $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a random variable $X \in L_2$, we have
> 
> $$
> \sigma(a + X) = \sigma(X)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Property 2: Scaling
> For a constant scale factor $b \in \mathbb{R}$ and a random variable $X \in L_2$, we have
> 
> $$
> \sigma(b X) = |b|\sigma(X)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Property 3: Zero Variability
> From Markov's Equality, we have that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \sigma(X) = 0 &\iff E(X - EX)^2 = 0  \\
> &\iff X = EX
> \end{align*}
> $$

We define **centred** versions of these random variables.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Centered Random Variables
> Let $X \in L_2$ be a random variable. Then, we define
> 
> $$
> \dot X = X - EX
> $$
> 
> as **centered $X$**. Then, we trivially have $E\dot X = 0$.

Thus, the standard deviation of any random variable $X$ is simply the length of the centered $X$:

$$
\sigma(X) = \sqrt{E(X - EX)^2} = \sqrt{E\dot X^2} = ||\dot X|| 
$$

Also note that, since centering is a relocation,

$$
\sigma(\dot X) = \sigma(X)
$$

> [!NOTE] Definition: Variance
> 
> The **variance** is of a random variable $X \in L_2$ is defined as
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{Var}(X) = [\sigma(X)]^2 = E(X - EX)^2 = EX^2 - (EX)^2
> $$
> 
> This is defined purely for convenience so we can avoid the square root of standard deviations.

Note that, by non-negativity and Markov's Equality,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) \ge 0 \text{ with equality iff } X = EX
$$

## Covariance and Correlation

Consider the variability in the **sum** of two random variable $X + Y$. We consider the variability in the sum compared to the variability in either one of them separately.

This variability in the sum depends on how much the two are related. If $Y = -X$, then $X + Y = 0$ and $\sigma(X + Y) = \sigma(0) = 0$. On the other hand, if $Y = X$, then $X + Y = 2X$ and so the variability in the sum is $\sigma(X + Y) = \sigma(2X) = 2\sigma(X)$.

More generally, the **triangle inequality** applied to the centered variables gives

$$
\sigma(X + Y) = ||\dot X + \dot Y|| \le ||\dot X|| + ||\dot Y|| = \sigma(X) + \sigma(Y)
$$

with equality if and only if $Y = a + bX$ for unique $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b > 0$.

The exact standard deviation of the sum can be computed explicitly. This is easy when working with the variance of the sum:

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) &= \sigma(X + Y)^2 \\
&= ||\dot X + \dot Y||^2 \\
&= ||\dot X||^2 + 2E\dot X \dot Y + ||\dot Y||^2 \\
&= \sigma(X)^2 + E\dot X \dot Y + \sigma(Y)^2 \\
&= \mathrm{Var}(X) + 2E(X - EX)(Y - EY) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)
\end{align*}
$$

We see that variance is not a linear operator; it is a quadratic operator. The term $E(X - EX)(Y - EY)$ that arises above is referred to as the **covariance** of $X$ and $Y$. 

> [!NOTE] Definition: Covariance
> Let $X, Y \in L_2$. We define the covariance of $X$ and $Y$ as the inner product of the centered random variables $\dot X$ and $\dot Y$:
> 
> $$
> \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = E\dot X \dot Y = E(X - EX)(Y - EY)
> $$

Then, we define the **correlation/correlation coefficient** of $X$ and $Y$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Correlation
> Let $X, Y \in L_2$. We define the **correlation/correlation coefficient** of $X$ and $Y$ as the cosine of the angle between centered $X$ and $Y$.
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \rho(X, Y) &= \cos \theta(\dot X, \dot Y) \\
> &= \dfrac{E\dot X \dot Y}{\sqrt{E\dot X^2 E\dot Y^2}} \\
> &= \dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X) \mathrm{Var}(Y)}} \\
> &= \dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma(X) \sigma(Y)}
> \end{align*}
> $$

Then, via Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

$$
|\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)| \le \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(Y)}
$$

or equivalently

$$
|\rho(X, Y)| \le 1 
$$

with equality if and only if $Y = a + bX$ for $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b > 0$.

Also, for $\beta \ne 0$,

$$
\rho(\alpha + \beta X, Y) = (\beta / |\beta|) \rho(X, Y) = \mathrm{sgn}(\beta) \rho(X, Y)
$$

# The Simple Linear Model

> [!NOTE] Proposition: The Simple Linear Model
> Given any two $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variables $X, Y$ in $L_2$, there exists unique scalars $\alpha, \beta$ and a unique random variable $Z$ such that
> 
> $$
> Y = \alpha + \beta X + Z \text{ with } EZ = 0 = \rho(Z, X)
> $$
> 
> Note that $\rho(Z, X) = \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)/\sigma(X)\sigma(Y) = 0$ so $X$ and $Z$ are orthogonal.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> **We start by deriving values for $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $Z$.** 
> 
> For every pair of constants $s$ and $t$, we may construct the random variable $Z = Y - s - tX$ such that, we have
> 
> $$
> Y = s + tX + Z
> $$
> 
> The "difference variable" $Z$ depends on both $s$ and $t$ and may be noted as $Z = Z_{st}$. We want to find the specific values $s = \alpha$ and $t = \beta$ for which the conditions hold.
> 
> Thus, assuming the conditions $EZ = 0 = \rho(Z, X)$ hold, we have the following system
> 
> $$
> \begin{cases}
> EY &= \alpha + \beta EX \\
> \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) &= \beta \mathrm{Var}(X)
> \end{cases}
> $$
> 
> since
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> EY &= E(\alpha + \beta X + Z) \\
> &= \alpha + \beta EX + EZ \\
> &= \alpha + \beta EX \tag{$EZ = 0$} \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> and
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) &= E(X - EX)(\alpha + \beta X + Z - E(\alpha + \beta X + Z)) \\
> &= E(X - EX)(\beta X + Z - \beta EX - EZ) \\
> &= E(X - EX)(\beta X + Z - \beta EX)  \tag{$EZ = 0$}\\
> &= E[(X - EX)(\beta X - \beta EX) + Z(X - EX)] \\
> &= \beta E(X - EX)^2 + EZ(X - EX) \\
> &= \beta E(X - EX)^2 + E(Z - EZ)(X - EX) \tag{$EZ = 0$}\\
> &= \beta \mathrm{Var}(X) + \rho(Z, X) \\
> &= \beta \mathrm{Var}(X) \tag{$\rho(Z, X) = 0$} \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> which, assuming $X$ is not a constant and thus $\mathrm{Var}(X) > 0$, supplies the required solution for $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $Z$:
> 
> $$
> \alpha = EY - \beta EX, \quad \beta = \dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(X)}, Z = Y - \alpha - \beta X
> $$
> 
> Then, **for the proof**, the unique solutions $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $Z$ satisfies
> 
> $$
> Y = \alpha + \beta X + Z \text{ with } EZ = 0 = \rho(Z, X)
> $$
> 
> as required.

We call the variable $Z = Y - \alpha - \beta X$ is called an **error** or **residual variable**. It is the difference between $Y$ and the linear expression $\alpha + \beta X$ in $X$ that is closes to $Y$.

![[residual.png]]

> [!NOTE] Corollary
> We have,  for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$,
> 
> $$
> ||Y - \alpha -  \beta X|| \le ||Y - s - tX||
> $$
> 
> with equality if and only if $s = \alpha$ and $t = \beta$.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> ||Y - s - tX||^2 &= ||T - \alpha - \beta X + (a - s) + (\beta - t)X||^2 \\
> &= ||Y - \alpha - \beta X||^2 + ||(a - s) + (\beta - t)X||^2
> \end{align*}
> $$

We also have the converse to this corollary because we can start from the assumption that, $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}$

$$
||Y - \alpha -  \beta X|| \le ||Y - s - tX||
$$

Then, by squaring both sides and expanding the right hand side around $a + \beta X$, and then shifting things around a bit, we obtain, $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$
-2E(Y - \alpha - \beta X)((a - s) + (\beta - t)X) \le ||(a - s) + (\beta - t)X||^2
$$

Substituting $a - s$ by $\pm s$, $\beta - t$ by $\pm t$, and $Y - \alpha - \beta X$ by $Z$, we get, $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$
\pm 2E(s + tX)Z \le ||s + tX||^2
$$

Replace $s$ by $2s/n$ and $t$ by $2t/n$ to get, $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$,

$$
|E(s + tX)Z| \le ||s + tX||^2/n
$$

By taking the limit as $n \to 0$, we conclude that the left hand side must be zero for every value of $s$ and $t$ i.e. $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$
E(s + tX) Z = 0
$$

Substituting $(s, t) = (1, 0)$ and $(s, t) = (0, 1)$, we arrive back at the conditions we started with:

$$
EZ = 0 = EZX = \mathrm{Cov}(Z, X) = \rho(Z, X)
$$

Now, we want to understand how the correlation $\rho(X, Y)$ measure the degree of linear dependence between $X$ and $Y$.

## The Geometry of Correlation

From the simple linear model,

$$
Y = \alpha + \beta X + Z \quad \text{with} \quad EZ= 0 = \rho(Z, X)
$$

the correlation between $Y$ and its closest linear expression $\alpha + \beta X$ is derived by using the orthogonality conditions.

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha + \beta X, Y) = \mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X) \ge 0
$$

Then, letting $v = EY = E(\alpha + \beta X)$, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
|\rho(X, Y)| &= \rho(\alpha + \beta X, Y) \\
&= \dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha + \beta X, Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X)}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}} \\
&= \dfrac{\mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X)}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}}\\
&= \dfrac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}}\\
&= \dfrac{\sigma(\alpha + \beta X)}{\sigma(Y)}\\
&= \dfrac{||\alpha + \beta X - v||}{||Y - v||}
\end{align*}
$$

We can see that the proportion of variability in $Y$ that is attributable to its linear dependence on $X$.

On the same orthogonality conditions, we adduce two fundamental quadratic expressions.

$$
\begin{align*}
||Y||^2 &= ||\alpha + \beta X||^2 + ||Z||^2 \\
\mathrm{Var}(Y) &= \mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X) + \mathrm{Var}(Z)
\end{align*}
$$

We can reduce the second one as 

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Var}(Y) &= \mathrm{Var}(\alpha + \beta X) + \mathrm{Var}(Z) \\
&= \beta^2 \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Z) \\
&=\rho(X, Y)^2 \mathrm{Var}(Y) + \mathrm{Var}(Z)
\end{align*}
$$

to show that

$$
||Z||^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Z) = (1 - \rho(X, Y)^2) \mathrm{Var}(Y)
$$

Taking the square root, we see that the expression gives the **relative** distance between $Y$ and its closest linear expression in $X$, $\alpha + \beta X$ compared to $Y$ and its closest constant $v = EY$.

$$
\dfrac{||Y - \alpha - \beta X||}{||Y - v ||} = \dfrac{\sigma(Z)}{\sigma(Y)} = \sqrt{1 - \rho(X, Y)^2}
$$

You can think of this intuitively as comparing the best linear guess to the best constant guess. If $X$ and $Y$ are perfectly correlated, then the ratio $\sigma(Z)/\sigma(Y)$ is one and the guesses are equally good. If $X$ and $Y$ are not linearly correlated at all, then the ratio is zero and the linear guess is not an improvement over the constant one: the best linear guess is the constant one.
