# Conditional Probability

$P(A \mid B)$ is the notation device commonly use to symbolize the simple frequency of an event $A$, when we strictly limit ourselves to the $B$ part of the sample space. $B$ is then thought of as the background **necessary condition** against which everything else takes place.

Thus, for a sample of the first $n$ outcomes $w_1, \ldots, w_n$ of some random variable $W$, the **conditional empirical relative frequency** of $A$ for all the $w_i$'s that pass the $B$ condition (i.e. along only the $w_i$'s for which $B$ occurs) is

$$
\begin{align*}
\hat P_n(W \in A \mid W \in B) &= \dfrac{I_A(w_1)I_B(w_1) + \cdots I_A(w_n)I_B(w_n)}{I_B(w_1) \cdots + I_B(w_n)} \\
&= \dfrac{I_{AB}(w_1) + \cdots + I_{AB}(w_n)}{I_B(w_1) + \cdots + I_B(w_n)} \\
&= \dfrac{n \hat P_n(W \in AB)}{n\hat P(W \in B)} \\
&= \dfrac{\hat P_n(W \in AB)}{\hat P_n(W \in B)} \\
\end{align*}
$$

provided the denominator is not $0$.

This gives the basis for the following definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Conditional Probability
> For any two events $A$ and $B$ and a random variable $W$ defined on a sample space $\Omega$, the **conditional probability of $A$ given $B$** is
> 
> $$
> P(W \in A \mid W \in B) \overset{defn}{=} \dfrac{P(W \in AB)}{P(W \in B)}
> $$
> 
> provided $P(W \in B) \ne 0$.

We can abbreviate this as

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(AB) / P(B)
$$

provided $P(B) \ne 0$.

Also note that

$$
P(AB) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A) P(A)
$$

provided $P(A)P(B) > 0$.

# Joint Distributions and Statistic Independence

Consider the circumstance wherein some given variable is an ordered pair i.e. $W = (X, Y)$. $W$ is then said to be in a **bivariate/joint distribution** with **coordinates/components** $X$ and $Y$.

Our discussions here do not require $X$ and $Y$ to be necessary real-valued here.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be distribution on their own individual coordinate sample space, say $X$ on $\mathcal{X}$ and $Y$ on $\mathcal{Y}$. Then, $W$ is distributed by the **joint distribution** of the ordered pair, which is found on the **product space** $\Omega = \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$.

The individual distributions are referred to as **marginal distributions** which form the **joint distribution**.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Marginal Distributions
> Given that $W = (X, Y) \sim P$ on $\Omega = \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$, we automatically have **marginal distributions** for each of $X$ and $Y$:
> 
> $X \sim P_X$ on $\mathcal{X}$ with
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P_X(A) &= P(X \in A) \\
> &= EI_A(X) \\
> &= EI_A(X) I_{\mathcal{Y}}(Y) \\
> &= EI_{A \times \mathcal{Y}}(X, Y) \\
> &= P((X, Y) \in A \times \mathcal{Y})
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> for any subset $A \subseteq \mathcal{X}$,
> 
> Similarly, we have $Y \sim P_Y$ on $\mathcal{Y}$ with $P_Y(B) = P((X, Y) \in \mathcal{X} \times B)$ for any subset $B \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$.

We want to investigate the extent to which either of the component variables influence the other.

In general, it will not be surprising to find that conditional probabilities will almost always different from the corresponding unconditional ones. In other words, it is rare for one random variable $Y$ to have no statistical-conditional influence on $X$.

When the variable $Y$ has no stochastic influence on the variable $X$, we say that **$X$ is statistically independent of $Y$**. Formally, this occurs if and only if for every event $B$ for $Y$ for which $P(Y \in B) \ne 0$, the **conditional probabilities/frequencies** for $X$ given $Y \in B$ is the same as its original marginal probabilities/frequencies.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Statistical Independence
> $X$ is said to be statistically independent of $Y$, denoted $X \perp Y$ if and only if, $\forall A \subseteq \mathcal{X}, B \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ with $P(Y \in B) \ne 0$,
> 
> $$
> P(X \in A \mid Y \in B) = P(X \in A) 
> $$

Immediately and equivalently to this definition, we acquire the multiplicative criterion/characterization of independence

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Statistical Independence (Multiplicative Criterion)
> We have
> 
> $$
> X \perp Y \iff \forall A \subseteq \mathcal{X}, B \subseteq \mathcal{Y}, P(X \in A, Y \in B) = P(X \in A) P(Y \in B) 
> $$
> 
> From here, we can see that statistic independence is symmetric:
> 
> $$
> X \perp Y \iff Y \perp X
> $$
> 
> We can also see that, if $P(Y \in B) \ne 0$ and $P(Y \in B^C) \ne 0$,
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> &P(X \in A \mid Y \in B) = P(X \in A) \\
> \iff& P(X \in A \mid Y \in B^c) = P(X \in A) \\
> \iff& P(X \in A^c \mid Y \in B) = P(X \in A^c)\\
> \iff& P(X \in A^c \mid Y \in B^c) = P(X \in A^c)\\
> \end{align*}
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Statistical Independence (Atomic Characterization)
> We have
> 
> $$
> X \perp Y \iff \forall A \subseteq \mathcal{X}, B \subseteq \mathcal{Y}, I_A(X) \perp I_B(Y)
> $$

# Binomial Distributions

Consider a random variable $S_n$ that counts exactly the number of successes in a fixed number $n$ of **independent and identically distributed Bernoulli trials**: $Z_i, i = 1, \ldots, n$ IID $Z \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$.

> [!NOTE] Review: Bernoulli Distributions
> Recall that $\mathrm{Bern}(p)$ for $0 \le p \le 1$ is a discrete distribution with the following finite scheme distribution
> 
> $$
> Z \sim \mathrm{Bern(0)} \iff Z \sim \begin{pmatrix}
> 0 & 1 \\
> 1 - p & p
> \end{pmatrix} \iff Z \sim \begin{pmatrix}
> 0 & 1 \\
> q & p
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
> 
> where $q = 1 - p$. For an event $A$, we can equivalently define the Bernoulli trial for the event as simply the indicator function
> 
> $$
> I_A = \begin{pmatrix}
> 0 & 1 \\
> 1 - P(A) & P(A)
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
> 
> By computations, we can see that
> 
> 1. For all $n > 0$, $EZ^n = EZ$
> 2. $EZ = p$
> 3. $\mathrm{Var}(Z) = p^2 - p = pq$

It's easy to see that $S_n = Z_1 + \cdots + Z_n$ yields the number of $1$s that will occur for any realization of the vector $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_N)$ made of those Bernoulli samples.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Binomial Distribution
> The random variable $X$ is said to have a **binomial distribution** on $n$ trials with probability of success per trial $p$, denoted $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}, 0 \le p \le 1$, if and only if
> 
> $$
> X \overset{d}{=} S_n = Z_1 + \cdots + Z_n
> $$
> 
> with $Z_1, \ldots, Z_n$ IID $Z \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$.

It is immediate that

$$
EX \overset{d}{=} ES_n = E(Z_1 + \cdots + Z_n) = EZ_1 + \cdots + EZ_n = nEZ = np
$$

So the long-run average number of successes in $n$ trials is the average number of successes per trial $p$ times the number of trials $n$.

Also, since covariance is bilinear,

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{Var}(X) &= \mathrm{Cov}(X, X) \\
&= \mathrm{Cov}\left(\sum_{i}^n Z_i, \sum_{j}^n Z_j\right) \\ 
&= \sum_{i}^n \sum_{j}^n \mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_i, Z_j\right) \\ 
&= \sum_{i}^n\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_i, Z_i\right) +  \sum_{0 \le 1 \ne  j \le n}^n \mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_i, Z_j\right) \\ 
&= \sum_{i}^n\mathrm{Var}\left(Z_i\right) + \sum_{0 \le i \ne j \le n}^n \mathrm{Cov}\left(Z_i, Z_j\right) \\ 
&= \sum_{i}^n\mathrm{Var}\left(Z_i\right) \\ 
&= n \mathrm{Var}(Z) \\ 
&= npq \\ 
\end{align*}
$$

We also have the following intuitive result.

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Sums of Independent Binomials
> Let $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m, p)$, $Y \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$, and $X \perp Y$. Then,
> 
> $$
> X + Y \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m + n, p)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Take any infinite sample $Z_i, i \in \mathbb{N}$ IID $Z \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$ so that
> 
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}
> X \\
> Y
> \end{pmatrix} \overset{d}{=} \begin{pmatrix}
> Z_1 + \cdots + Z_m \\
> Z_{m+1} + \cdots + Z_{m + n}
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
> 
> whence $X + Y \overset{d}{=} Z_1 + \cdots + Z_{m + n}$.

We can extend this via induction to the sum of any number of independent binomials.

> [!NOTE] Corollary
> Assume $X_i \overset{\text{indep}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bin}(n_i, p)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, k$. Then
> 
> $$
> \sum_{i = 1}^k X_i \sim \mathrm{Bin}\left(\sum_{i = 1}^k n_i, p\right)
> $$

Generally, we don't really need a probability mass function for a binomial distribution. Regardless, we can compute it.

Any single outcome of the $n$-vectors of $Z$-trials will necessarily be one of the $2_6n$ potentially possible sequences of $0$'s and $1$'s that occur in $\mathbb{R}^n$. So the effective **sample space** for the random vector $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)$ is the $2^n$ corners

$$
0 \times 1 = \{0, 1\}^n = 2^n
$$

of the unit cube $[0, 1] = [0, 1]^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

Since the trials are independent, the frequency that any particular corner occurs is the simple product of the proportions of time that each of its coordinates occur individually:

$$
\forall z \in 0 \times 1, P(Z = z) = \prod_{i = 1}^n P(Z_i = z_i)
$$

Individually, we may write that

$$
\forall i = 1, \ldots, n, z_i \in \{0, 1\}, P(Z_i = z_i) = p^{z_i}q^{1 - z_1}
$$

to give the full expression to the probability mass function of the vector variable $Z$ over all $2^n$ elements of its domain

$$
\forall z \in 0 \times 1, P(Z = z) = p^{\sum z_i}q^{n - \sum z_i} 
$$

And thus we can determine the probability mass function for the counting variable $S_n = Z_1 + \cdots + Z_n$ itself. We note that $S_n$ will have the value $K$ if and only if $Z$ lands on any one of the specific corners of unit curve that has exactly $k$ $1$'s and $n-k$ $0$'s:

Accordingly, let $C_k^n = \{z \in 2^n \mid \sum z_i = k\}$ for each $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n$. Then, since $S-n = k \iff Z \in C^n_k$,

$$
\begin{align*}
P(S_n = k) &= P(Z \in C_k^n) \\
&= \sum_{z \in C_k^n} P(Z = z) \\
&= \sum_{\{z \mid \sum z_i = k\}} p^{\sum z_i}q^{n - \sum z_i} \\
&= |C_k^n|  p^k q^{n-k} \\
&= {n \choose k} p^k q^{n-k}
\end{align*}
$$

Now, we just need to determine the cardinalities $|C^n_k|$. For those who know, this ends up equaling the binomial coefficients:

$$
{n \choose k} = |C_k^n|
$$

We evaluate this as

> [!NOTE] Computation: Binomial Coefficients
> For any $n \in \mathbb{N}$ an any $0 \le p \le 1$, we have
> 
> $$
> \sum_{k = 0}^n {n \choose k} p^k q^{n - k} = \sum_{k = 0}^n P(S_n = k) = 1
> $$
> 
> Substituting $p = t/(1 + t)$, $t \ge 0$, we acquire the polynomial (called Newton's theorem)
> 
> $$
> (1 + t)^n = \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} t^k
> $$
> 
> Then, taking derivatives of both sides and comparing coefficients, we get
> 
> $$
> {n \choose k} = \dfrac{n}{k} {n - 1 \choose k - 1} = \dfrac{n^{(k)}}{k!} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
> $$
> 
> where $n^{(k)} = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n - k + 1)$.

You can verify that the binomial coefficients for fixed $n$ get bigger on $0 \le  k < \tfrac{n+1}{2}$ and smaller on $\tfrac{n+1}{2} < k \le n$ with

1. A unique maximum at $n/2$ when $n$ is even, or
2. A pair of maxima at $(n \pm 1)/2$ when $n$ is odd

Then, the binomial probabilities for fixed $n$,

$$
P_n(k) = P(S_n = k) = {n \choose k} p^k q^{n-k}
$$

increase strictly to a unique maximum $k_{\mathrm{max}} = [(n+1)p]$ and decrease strictly thereafter.

Now that we have the probability mass function, we can compute various expected values. If $X \sim \mathrm{bin}(n, p)$, then for each $r = 1, 2, \ldots$, we define the **$r$th descending factorial** of $X$ as

$$
X^{(r)} = X(X-1)\cdots(X-r+1)
$$

which is a polynomial of degree $r$ for every $r \in \mathbb{N}$.

Note that $X^{(r)}$ is zero when $X$ itself is between $0$ and $r-1$, so there are really only $n$ of these variables that are not entirely identically $0$ no matter the outcome of $X$.

Then, we compute the **$r$th descending factorial moment** of $X$ for $r = 1, \ldots, n$:

$$
\begin{align*}
EX^{(r)} &= \sum_{k=0}^n k^{(r)} {n \choose k} p^k q^{n-k} \\
&= \sum_{k=r}^n k^{(r)} {n \choose k} p^k q^{n-k} \\
&= \sum_{k=r}^n \dfrac{n^{(k)}}{(k-r)!} {n \choose k} p^k q^{n-k} \\
&= n^{(k)} p^r \sum_{k=r}^n \dfrac{(n-r)^{k-r}}{(k-r)!} {n \choose k} p^{k-r} q^{n-k} \\
&= n^{(r)} p^r
\end{align*}
$$

while, for $r = n+1, n+2, \ldots$,

$$
EX^{(r)}= 0
$$

We may use these descending factorial moments to solve for the **$r$th power moments**, $r = 1, 2, \ldots$. For example, $X^{(2)} = X(X-1) = X^2 - X$, so

$$
n^{(2)} p^2 = EX^{(2)} = EX^2 - EX - EX^2 - np
$$

Rearranging, we get

$$
EX^2 = n^{(2)}p^2 + np
$$

# Negative-Binomial Distributions

Now, we consider a random variable $T_k$ that counts the number of trials required to obtain a **fixed number** $k$ of successes.

Given $(Z_n, n \in \mathbb{N})$ IID $Z \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ and $(S_n, n \in S_n)$ with $S_n = Z_1 + \cdots + Z_n$ for each $k \in \mathbb{N}$, let $T_k$ count the random number of trials until (and including) the $k$th success.

$$
T_k = \min\{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\}
$$

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The random variable $Y$ is said to have a **negative-binomial** distribution on $k$ **successes**, denoted $Y \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(k, p)$ for $0 < p \le 1, k \in \mathbb{N}$, if and only if
> 
> $$
> Y \overset{d}{=} T_k 
> $$

We need to show that the set $\{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\}$ is non empty for $T_k$ to be defined.

> [!NOTE] Lemma
> We have
> 
> $$
> \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, P(\{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\} \ne \emptyset) = 1$
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We note that
> 
> $$
> \{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\} \ne \emptyset \iff \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (S_n \ge k) \ne \emptyset 
> $$
> 
> where $P(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty (S_n \ge k) \ne \emptyset) = 1$ for any value of $k \in \mathbb{N}$.
> 
> We can also see this using the complement, since
> 
> $$
> \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty) \le P(S_n < k)
> $$
> 
> Then, taking $n$ sufficiently large so that $np - k > 0$, we get
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(S_n < k) &= P(S_n - np < k - np) \\
> &= P(S_n - np < -(np - k)) \\
> &\le P(|S_n - np| > np - k) \\
> &\le \dfrac{E(S_n - np)^2}{(np-k)^2} \tag{Markov} \\
> &\le \dfrac{npq}{(np - k)^2} \to 0 
> \end{align*}
> $$

Thus, we have

$$
P(T_k \in \mathbb{N}) = 1 = \sum_{n = k}^\infty P(T_k = n)
$$

By the **well ordering principle**, we have that $T_k \in \{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\}$ and therefore then that $S_{T_k} \ge k$ and $S_n \ge k \iff T_k \le n$ (the number of successes in $n$ trials is at least $k$ if and only if the minimum number of trials to get $k$ successes is less than or equal to $n$).

If $S_{T_K} > k$, this would require $S_{T_k - 1} \ge k$, which would mean $T_k$ wasn't actually the minimum in the set. Therefore, we know that for every $k \in \mathbb{N}$ we must have $S_{T_k} = k$. Therefore, we can rewrite the original definition as

$$
T_k = \min\{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n \ge k\} = \min\{n \in \mathbb{N} \mid S_n = k \}
$$

The probability mass function for $T_k$ is relatively easy to derive. It is obvious that the $k$th success will occur on the $n$th trial if and only if there are exactly $k-1$ successes in the first $n-1$ trials then a success on the $n$th. So we can write this event as

$$
\forall n \ge k, (T_k = n) = \left(\sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} Z_i = k - 1, Z_n = 1 \right)
$$

and thus we can compute for $Y \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(k, p)$, $0 < p \le 1$, $k \in \mathbb{N}$, 

$$
\begin{align*}
P(Y = n) &= P(T_k = n) \\ 
&= P \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} Z_i = k - 1, Z_n = 1\right) \\
&= {n - 1 \choose k - 1}p^{k-1} q^{(n - 1) - (k - 1)} \cdot p \\
&= {n - 1 \choose k - 1}p^{k} q^{n-k} \\
\end{align*}
$$

for all $n \in \mathbb{N}, n \ge k$.

# Geometric Distributions

Consider the special case of the negative binomial when $k = 1$.

$$
\begin{align*}
P(T_1 = n) &= P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} Z_i = 0, Z_n = 1\right) \\
&= pq^{n-1}
\end{align*}
$$

The **probability mass function** in this case comprises a geometric series and thus has its own name.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The random variable $W$ is said to have a **geometric distribution**, denoted $W \sim \mathrm{Geo}(p)$, $0 < p \le 1$, if and only if
> 
> $$
> W \overset{d}{=} T_1
> $$

As seen above, it has probability mass function

$$
\forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(W = n) = pq^{n-1}
$$

As we will see, $W$ is not just a special case of negative binomials; we can actually construct negative binomials from geometric distributions.

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Sums of Independent Negative-Binomials
> If we have 
> 
> $$
> T \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(k, p), W \sim \mathrm{Geo}(p), T \perp W
> $$
> 
> then,
> 
> $$
> T + W \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(k + 1, p)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> Take any $\mathrm{Bern}(p)$ sequence: ($Z_i, i \in \mathbb{N}$) IID $Z \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$. 
> 
> Then, for each value of $n \ge k + 1$, we find that
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(T + W = n) = \sum_{i = 1}^\infty
> \end{align*}
> $$
