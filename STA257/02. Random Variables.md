# The Law of Large Numbers

Consider an arbitrary **mechanism**/**process**/**system** $W$ that we might wish to study. $W$ generates certain **outcomes** $w$ within a **sample space** $\Omega$:

$$
W: w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n, \ldots
$$

$W$ may denote anything from a simple computer algorithm, measurements of the length of a table, etc.

The **sample space** $\Omega$ is a set that contains all of the potential outputs/outcomes/realizations $w$, with all their potential variability, that might be output from $W$.

These outcomes are often referred to as **trials** (or **trial outcomes**) of the **process** $W$. The outcome $w_n$ is referred to as the **$n$th trial;** the finite sequence $(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n)$ is referred to as the **first $n$ trials**.

## Random Variables

Consider any real-valued function $g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on the sample space $\Omega$ and let $X = g(W)$ denote the **extended process** that first generates an outcome $w$ from $W$ and then applies the real-valued function $g$ to the outcome $w$ to produce the outcome $x = g(w)$ from the process whose name is $X = g(W)$.

The new process has its own sequence of trial outcomes, namely

$$
x_1 = g(w_1), x_2 = g(w_2), \ldots, x_n = g(w_n), \ldots
$$

The key is that the outcomes $x_i$ are real numbers, upon which we can operate with familiar operators.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Random Variables
> We call a function that maps outcomes from the sample space to a measurable space (typically a subset of $\mathbb{R}$) a **random variable**. 
> 
> Since the underlying process generates certain outcomes with certain probabilities, applying this function upon **the process outputs certain real numbers with certain probabilities**.
> 
> Oftentimes, a random variable, despite technically being a function, is treated like a variable that can take on certain values.

> [!NOTE] Definition: The Sample Mean Value Sequence
> For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the **sample mean** over the first $n$ trial is the **arithmetic average** of the function values over those $n$ trials:
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> \hat E_n X &\overset{or}{=} \hat E_n g(W) \overset{or}{=} \hat E_n g \\
> &\overset{defn}{=} \dfrac{g(w_1) + \cdots + g(w_n)}{n} \\
> &\overset{or}{=} \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n g(w_i)}{n} \\
> &\overset{or}{=} \dfrac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n} \\
> &\overset{or}{=} \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n} \\
> &\overset{or}{=} \overline x_n \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> These may also be called **sample means**, **sample averages**, **observed average**, **empirical averages**, etc.

Now, we can define the **law of large numbers**.

> [!NOTE] Definition: The Empirical Law of Large Numbers (ELLN)
> A given process $W$ is said to be a **random process/variable** if and only if it satisfies the **empirical law of large numbers** (**ELLN**): for any real-valued $X = g(W)$, we have
> 
> 1. **Stability**: the sequence of sample averages $\hat E_n X$ converges as $n \to \infty$;
> 2. **Invariance**: the limit is independent of any particular output $w_n$. 
> 
> In that case, we obtain a well-defined **expected value** in the limit
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> EX &\overset{or}{=} Eg(W) \overset{or}{=} E_W \\
> &\overset{defn}{=} \lim_{n \to \infty} \hat E_n g(W) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \overline x_n \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Probability itself its a special case of an expected value: for an **indicator function** $g = I_A$ with $A \subseteq \Omega$, we can compute the usual sequence of samples averages; this yield the proportion of times that an element in $A$ occurs in the first $n$ trials. In particular, we call these sample averages **empirical relative frequencies**.
> 
> $$
> \hat P_n(W \in A) \overset{defn}{=} \hat E_n I_A(W) = \dfrac{I_A(w_1) + \cdots + I_A(w_n)}{n}, n \in \mathbb{N}
> $$
> 
> As we take the limit $n \to \infty$, we get the **long-run frequency** which we call **probability**:
> 
> $$
> P(W \in A) \overset{or}{=} P_W(A) \overset{defn}{=} EI_A(W) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \hat P_n(W \in A)
> $$
> 
> The function $P_W$ mapping sets $A \subseteq \Omega$ to long-run frequencies is called a **probability distribution**. Random variables $W$ on a sample space $\Omega$ are imbued with a probability distribution $P_W$. We notate this as
> 
> $$
> W \sim P_W \text{ on } \Omega
> $$
> 
> and read it as "$W$ is distributed as $P_W$ on $\Omega$".

# Uniform Distributions

## Finite Discrete Uniform Distributions

Suppose we have a fair $n$-sided die: each of the die's $n$ sides has the very same change of coming up.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Finite Discrete Uniform Distributions
> For any specific $N \in \mathbb{N}$, the random variable $X$ is said to have a **finite discrete uniform distribution** on the sample space $\Omega = \{1, \ldots, N\}$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> P(X = k) = 1/N, k = 1, \ldots, N
> $$
> 
> This is denoted as $X \sim \text{unif}\{1, \ldots, N\}$.

An uniform distribution is unsurprisingly useful when modelling a fair die.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $X$ denote the process wherein we toss an ordinary fair die with the usual six sides but report the outcome only if it is one of the number $1, \ldots, 5$.
> 
> Then, the sample space is $\Omega = \{1, \ldots, 5\}$, and since every element of $\Omega$ must occur with precisely identical frequency, we have
> 
> $$
> P(X = k) = 1/5
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $Y$ denote the $2$-stage procedure:
> 
> 1. Divide the ten digits $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 0\}$ into two batches
> 
>    $$
>    A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\} \quad \text{and} \quad B = \{6, 7, 8, 9, 0\}
>    $$
> 
> 2. Toss a standard six-sided die twice so that on the first toss we go to $A$ if the die shows $1$, $2$, or $3$ and we go to $B$ if the die shows $4$, $5$, or $6$. Thus, each batch in selected half the time
> 3. Toss the die again to yield a number $k$, ignoring the $6$; we take the $k$th digit in the batch and report the result.
> 
> Since each batch is selected with uniform probability and each digit in the badge is selected with uniform probability, we arrive at each of the digits with identical frequency i.e. $1/10$ of the time each.

## Continuous Uniform Distributions

Consider the following hypothetical.

> [!NOTE] Consider the Following
> 
> Let $U$ denote the random variable that generates an infinite decimal expansion of a number between $0$ and $1$ by generating a digit from $0$ to $9$ and infinite number of times.
> 
> In other words, a single outcome $u$ of $U$ entails an infinite number of repetitions $Y_i$ of the finite procedure $Y$, which generates a digit from $0$ to $9$, to yield
> 
> $$
> U = \sum_{i=1}^\infty Y_i/10^i = 0.Y_1Y_2Y_3\cdots
> $$
> 
> In practice, we may only generate an outcome to a finite number of decimal places. For example, we might generate $U$ to four decimal places $0.y_1y_2y_3y_4$, then there are $10000$ equally likely possibilities for what the generated result may be.
> 
> The "actual" value of $U$ is only known to be somewhere between the value $0.y_1y_2y_3y_4$ and $0.y_1y_2y_3y_4 + 0.0001$; In other words, the outcome is only known to be in one particular subinterval (out of $10000$ equally likely subintervals) of $[0, 1]$. That is,
> 
> $$
> P(0.y_1y_2y_3y_4 \le U \le 0.y_1y_2y_3y_4 + 0.0001) = 1/10000
> $$
> 
> for each of the $10000$ possible values $0.y_1y_2y_3y_4$.
> 
> Thus, we deduce that for any number $0.a_1a_2a_3a_4$,
> 
> $$
> P(0 \le U \le 0.a_1a_2a_3a_4) = a_1a_2a_3a_4 /10000 = 0.a_1a_2a_3a_4
> $$
> 
> More generally, we can conclude that if $u$ is any finite decimal in the interval $[0, 1]$, then $P(U \le u) = u$; for any pair of such finite decimals $a, b \in [0, 1]$, we have the **uniformity condition**
> 
> $$
> P(a \le U \le b) = b - a
> $$
> 
> Note that this means the probability of obtaining one specific value $u$ is zero.
> 
> $$
> P(U = u) = P(u \le U \le u) = u - u = 0
> $$

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The random variable $U$ is said to have a **continuous uniform distribution** on the **unit interval** $[0, 1]$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> \forall 0 \le u \le 1, P(U \le u) = u
> $$
> 
> We denote this as $U \sim \mathrm{unif}[0, 1]$.

To extend the evaluation of probabilities from finite decimals to infinite decimals, we require continuity of probability; this will be explored later.

Consider the random variable $V = 1 - U$. We can compute the distribution of $V$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
P(V \le u) &= P(1 - U \le u) \\ 
&= P(U \ge 1 - u)\\
&= 1 - P(U \ge 1 - u) \\
&= 1 - (1-u) \\
&= u \\
&= P(U \le u)
\end{align*}
$$

As random variables, $U$ and $B$ behave exactly the same way; they have the same stochastic behaviour. Thus, they are said to be **equal-in-distribution**: $V \overset{d}{=} U$.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Equality-in-Distribution
> Two random variables $W_1$ and $W_2$ on the same sample space $\Omega$ are said to be **identically distributed/equi-distributed/stochastically identical** if and only if
> 
> $$
> \forall g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, E g(W_1) = E g(W_2) 
> $$
> 
> This is denoted as $\overset{d}{=}$ is an **equivalence relation** between random variables.
> 
> For real-valued random variables, it is equivalent to the statement
> 
> $$
> \forall w \in \mathbb{R}, P(W_1 \le w) = P(W_2 \le w)
> $$
> 
> but the expected value form lends itself better to proofs.

Equality-in-distribution is also perfectly preserved under arbitrary transformation:

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Invariance 1
> For any function $\phi: \Omega \to \mathcal{X}$ and random variables $W_1$ and $W_2$ on $\Omega$.
> 
> $$
> W_1 \overset{d}{=} W_2 \implies \phi(W_1) \overset{d}{=} \phi(W_2)
> $$
> 
> **Proof**. Let $\phi: \Omega \to \mathcal{X}$ be a function. Assume $W_1 \overset{d}{=} W_2$ on $\Omega$. By definition, this means that 
> 
> $$
> \forall g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, E g(W_1) = E g(W_2) \tag{$\star$}
> $$
> 
> Now, we want to show that
> 
> $$
> \forall h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}, E h(\phi(W_1)) = E h(\phi(W_2))
> $$
> 
> Let $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function. Take $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R} = h \circ \phi$. By $(\star)$,
> 
> $$
> E f(W_1) = E f(W_2) \implies E h(\phi(W_1)) = E h(\phi(W_2))
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Invariance 2
> For random variables $W_1$ and $W_2$ on $\Omega$,
> 
> $$
> W_1 \overset{d}{=} W_2 \iff \forall g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, g(W_1) \overset{d}{=} g(W_2)
> $$
> 
> **Proof**. ($\implies$) Assume $W_1 \overset{d}{=} W_2$. By definition, this means that 
> 
> $$
> \forall f_1: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, Ef_1(W_1) = Ef_1(W_2) \tag{$\star$}
> $$
> 
> Let $g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. We must show that $g(W_1) \overset{d}{=} g(W_2)$ i.e.
> 
> $$
> \forall f_2: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, Ef_2(g(W_1)) = Ef_2(g(W_2))
> $$
> 
> Let $f_2: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. Then let $h: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined by $h = f_2 \circ g$. By $(\star)$, we have
> 
> $$
> Eh(W_1) = Eh(W_2) \implies Ef_2(g(W_1)) = Ef_2(g(W_2))
> $$
> 
> as required.
> 
> ($\Longleftarrow$). Assume that for an arbitrary function $g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, we have $g(W_1) \overset{d}{=} g(W_2)$. By definition, this means that
> 
> $$
> \forall g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, f_1: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, Ef_1(g(W_1)) = Ef_1(g(W_2)) \tag{$\bullet$}
> $$
> 
> We must show that $W_1 \overset{d}{=} W_2$ i.e.
> 
> $$
> \forall f_2: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, Ef_2(W_1) = Ef_2 (W_2)
> $$
> 
> Let $f_2: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function. Then, by $(\bullet)$ with $g = f_2$ and $f_1(x) = x$ (identity function). Then, we have 
> 
> $$
> Ef_1(g(W_1)) = Ef_1(g(W_2)) \implies Ef_2(W_1) = Ef_2(W_2)
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

## The Exponential Distribution

Let $Z = -\ln U$ with $U \sim \mathrm{unif}[0, 1]$. Then it is straightforward to compute that, for any non-negative $0 \le s \le t$,

$$
P(s \le Z \le t) = e^{-s} - e^{-t}
$$

We compute

$$
\begin{align*}
s \le Z \le t  &\iff s \le -\ln U \le t \\
&\iff -t \le \ln U \le -s \\
&\iff e^{-t} \le U \le e^{-s}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, we have

$$
P(s \le Z \le t) = P(e^{-t} \le U \le e^{-s}) = e^{-s} - e^{-t}
$$

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The random variable $Z$ is said to have a **standard exponential distribution** on $[0, \infty)$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> Z \overset{d}{=} -\ln U \quad \text{with} \quad U \sim \mathrm{unif}[0, 1]
> $$
> 
> We denote this as $Z \sim \mathrm{exp}(1)$.

Now, we can take any positive number $\theta > 0$ and let $X \overset{d}{=} \theta Z$. Then we have, for any $x \ge 0$,

$$
P(X \le x) = P(Z \le x/\theta) = 1 - e^{-x/\theta}
$$

> [!NOTE] Definition
> The random variable $X$ is said to have a **scaled exponential distribution** with **scale parameter** $\theta > 0$ if and only if
> 
> $$
> X \overset{d}{=} \theta Z \quad \text{with} \quad Z \sim \mathrm{exp}(1)
> $$

## Distribution Functions

Take any strictly monotone and continuously differentiable function $g$ (Notated as either $g \uparrow \uparrow, C^1$ or $g \downarrow \downarrow, C^1$) on the interval $[0, 1]$ and let $X \overset{d}{=} g(U)$ with $U \sim \mathrm{unif}[0, 1]$.

Then, just as we did for the exponential distribution above, here we determine that, for any $x \in g([0, 1])$,

$$
\begin{align*}
P(X \le x) &= P(g(U) \le x) \\ 
&= \begin{cases}
P(U \le g^{-1}(x)) & \text{if }g \uparrow \uparrow \\
P(U \ge g^{-1}(x)) & \text{if }g \downarrow \downarrow \\
\end{cases} \\
&= \begin{cases}
g^{-1}(x) & \text{if }g \uparrow \uparrow \\
1 - g^{-1}(x)& \text{if }g \downarrow \downarrow \\
\end{cases} \\
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, we have, for any $s \le t$ in $g[0, 1]$,

$$
P(s < X \le t) = \begin{cases}
g^{-1}(t) - g^{-1}(s) & \text{if }g \uparrow \uparrow \\
g^{-1}(s) - g^{-1}(t) & \text{if }g \downarrow \downarrow \\
\end{cases} \\
$$

Now consider this scenario in reverse. Suppose that we are given some $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variable $X$ and already know how to get all probabilities $P(X \le x)$ for every value of $x \in \mathbb{R}$. This means that we have full knowledge of the function

$$
F: \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1] \quad \text{by} \quad x \mapsto P(X \le x)
$$

No matter what $X$ is, we know that this function $F$ would certainly be non-decreasing ($F\uparrow$). We also know that

$$
P(s < X \le t) = F(t) - F(s) \tag{$\star$}
$$

This function $F$ is referred to as the **distribution function** of $X$. Formally,

> [!NOTE] Definition: Distribution Functions
> For any $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variable $X$, the **distribution function** of $X$ is given by
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> F(x) &\overset{or}{=} F_X(x) \\
> &\overset{defn}{=} P(X \le x) \\
> &\overset{of}{=} P_X(-\infty, x]
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

### Distribution Functions for Continuous Functions

We get the following general result in the continuously differentiable case.

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Distribution Functions for Continuous Functions
> Define $X \overset{d}{=} g(U)$ with continuous, strictly monotone isomorphism $g: [0, 1] \to [a, b]$ and $U \sim \mathrm{unif}[0, 1]$. 
> 
> Then, the following is an equivalent statement: the distribution function $F_X: [a, b] \to [0, 1]$ is also a continuous isomorphism.

> [!NOTE] Proof
> ($\implies$) We have
> 
> $$
> F = \begin{cases}
> g^{-1} & g \uparrow \uparrow \\
> 1 - g^{-1} & g \downarrow \downarrow
> \end{cases}
> $$
> 
> so in either case $F$ is $\uparrow \uparrow, C^1$
> 
> ($\Longleftarrow$) Let $g = F^{-1}$. Then we have
> 
> $$
> P(g(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x) = P(X \le x)
> $$
> 
> and this it is clear that $X \overset{d}{=} g(U)$ with $g \uparrow \uparrow, C^1$.

Recall the above equation $(\star)$. We can apply the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Let $f(x) = F'(x)$ for all $x$. Then, for any $a \le s \le  t \le b$, for any

$$
P(s < X \le t) = F(t) - F(s) = \int_s^t f(x) dx
$$

while, at each $x \in g([0, 1])$, we have

$$
\lim_{s \to x^-, t \to x^+} \dfrac{P(s < X \le t)}{t - s} = \lim_{s \to x^-, t \to x^+} \dfrac{F(t) - F(s)}{t - s} = F'(x) = f(x)
$$

We can interpret $f(x)$ as an "amount of probability per unit length at the point $x$". We call this function $f = F'$ is referred to as the **probability density function** of a random variable $X$. 

More generally, we have the following definition.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Probability Density Functions
> An $\mathbb{R}$-valued random variable $X$ is said to be **absolutely continuous** if and only if $\exists f: \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)$ such that, $\forall s, t \in \mathbb{R}, s \le t$, we have
> 
> $$
> P(s < X \le t) = \int_s^t f(x) dx
> $$
> 
> in which case, the function $f$ (also written as $f_X$) is referred to as the **probability density function** of $X$. Note that this function $f$ is **not** necessarily unique.
> 
> Further note that, for any absolutely continuous $X$, we have
> 
> $$
> \forall x \in \mathbb{R}, P(X = x) = \int_x^x f(x) dx = 0
> $$
> 
> There is no discrete contribution to the distribution at any $x \in \mathbb{R}$. This means the including or excluding the boundary points of intervals will make no different in the evaluation of a probability
> 
> $$
> P(s \le X \le t) = P(s < X < t) = P(s < X \le t) = P(s \le X < t)
> $$
> 
> This **does not hold** for discrete distributions.

We use this to extend our previous proposition on distribution functions for continuous functions

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Distribution Functions for Continuous Functions, Continued
> Let $F_X: [a, b] \to [0, 1]$ be a continuous automorphism.
> 
> The following statement is equivalent: Let $f = F'$ with $f > 0$ and $F$ continuous on $[a, b]$
> 
> $$
> F_X(x) = \int_a^x f(s) ds 
> $$ 

Now, we can construct a large class of absolutely continuous random variables $X = g(U)$ on an arbitrary interval $[a, b] \subset \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$.

1. Take any continuous function $h: (a, b) \to (0, \infty)$ with finite total integral, and let $f(x) = h(x) / \int_a^b h(s) dx$ and thus $F(x) = \int_a^x h(x) dx / \int_a^b h(s) dx$. Then take $g = F^{-1}$.
2. Take any $H: [a, b] \to [c, d]$ with $0 < d - c$ such that $H \uparrow \uparrow, C^1$. Let $F(x) = (H(x) - H(a))/(H(b) - H(a))$. Then take $g = F^{-1}$.
3. Take any $\phi: [c, d] \to [a, b]$ with $c < d$ such that $\phi \uparrow \uparrow, C^1$ and let $g(u) = \phi((d-c)u + c)$

> [!NOTE] Proposition: Invertible Distribution Functions
> If we define a random variable $X$ to be $X = f(U)$ with $g = F^{-1}$, then we have
> 
> $$
> F(X) \overset{d}{=} U
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> $$
> P(F(X) \le u) = P(X \le g(u)) = F(g(u)) = u = P(U \le u)
> $$

For any $0 \le p \le 1$, the value $x_p = g(p)$ is referred to as the **$100 \times p$th percentile/quantile** of $X$ and the function $g$ is referred to as the **percentile function/quantile function**:

$$
P(X \le x_p) = F(g(p)) = p
$$

In any case, the three methods outlined above will both lead to the same basic result, each with its own **probability distribution functions** $f$, its own distribution function $F$, and its own percentile function $g$.

# Expected Value as a Linear Operator

## Probability as an Additive Map

The expected value operator is linear. Recall that the data that come from the random variables $X + Y$ and $aX$ (for a scalar $a \in \mathbb{R}$) look respectively like

$$
\begin{align*}
X + Y&: x_1 + y_1, x_2 +y_2, \ldots, x_n + y_n, \ldots \\
aX&: ax_1, ax_2, \ldots, ax_n, \ldots
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, by definition, we get

$$
\begin{align*}
E(X + Y) &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{(x_1 + y_1) + \cdots + (x_n + y_n)}{n} \\
&= \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n} + \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{y_1 + \cdots + y_n}{n} \\
&= EX + EY
\end{align*}
$$


and

$$
\begin{align*}
E(aX) &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{(ax_1) + \cdots + (ax_n)}{n} \\
&= a\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n} \\
&= aEX
\end{align*}
$$

### Finite Additivity

Applying linearity to indicator functions yields the property of **finite additivity** for the measure of probability: If we have two mutually disjoint sets $A$ and $B$, then we have the result

$$
I(A + B) = I(A) + I(B)
$$

> [!TIP] Remark
> Recall that we can write $A + B$ to mean $A \cup B$ when $A$ and $B$ are disjoint.

Thus, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
P(A + B) &= EI_{A + B}(W) \\
&= E(I_A(W) + I_B(W)) \\
&=EI_A(W) + EI_B(W) \\
&= P(A) + P(B)
\end{align*}
$$

This extends to any finite number of mutually disjoint sets $A_1, \ldots, A_n$. Thus, probability is an **additive map** over disjoint sets.3bl
### Constant Variables

Consider the constant functions $g$ on the sample space $\Omega$ i.e. $g(W) = c$. These are perfectly valid functions and represent a transformed process that outputs the same number over and over again.

Thus, we trivially have

$$
\forall c \in \mathbb{R}, Ec = c
$$

### Probability of the Entire Sample Space

Consider the indicator function $I_\Omega$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space. This indicator function is always $1$ for all outputs of a process $W$ with sample space $\Omega$. Thus, we have, via the expectation of constant variables,

$$
I_\Omega(W) = 1 \implies P(\Omega) = E1 = 1
$$

### Non-Negativity

We also have **non-negativity** over the expectation operator:

$$
X \ge 0 \implies EX \ge 0
$$

Thus, when the expectation operator is applied to indicator functions, which are always non-negative, we have, for any event $A \subseteq \Omega$,

$$
0 \le I_A \le 1 \implies 0 \le EI_A = P(A) \le 1
$$

## Arbitrary Finite Discrete Distributions

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A **finite scheme** is a name for any **finite discrete distribution** where we write
> 
> $$
> W \sim \begin{pmatrix}
> \omega_1 & \cdots & \omega_N \\
> p_1 & \cdots & \omega_N
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
> 
> to symbolize the **probability mass function** (**PMF**)
> 
> $$
> i = 1, \ldots, N, P(W = \omega_i) = p_i, \quad \text{where } \sum_{i=1}^N p_i = 1 \text{ and } p_i \ge 0
> $$

We can generate such a scheme for any set of rational $p_i$'s: Just write the $p_i$'s over a common denominator $n$ and then divide the sample space for a **standard finite discrete uniform distribution** $U \sim \mathrm{unif}\{1, \ldots, n\}$ into $N$ mutually disjoint batches of cardinality $np_i$ each, naming each batch in turn $\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_N$.

If the uniform distribution outputs and outcome in batch $i$, the scheme outputs $\omega_i$.

We may sometimes adopt the ‘vector’ notation to write such a scheme more elegantly

$$
W \sim \begin{pmatrix}
 \omega \\ p
\end{pmatrix} \quad \text{where } p \ge 0, p \cdot 1 = 1
$$

Then, for any $\mathbb{R}$-valued function $g(W)$, the **expected value** is

$$
E g(W) = \sum_{i=1}^N g(\omega_i) P(W = \omega_i) = \sum_{i = 1}^N g(\omega_i) p_i
$$

To verify this, it is only necessary to realize that any such **finite discrete random variable** may be explicitly represented as a finite linear combination of indicator functions

$$
g(W) = \sum_{i=1}^N g(\omega_i) I(W = \omega_i) = \sum_{i=1}^N g(\omega_i) I_{\{\omega_i\}}(W)
$$

Note that this means

$$
g(W) = \sum_{i=1}^N g(\omega_i) I(W = \omega_i) \implies g(W) =  g(\omega_j) \text{ when } W = \omega_j
$$

Applying $E$ to this gives the result above; recall that probability is a special case of expected value: $P(W = \omega) = EI_{\{\omega\}}(W)$.
