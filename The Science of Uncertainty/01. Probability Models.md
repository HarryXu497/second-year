# Probability: A Measure of Uncertainty

Probability is the science of uncertainty. It provides precise mathematical rules and a framework for working with our limited knowledge and making sensible decisions based on what we do and do not know.

A common way of thinking about probability is in terms of **relative frequency**. For example, to say that a coin has a 50% chance of coming up heads can be interpreted as saying that, if we flipped the coin many, many times, then approximately half of the time it would come up heads. This interpretation has some limitations. In many cases, it is impossible to repeat events. Furthermore, this interpretation lacks rigour. However,  despite these limitations, the relative frequency interpretation is a useful way to think of probabilities and to develop intuition about them.

Another interpretation is the **Bayesian interpretation**, where probability is defined as a degree of belief in a event, given prior information. This perspective, along with Bayesian statistics, is more advanced and will be explored later.

# Probability Models

A formal definition of probability begins with a **sample size**, often written $S$. This sample space is any set that lists all possible **outcomes** (or **responses**) of some unknown experiment or situation that we are interested in. For example, perhaps

$$
S = \{\text{rain}, \text{snow}, \text{clear}\}
$$

when predicting tomorrow's weather. Or perhaps $S$ is the set of all positive real numbers, when predicting next week's stock price. $S$ can be any set at all, even an infinite set.

A probability model requires a collection of **events**, which are subsets of $S$ to which probabilities can be assigned. For the above weather example, the subsets

$$
\{\text{rain}\}, \{\text{snow}\}, \{\text{rain}, \text{snow}\}, \{\text{rain}, \text{snow}, \text{clear}\}, \emptyset
$$

are all examples of subsets of $S$ that could be events. Note that here the comma means "or". For example $\{\text{rain}, \text{snow}\}$ is the even that it will rain or snow. We will generally assume that all subsets of $S$ are events (Sometimes this isn't true but that is very complex).

Finally, and most importantly, a probability model requires a probability measure, usually written $P$. This probability measure must assign, to each event $A$, a probability $P(A)$. We require the following properties:

1. $P(A)$ is always a nonnegative real number, between $0$ and $1$ inclusive.
2. $P(\emptyset) = 0$ i.e. if $A$ is the empty set $\emptyset$, then $P(A) = 0$.
3. $P(S) = 1$ i.e. if $A$ is the entire sample space $S$, then $P(A) = 1$.
4. $P$ is (countably) additive, meaning that if $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ is a finite or countable sequence of disjoint events, then $P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots )= P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \cdots$

The first three properties are standard and intuitive. The fourth property is the most subtle. It says that we can calculate probabilities of complicated events by adding up the probabilities of smaller events, provided that those events are **disjoint** and together contain the entire complicated event. Events are **disjoint** if they contain no outcomes in common. For example, $\{\text{rain}\}$ and $\{\text{snow, clear}\}$ are disjoint, whereas $\{\text{rain}\}$ and $\{\text{rain, clear}\}$ are not disjoint. Thus, we should have $P(\{\text{rain}\}) + P(\{\text{snow, clear}\} = \{\text{rain, snow, clear}\}$.

We now formalize the definition of a probability model.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A **probability model** consists of a nonempty set called the sample space $S$; a collection of events that are subsets of $S$; and a probability measure assigning a probability between $0$ and $1$ to each event, with $P(\emptyset) = 0$ and $P(S) = 1$ and with $P$ (countably) additive.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose we flip three fair coins in a row and keep track of the sequence of heads and tails that result. Then,
> 
> $$
> S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}
> $$
> 
> Furthermore, each of these eight outcomes is equally likely. Thus, $P(HHH) = 1/8$. Also, the probability that the first coin is heads and the second is rails, but the third coin can be anything, is equal to the sum of the probabilities of the events $HTH$ and $HTT$ i.e.
> 
> $$
> P(HTH) + P(HTT) = 1/8 + 1/8 + 1/4
> $$

Note that it is also possible to define probability models on (countably and uncountably infinite sample spaces as well.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose that $S = [0, 1]$ is the unit interval. We can define a probability measure $P$ on $S$ by saying that 
> 
> $$
> P([a, b]) = b - a, \quad \text{whenever }0 \le a \le b \le 1
> $$
> 
> This example is called the **uniform distribution on $[0,1]$**. It is one example of a distribution on uncountable state spaces.

A \cup B = (A \cap B^c) \cup (A \cap B) \cup (A^c \cap B)

(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c

$$
(A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c

namely, the subset of elements that are not in both $A$ and $B$ is given by the set of elements not in $A$ or not in $B$.

# Properties of Probability Models

The additive property of probability measures automatically implies certain basic properties. There are true for **any** probability model.

If $A$ is any event, we know that $A$ and its complement $A^c$ are always disjoint. Furthermore, their union is always the entire sample space: $A \cup A^c = S$. hence, by the additive property, we must have $P(A) + P(A^c) = P(S)$. But we always have $P(S) = 1$. Thus

$$
P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)
$$

This makes intuitive sense: the probability that any event does not occur is equal to one minus the probability that is does occur.

Now suppose that $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ are events that form a **partition** of the sample space $S$. This means that $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ are disjoint and their union is equal to the sample space $S$. We have the following basic theorem that allows us to decompose the calculation of the probability of $B$ into the sum of the probabilities of the sets $A_i \cap B$. Often these are easier to compute.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Law of Total Probability, Unconditioned Version
> Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be events that form a partition of the sample space $A$. Let $B$ be any event. Then,
> 
> $$
> P(B) = P(A_1 \cap B) + P(A_2 \cap B) + \cdots
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> The events $(A_1 \cup B), (A_2 \cup B), \ldots$ are disjoint since $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ are disjoint. Their union is $B$ since $A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots = S$. Hence, the result follows immediately from the additivity property.

A somewhat more useful version of the law of total probability, and applications of its use, are provided later.

Suppose now that $A$ and $B$ are two events such that $A$ contains $B$ (i.e. $B \subseteq A$). In words, all outcomes in $B$ are also in $A$. Intuitively, $A$ is a "larger" event than $B$, so we would expect its probability to be larger. We have the following result.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A$ and $B$ be two events with $B \subseteq A$. Then
> 
> $$
> P(A) = P(B) + P(A \cap B^c)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We can write $A = B \cup (A \cap B^c)$, where $B$ and $A \cap B^c$ are disjoint. Hence $P(A) = P(B) + P(A \cap B^c)$ by additivity.

Because we always have $P(A \cap B^c) \ge 0$, we conclude the following,

> [!NOTE] Corollary: Monotonicity
> Let $A$ and $B$ be two events, with $B \subseteq A$. Then
> 
> $$
> P(B) \le P(A)
> $$

We also obtain the following

> [!NOTE] Corollary
> Let $A$ and $B$ be two events, with $B \subseteq A$. Then
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(B)
> $$

More generally, even if we do not have $B \subseteq A$, we have the following property.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion, Two-Event Version
> Let $A$ and $B$ be events. Then,
> 
> $$
> P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We can write $A \cup B = (A \cap B^c) \cup (B \cap A^c) \cup (A \cap B)$, where $A \cap B^c$, $B \cap A^c$, and $A \cap B$ are disjoint. By additivity, we have
> 
> $$
> P(A \cup B) = P(A \cap B^c) + P(B \cap A^c) + P(A \cap B) \tag{1}
> $$
> 
> Using the above corollary (with $B$ replace by $A \cap B$), we have
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B^c) = P(A \cap (A \cap B)^c) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) \tag{2}
> $$
> 
> and similarly,
> 
> $$
> P(B \cap A^c) = P(B) - P(A \cap B) \tag{3}
> $$
> 
> Then, substituting $(1)$ and $(2)$ into $(3$), we obtain
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(A \cup B) &= P(A) - P(A \cap B) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B) \\
> &= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> as required. $\blacksquare$

This extends to $n$ events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion, $n$-Event Version
> Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be events. Then,
> 
> $$
> P(A \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = \sum_{i = 1}^n P(A_i) - \sum_{i, j = 1; i < j}^n P(A_i \cap A_j) + \sum_{i, j, k = 1; i<j<k}^n P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots \pm P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n)
> $$
> 
> This can be proven with induction. Below is a derivation of the $n = 3$ case:
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(A \cup B \cup C) &= P(A \cup B) + P(C) - P((A \cup B) \cap C) \\
> &=P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) + P(C) - P((A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)) \\ 
> &=P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) + P(C) - P(A \cap C) -  P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \\ 
> &=P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) + P(C) - P(A \cap C) -  P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \\ 
> &=P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) -  P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \\ 
> \end{align*}
> $$

Sometimes we do not need to evaluate the probability content of a unit. We need only know it is bounded above by the sum of the probabilities of the individual events. This is called subadditivity.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Subadditivity
> Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be a finite or countably infinite sequence of events, not necessary disjoint. Then
> 
> $$
> P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) \le P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \cdots
> $$

# Uniform Probability on Finite Spaces

If the sample space $S$ is finite, then one possible probability measure on $S$ is the **uniform** probability measure, which assigns probability $1/|S|$ to each outcome. Here $|S|$ is the number of elements in the sample space $S$. By additivity, it then follows that for any event $A$ we have

$$
P(A) = \dfrac{|A|}{|S|}
$$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose we roll a six-sided die. The possible outcomes are $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, so $|S| = 6$. If each outcome is equally likely, then we have $P(\{i\}) = 1/6$ for each $i \in S$.  We also have, for example, $P(\{3, 4\}) = 2/6 = 1/3$, $P(\{1, 5, 6\}) = 3/6 = 1/2$, etc.

## Combinatorial Principles

The field of mathematics concerns with counting is called **combinatorics**. In this section, we consider a few simple combinatorial rules and their application in probability theory when the uniform distribution is appropriate.

Suppose we have $k$ finite sets $S_1, \ldots, S_k$ and we want to count the number of sequences of length $k$ where the $i$th element comes from $S_i$ i.e. the number of elements in 

$$
S = \{(s_1, \ldots, s_k): s_i \in S_i\} = S_1 \times \cdots \times S_k
$$

The **multiplication principle** says that the number of such sequences is obtained by multiplying together the number of elements in each set $S_i$ i.e.

$$
|S| = |S_1|\cdots |S_k|
$$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose we roll two fair six-sided die. What is the probability that the sum of the numbers showing is equal to $10$? By the multiplication principle, the total number of possible outcomes is equal to $6 \times 6 = 36$. Of these outcomes, there are three that sum to $10$, namely $(4, 6)$, $(5, 5)$, and $(6, 4)$. Thus, the probability that the sum is $10$ is equal to $3/36 = 1/12$.

### Counting Permutations

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose four friends go to a restaurant, and each checks his or her coat. At the end of the meal, the four coats are _randomly_ returned to the four people. What is the probability that each of the four people gets their own coat? Here the total number of different ways the coats can be returned is equal to $4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 4!$.
> 
> This is because the first coat can be returned to any of the four friends, the second coat to any of the three remaining friends, and so on. Only one of these assignments is correct. Hence, the probability that each of the four people gets their own coat is equal to $1/4! = 1/24$.
> 
> Here we are counting permutations, or sequences of elements from a set where no element appears more than once

We can use the multiplication principle to count permutations more generally. For example, suppose $|S| = n$ and we want to count the number of elements of the set

$$
 \{(s_1, \ldots, s_k): s_i \in S, s_i \ne s_j \text{ when }i \ne j\}
$$

Then we have $n$ choices for the first element $s_1$, $n-1$ choices for the second element, and finally $n - (k-1) = n - k+1$ choices for the last element. So there are 

$$
n \cdot (n-1) \cdots (n - k + 1) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}
$$

permutations of length $k$ from a set of $n$ elements. Notice that when $k = n$, there are $n!$ permutations of length $n$.

### Counting Subsets

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose 10 fair coins are flipped. What is the probability that exactly seven of them are heads? By the multiplication principle, we know the total number of possible outcomes is equal to $2^10 = 1024$. hence, the probability of any particular sequence occurring is $1/1024$. But of these sequences, how many have exactly seven heads?
> 
> To answer this, notice that we may specify such a sequence by giving the positions of the seven heads, which involves choosing a subset of size 7 from the set of possible indices $\{1, \ldots, 10\}$. There are $10!/3! = 10 \cdots 4$ different permutations of length $7$ from $\{1, \ldots, 10\}$, and each permutation specifies a sequence of seven heads and three tails. But we can permute the indices specifying where the heads go in $7!$ different ways without changing the sequence of heads and tails. So the total number of outcomes is equal to
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{10!}{3!7!} = 120
> $$
> 
> The probability that exactly seven of the 10 coins are heads is therefore equal to $120/1024$, or just under $12\%$.

In general, if we have a set $S$ of $n$ elements, then the number of different subsets of size $k$ that we can construct by choosing elements from $S$ is

$$
{n \choose k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
$$

which is called the **binomial coefficient**. This is because there are $n!/(n-k)!$ permutations of length $k$ obtains from the set; each such permutation, and the $k!$ permutations obtained by permuting it, specify a unique subset of $S$.

It follows, for example, that the probability of obtaining exactly $k$ heads when flipping a total of $n$ fair coins is given by

$$
{n\choose k} 2^{-n} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}2^{-n}
$$

More generally, if each coin has probability $\theta$ of being heads (and probability $1- \theta$ of being tails), where $0 \le \theta \le 1$, then the probability of obtaining exactly $k$ heads when flipping a total of $n$ such coins is given by

$$
{n\choose k} \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}
$$

because each of the $n \choose k$ different patterns of $k$ heads and $n-k$ tails has probability $\theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}$ of occurring (this follows from the discussion of independence later). If $\theta = 1/2$, then this reduces to the previous formula.

### Counting Sequences of Subsets and Partitions

Suppose we have a set $S$ of $n$ elements and we want to count the number of elements of

$$
\{(S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_l): S_i \subset S, |S_i| = k_i, S_i\cap S_j = \emptyset \text{ when }i \ne j \}
$$

Namely, we want to count the number of sequences of $l$ subsets of a set where no two subsets have any elements in common and the $i$th subset has $k_i$ elements. By the multiplication principle, this equals

 $$
{n \choose k_1}{n - k_1 \choose k_2}\cdots{n - k_1 - \cdots - k_{l-1}\choose k_l} \\ 
= \dfrac{n!}{k_1! \cdots k_{l-1}!k_l! (n-k_1 - \cdots - k_l)!}
$$

because we can choose the elements of $S_1$ in $n \choose k_1$ ways, choose the elements of $S_2$ in $n - k_1 \choose k_2$ ways, etc.

When we have that $S = S_1 \cup S_2 \cup \cdots \cup S_l$, in addition to the individual sets being mutually disjoint, then we are counting the number of **ordered partitions** of a set of $n$ elements with $k_1$ elements in the first set, $k_2$ elements in the second set, etc. In this case, we have

$$
{n \choose k_1 \ k_2 \ \ldots \ k_l} = \dfrac{n!}{k_1!k_2!\cdots k_l!}
$$

which is called the **multinomial coefficient**.

For example, how many different bridge hands are there? By this we mean how many different ways can a deck of $52$ cards be divided up into four hands of $13$ cards each, with the hands labelled North, East, South, and West, respectively. By the above formula, we get

$$
{52 \choose 13 \ 13 \ 13 \ 13} = \dfrac{52!}{13!13!13!13!} \approx 5.364474 \times 10^{28}
$$

which is a very large number.

# Conditional Probability and Independence

Consider the three-coin example again, where we flip three different fair coins, and 

$$
S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}
$$

with $P(s) = 1/8$ for each $s \in S$. The probability that the first coin comes up heads is $1/2$. Formally, this is because

$$
P(\text{first coin heads}) = P(\{HHT, HHT, HTH, HTT\}) = 4/8 = 1/2
$$

Suppose now that we know that exactly two of the three coins came up heads. This changes our available information and also our corresponding probabilities. If we know that exactly two of the coins are heads, then we know that the outcome was one of $HHT$, $HTH$, and $THH$. These three outcomes are still equally likely, and because only the first two correspond to the first coin being heads, then the probability of the first coin being heads is $2/3$.

We have just computed a **conditional probability**. We write this in mathematical notation as

$$
P(\text{first coin heads} \mid \text{two coins heads}) = 2/3
$$

Here the vertical bar is read as "conditional on" or "given that".

## Conditional Probability

In general, given two events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the **conditional probability** of $A$ given $B$, written $P(A \mid B)$, stands for the fraction of the time that $A$ occurs once we know that $B$ occurs. It is computed as the ratio of the probability that $A$ and $B$ both occur, divided by the probability that $B$ occurs, as follows.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> Given two events $A$ and $B$, with $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is equal to
> 
> $$
> P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
> $$

In the previously computed example, $A$ is the event that the first coin is heads, while $B$ is the event that exactly two coins were heads. In mathematical terms, $A = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT\}$ and $B = \{HHT, HTH, THH\}$. It follows that $A \cap B = \{HHT, HTH\}$. Therefore

$$
P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \dfrac{P(\{HHT, HTH\})}{P(\{HHT, HTH, THH\})} = \dfrac{2/8}{3/8} = \dfrac{2}{3}
$$

as already computed. On the other hand, we similarly compute that 

$$
P(\text{first coin tails} \mid \text{two coins heads}) = 1/3
$$

We thus see that conditioning on some event can make probabilities either increase or decrease.

The definition of $P(B \mid A)$ immediately leads to the **multiplication formula**

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B \mid A)
$$

This allows us to compute the joint probability of $A$ and $B$ when we are given the probability of $A$ and the conditional probability of $B$ given $A$.

Conditional probability allows us to express the law of total probability, in a different and sometimes more helpful way.

### Law of Total Probability, Conditioned Version

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Law of Total Probability, Conditioned Version
> Let $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be events that form a partition of the sample space $S$, each of positive probability. Let $B$ be any event. Then
> 
> $$
> P(B) = P(A_1)P(B \mid A_1) + P(A_2)P(B \mid A_2) + \cdots
> $$
> 
> This follows immediately from the unconditioned version of the law of total probability and the multiplication formula above.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose a class contains $60\%$ girls and $40\%$ boys. Suppose that $30\%$ of the girls have long hair and $20\%$ of the boys have long hair. A student is chosen uniformly at random from the class. What is the probability that the chosen student will have long hair?
> 
> To answer this, let $A_1$ be the set of girls and $A_2$ be the set of boys. Then $\{A_1, A_2\}$ is a partition of the class. We further let $B$ be the set of all students with long hair.
> 
> We want $P(B)$. We compute this by the conditioned version of the law of total probability as
> 
> $$
> P(B) = P(A_1)P(B \mid A_1) + P(A_2)P(B \mid A_2) = (0.6)(0.3) + (0.4)(0.2) = 0.26
> $$
> 
> so there is a $26\%$ chance that the randomly chosen student has long hair.

### Bayes' Theorem

Suppose now that $A$ and $B$ are two events, each of positive probability. In some applications, we are given the values of $P(A)$, $P(B)$, and $P(B \mid A)$ and want to compute $P(A \mid B)$. The following result establishes a simple relationship among these quantities.

> [!NOTE] Theorem: Bayes' Theorem
> Let $A$ and $B$ be two events, each of positive probability. Then
> 
> $$
> P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)} P(B \mid A)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Proof
> We compute that
> 
> $$
> \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)} P(B \mid A) = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}\dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = P(A \mid B)
> $$
> 
> This gives the result. $\blacksquare$

### Two-Stage Systems

Standard applications of the multiplication formula, the law of total probabilities, and Bayes' theorem occur with **two­-stage systems**. The response for such systems can be thought of as occurring in two steps or stages. 

Typically, we are given the probabilities for the first stage and the conditional probabilities for the second stage. The multiplication formula is then used to calculate joint probabilities for what happens at both stages; the law of total probability is used to compute the probabilities for what happens at the second stage; and Bayes' theorem is used to calculate the conditional probabilities for the first stage, given what has occurred at the second stage.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose urn 1 has $3$ red and $2$ blue balls, and urn 2 has $4$ red and $7$ blue balls. Suppose that one of the two earns is selected with uniform probability, and then one of the balls within that urn is picked uniformly at random.
> 
> What is the probability that urn 2 is selected at the first stage (event $A$) and a blue ball is selected at the second stage (event $B$)? The **multiplication formula** provides the answer:
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) = \dfrac{1}{2} \dfrac{7}{11} = \dfrac{7}{22}
> $$
> 
> What is the probability that a blue ball is obtained? The **law of total probability** provides the answer:
> 
> $$
> P(B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) + P(A^c)P(B \mid A^c) = \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{7}{11} + \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{2}{5}
> $$
> 
> Now suppose we are given the information that the ball picked is blue. Then, using **Bayes' theorem,** the condition probability that we had selected urn 3 is given by
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(A \mid B) &= \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)} P(B \mid A) = \left(\dfrac{1/2}{(1/2)(2/5) + (1/2)(7/11)}\right) \dfrac{7}{11} \\
> &= 35/57 \approx 0.614
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Note that, without the information that a blue ball occurred at the second stage, we have that
> 
> $$
> P(\text{urn 2 selected}) = 1/2
> $$
> 
> We see that knowing the ball was blue significantly increase the probability that urn 2 was selected.

## Independence of Events

Consider the following example, where we roll one fair die and flip one fair coin so that

$$
S = \{1H, 2H, 3H, 4H, 5H, 6H, 1T, 2T\}
$$

and $P(\{s\}) = 1/12$ for each $s \in S$. We compute the probability that the die comes up $5$ as $P(\{5H, 5T\}) = 2/12 = 1/6$.

Now, what is the probability that the die comes up $5$, **conditional** on knowing that the coin came up tails. We compute this as

$$
\begin{align*}
P(\text{die = 5} \mid \text{coin = tails}) &= \dfrac{P(\text{die = 5 and coin = tails})}{P(\text{coin = tails})} \\
&= \dfrac{P(\{5T\})}{P(\{1T, 2T, 3T, 4T, 5T, 6T\})} \\
&=  \dfrac{1/12}{6/12} = 1/6
\end{align*}
$$

which is the same as the unconditional probability, $P(\text{die = 5})$. Knowing that the coin was tails had no effect on the probability that the coin came up $5$. This property is called **independence**.

> [!NOTE] Definition: Independence
> Two events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)
> $$

Because $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$, we see that $A$ and $B$ are independent if and only if $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ or $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$, provided that $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$. The previous definition has the advantage $P(B) = 0$ or $P(A) = 0$, respectively. Intuitively, events $A$ and $B$ are independent if neither one has any impact on the probability of the other.

> [!NOTE] Example
> In the previous example, if $A$ is the event that the die of $5$, and $B$ is the event that the coin was tails, then $P(A) = P(\{5H, 5T\}) = 2/12 = 1/6$, and 
> 
> $$
> P(B) = P(\{1T, 2T, 3T, 4T, 5T, 6T\}) = 6/12 = 1/2
> $$
> 
> Also, $P(A \cap B) = P(\{5T\}) = 1/12$, which is indeed each to $(1/6)(1/2)$. Hence $A$ and $B$ are independent in this case.

For multiple events, the definition of independence is somewhat more involved.

> [!NOTE] Definition
> A collection of events $A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots$ are **independent** if 
> 
> $$
> P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_j}) = P(A_{i_1}) \cdots P(A_{i_j})
> $$
> 
> for all finite subcollection $A_{i_1}, \ldots, A_{i_j}$ of distinct elements

> [!NOTE] Example
> According to the previous example, three $A$, $B$, and $C$ are independent if all of the following equations hold:
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(A \cap B) &= P(A)P(B) \\
> P(A \cap C) &= P(A)P(C) \\
> P(B \cap C) &= P(B)P(C) \\
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> and 
> 
> $$
> P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)
> $$
> 
> All of these must hold to verify independent. If the firs three hold but the last one does not, events $A$, $B$, and $C$ are **pairwise independent** but they are not independent.

# Continuity of $P$

Suppose $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ is a sequence of events that are getting "closer" (in some sense) to another event, $A$. Then, we might expect the probabilities $P(A_1), P(A_2), \ldots$ are getting close to $P(A)$ i.e. that $\lim_{n \to \infty}P(A_n) = P(A)$. How do we formalize this?

Properties like this, which say that $P(A_n)$ is close to $P(A)$ whenever $A_n$ is "close" to $A$, are **continuity properties**. The above question can thus be translated, roughly, as asking whether or not probability measures $P$ are "continuous". It turns out that $P$ is indeed continuous in some sense.

Specifically, let us write $\{A_n\} \nearrow A$ and say that the sequence $\{A_n\}$ increases to $A$, if $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq A_3 \subseteq \cdots$, and also $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A$. That is, the sequence of events is an **increasing sequence**, and furthermore its union is equal to $A$. For example, if $A_n = (1/n, n]$, then $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \cdots$ and $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = (0, \infty)$. Hence $\{(1/n, n]\} \nearrow (0, \infty)$. The below figure depicts an increasing sequence of subsets.

![[Second Year/STA257/Images/increasing sequence.png]]

Similarly, let us write $\{A_n\} \searrow A$ and say that the sequence $\{A_n\}$ decreases to $A$, if $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq A_3 \supseteq \cdots$, and also $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A$. That is, the sequence of events is a **decreasing sequence** , and furthermore its intersection is equal to $A$. For example, if $A = (-1/n, 1/n]$, then $A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$ and $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty A_n = \{0\}$. Hence, $\{(-1/n, 1/n]\} \searrow \{0\}$. The below figure depicts an decreasing sequence of subsets.

![[Second Year/STA257/Images/decreasing sequence.png]]

We will consider such sequences of sets at several points in the text. For this we need the following result.

> [!NOTE] Theorem
> Let $A, A_1, A_2, \ldots$ be events, and suppose that either $\{A_n\} \nearrow A$ or $\{A_n\} \searrow A$. Then
> 
> $$
> \lim_{n \to \infty}P(A_n) = P(A)
> $$

> [!NOTE] Example
> Suppose $S$ is the set of all positive integers, with $P(s) = 2^{-s}$ for all $s \in S$. Then what is $P(\{5, 6, 7, 8, \ldots\})$?
> 
> We begin by noting that the events $A_n = \{5, 6, 7, 8, \ldots, n\}$ increase to $A = \{5,6, 7, 8, \ldots\}$ since $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots$ and $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n = A$. Hence, using continuity of probabilities, we must have
> 
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> P(\{5, 6, 7, 8, \ldots\}) &= \lim_{n \to \infty}  P(\{5, 6, 7, 8, \ldots, n\}) \\
> &= \lim_{n \to \infty} [P(5) + P(6) + \cdots + P(n)] \\
> &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(2^{-5} + 2^{-6} + \cdots + 2^{-n}\right) \\
> &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( \dfrac{2^{-5} - 2^{-n-1}}{1 - 2^{-1}}\right) \\
> &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(2^{-4} - 2^{-n}\right) = 2^{-4} = 1/16
> \end{align*}
> $$
> 
> Alternatively, we could use countable additivity directly, to conclude that
> 
> $$
> P(\{5, 6, 7, 8, \ldots\}) = P(5) + P(6) + P(7) + \cdots,
> $$
> 
> which amounts to the same thing.

> [!NOTE] Example
> Let $P$ be some probability measure in the space $S = \mathbb{R}^1$. Suppose
> 
> $$
> P((3, 5 + 1/n)) \ge \delta
> $$
> 
> for all $n$, where $\delta > 0$. Let $A_n = (3, 5 + 1/n)$. Then $\{A_n\} \searrow A$ where $A = (3, 5]$. Hence, we must have $P(A) = P((3, 5]) \ge \delta$ as well.
> 
> Note, however, that we could still have $P((3, 5)) = 0$. For example, perhaps $P(\{5\}) = \delta$, but $P((3, 5)) = 0$.
