# Introduction

Linguistics is the scientific study of language. Linguistics is generally about speech or signs; reading and writing are often not considered part of linguistics proper.

Language at its core is a system of communication. It is composed of arbitrary signs/symbols with assigned meanings and is governed by certain "rules" or patterns on how to compose these symbols.

# Descriptivism and Prescriptivism

The **prescriptive approach to language** relate to prescribing how language should be or ought to be. These are often framed as directives or laws on how a language should be used.

The **descriptive approach to language** relate to descriptions and inferences about language based on systematic, empirical observations. These observations describe a language as it is actually used by language users (in context).

For example, we could **describe** English plurals this way:

> Adding -s to a noun allows it to refer to many of something, like apples, books, or shoes.

Or we could **prescribe** how you think people should plurals this way:

> Because the word virus is derived from Latin, you should pluralize it as viri, not viruses.

When talking about language, we should do so with a **descriptive** approach to language, not a **prescriptive** approach.

A descriptivist approach is more scientific, captures language as it changes, and mitigates some of the linguistic biases we may have against particular spellings, dialects, languages, etc..

# Fundamental Properties of Language

## Modality

Language is expressed through a **modality**. This is the physical implementation of language transmission. 

For example:

- **Oral-Aural**: using the mouth (larynx, tongue, teeth, lips, etc) to send a message and using the ears to receive it. This is the primary modality of spoken languages.
- **Manual-Visual**: using the fingers, hands, wrists, forearms to send a message and the eyes to receive it. This is the primary modality of signed languages.

## Arbitrariness

Language encompasses links between **signs/forms** and **meanings**.

The form is the "piece" of what language looks like (e.g. a word, a symbol, a prefix/suffix), whereas the meaning is notion of what the form refers to.

For the most part, **there is no inherent relationship between form and meaning**. Different languages will have very different words for the same concept.

It is the language users who agree that a form is assigned a certain meaning.

## Creativity

Creativity has to do with **productivity** (or **generativity**): Every language can use its finite set of vocabulary and its finite set of principles to produce an infinite number of (new) sentences as the need arises.

**Knowing a language means you can combine and apply a finite set of principles on a finite inventory of linguistic units in an infinite number of ways.**

### Systematicity

Although language is creative, there are constraints that establish boundaries within which forms are possible and which forms are impossible.

For example, the term PDF is an **initialism** (pronounced as letters) and not an **acronym** (pronounced as a word) due to certain systemic limits in English.

Also consider the possible English word **prasp** and the impossible English word **pwant**. Both these words don't exist, but only one could exist.

An asterisk denotes something as **ungrammatical**: unacceptable or unlikely forms in language. For example, **\*pwant**.

# Linguistic Competence and the Mental Grammar

While our knowledge of the lexicon may be conscious or explicit, we're less conscious about how we actually produce certain sounds.

When we know a language (fluently), a lot of grammatical knowledge is actually **implicit**; we call this implicit knowledge **linguistic competence**.

Linguistic competence is a person's **internal knowledge** of their language, giving them the ability to recognize certain forms as grammatical or ungrammatical, given rules and constraints

imposed by the **mental grammar**.

The **mental grammar** is the unconscious system that encompasses the forms and structures of a language. Grammar is the intricate network of knowledge that underlies our ability to use language.

All languages have a grammar; all these grammars are equal and alike. These grammars tend to change over time and is largely unconscious.

**Linguistic competence is the manifestation of mental grammar**. The inner workings of mental grammar can be observed and described in the form of speech that results from it (and how it is judged by users). 

For example, when Tracy tells us that "**She coffee drinks**" is an unacceptable sentence, this demonstrates that Tracy has knowledge of what word order is grammatical in English, even if Tracy cannot explain the syntactic rules involved.

## Components of Grammar

Below are the components of grammar.

- **Phonetics**: articulation and perception of sounds
- **Phonology**: patterning of sounds
- **Morphology**: word formation
- **Syntax**: phrase and sentence formation
- **Semantics**: meanings of words and sentences in **context**

There is also the **mental lexicon**, which is a speaker's mental dictionary. it contains all information about words.

## Determining the Structure of Grammar

We look at grammatical and ungrammatical structures.

- **Grammatical**: forms that **can** be generated by the mental grammar; **acceptable** forms for (native) speakers; we call these "well-formed" words or sentences.
- **Ungrammatical**: forms that **cannot** be generated by the mental grammar; **unacceptable** forms for (native) speakers; we mark these with an asterisk.

Grammaticality is about whether or not a form **could** exist; it does not guarantee alone that a word/sentence will make sense. For example, the sentence "colourless green ideas sleep furiously" is grammatical, but is semantically nonsensical.

### Grammaticality as a Gradient

However, note that grammaticality is a **gradient** matter. A word or sentence may be evaluated as more or less grammatical. 

Furthermore, there are a number of external factors that may influence whether a certain form is judged as grammatical or not. For example: people who are far more accepting of gender diversity are more likely to judge the use of **singular they** as grammatical.

## Writing

Writing is **not** a (manual-visual) modality of human language.

Writing is **secondary**: it is the written representation of a primary modality (e.g. Hangul is the representation of spoken Korean). Writing must be consciously learned through formal instruction, whereas other modalities are acquired naturalistically.

Writing is not a primary part of the mental grammar: Language users don't need to know how to write (or read) in order to have a mental grammar; many languages don't have a writing system.

# Studying Language

## Empirical Observations

Linguists observe phenomena and try to come up with generalizations.

We can study components of language via data collected from:

- Grammaticality of acceptability judgements
- Surveys
- Interviews
- Corpora
- Experiments

# Applications of Linguistics

## Harms of Linguistics

The field of linguistics has done and continues to do harm:

- Spread of colonial languages has led to the loss of Indigenous languages
- Spoken language is often viewed as more important than signed language (this is called **oralism**).
- Linguistic behaviour that deviates from the "norm" are considered deficient.

## Doing Good with Linguistics

Linguistics can be applied across a broad range of fields:

- **Speech language pathology**: identifying and treating communication disorders.
- **Tech**: speech synthesis and recognition, text classification, automated translation, LLMs and natural language processing, etc
- **Education**: foreign language teaching, language planning, literacy.
- **Law**: writing and interpreting contracts and policies, foreign linguistics.
- **Entertainment**: dialect coaching, constructing languages for film and TV.
