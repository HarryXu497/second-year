# Syntax

**Syntax** is the study of **word order**, **sentence structure**, and the **relations** between words and phrases in a sentence.

Linguists working on syntax are interested in discovering the **structures** and **form** of **grammatical sentences**.

## Grammaticality Judgements

The notion of **grammaticality** is crucial for syntax. Speakers' **grammaticality judgements** offer insights on possible sentences in a language. 

Consider the following "wh-words" in questions in English, Polish, and Western Naskapi:

1. **English**. "Who lost what?" vs "\*Who what lost?"
2. **Polish**. "\*Who lost what?" vs "Who what lost?"
3. **Western Naskapi**. "Who lost what?" vs "Who what lost?"

## Words

In general, syntax focuses on the structures of complete sentences. Sentences are formed from **combinations/sequences of words** that that act as a unit, called **constituents**.

Thus, **words** are often the starting point for analysis in syntax. The grammar takes individual words and combines them to form phrases and sentences.

Thus, we should know something about categorizing words before we start.

# Syntactic Categories

We already say some **syntactic categories** in the previous chapter: **Noun**, **Verb**, **Adjective**, **Adverb**. These are **lexical categories** that carry descriptive meanings.

We will add the following: **Preposition**, **Auxilary**, **Determiner**, **Degree**. These are **functional categories** that express grammatical relations.

Linguists rely on **what kind of morphology they can take** (i.e. what kind of affixes they can combine with) and **where they are allowed to appear in sentences** (i.e. what kind of words can occupy a particular slot)?

Words that have the **same (positional) distribution** are part of the same syntactic category.

## Nouns (N)

Nouns have the following properties.

1. **Morphological properties**
	1. Can take inflectional affixes: plural -s (e.g. bottles); possessive -'s (e.g. bird's nest).
2. **Syntactic distribution**
	1. Can occur after **determiners** (e.g. **a** or **the**); an **adjective** can optionally precede it.

## Verbs (V)

Verbs have the following properties.

1. **Morphological properties**
	1. Often marked for **tense** (3-person singular present tense -s; past tense -ed) and **aspect** (progressive aspect -ing; perfective aspect -ed/-en).
2. **Syntactic distribution**
	1. Can occur after **infinitive to** or **auxiliary** (will/would/…).

## Adjectives (Adj)

Adjectives have the following properties.

1. **Morphological properties**
	1. Can take the suffix -ish; comparative form -er; or superlative -est.
	2. Can take the suffix -ly to form an adverb.
2. **Syntactic distribution**
	1. Can occur between the **determiner** (the, a) and **noun**; or after the verb **be** (a copula).

## Adverbs (Adv)

Adverbs have the following properties.

1. **Morphological properties**
	1. Can end with a -ly suffix (Adj + ly → Adv)
2. **Syntactic distribution**
	1. Can before or after **verbs**

## Determiners (Det)

Determiners introduce and narrow down reference by providing information on definiteness, specificity, and/or quantity: **the**, **a**, **this**, **that**, **these**, **those**, **every**, **some**, **each**, **most**, etc

> [!TIP] Remark
> **Pronouns** that mark **possession** (my, your, his, her, its, our) may sometimes be grouped under determiners.

Determiners can occur before nouns (not verbs) or before optional adjectives + noun. For example

- **A** cat
- **An** orange
- **A** big cat

## Prepositions (P)

Prepositions indicate spatial, temporal, logical, relations, or grammatical relations: **on**, **up**, **beside**, **through**, **outside**, **in**, **above**, **to**, **of**, **with**, **for**, **without**.

Prepositions are almost always followed by nouns (e.g. the man with the hat). They can also be modified by **prepositional qualifier (PQ)** words that appear before the preposition (to help qualify the relation): **right**, **deep**, **far**, **almost**, **way**, **just**, **straight**, etc. For example, she is **right** under the tree.

## Auxiliaries (Aux)

Auxiliaries are verbs that add functional/grammatical meaning (tense, aspect, modality, etc) to the content of the **main verb** that co-occurs with it.

For example, **have**, **be**, **do**, **will**, **would**, **can**, **could**, **may**, **might**, **must**, **should**, **shall**. Note that have, be, and do can also be used as main verbs.

**Morphologically**, they can be inflected for tense (present or past): will/would; can/could; etc

**Syntactically**, they always occur with a main verb; and can be a response (when the main verb is implied). For example, "you might like this album" and "I might".

## Degree Words (Deg)

Degree words modify the degree of adjectives and adverbs: **so**, **too**, **quite**, **more**, **very**, **extremely**, **way**.

**Syntactically**, they can occur before adjectives and adverbs. For example, The cat is **very** cute; The dog ate **way** quickly.

## Belonging to Multiple Categories

Some words belong to more than one syntactic categories; we use **linguistic context** to determine what their category is.

For example,

> If the brief (N), you write is too brief (A), I will brief (V) the investors myself

Also note that what characterizes a syntactic category (i.e. their morphological and syntactic distributions) will not necessarily characterize the same category in other languages. 

For example, nouns may be inflected for number (singular/plural) in some languages (e.g. English) but not in others (eg. Karitiana)

The tests we use to determine the syntactic category of a word are not always definitive, and **they will certainly not work across different languages**.

# Phrases, Constituents, and Constituency Tests

## Phrases

We intuitively have a sense of grouping words in sentences which seem to belong together more closely than other strings of words.

For example, the sentence "the diligent author wrote the story in a book chapter" can be split as

![[phrases 1.png]]

A **(syntactic) phrase** is a set of words that act as a unit. This unit (known as a **constituent**) is something that speakers can manipulate as a single chunk; it has its own independent existence separate from everything else.

## Phrase Structure

Phrases are specific types of constituents consisting of an obligatory **head** and optional elements (modifiers)

For example,

1. **Noun phrase (NP)**
	- Basic phrase structure: (Det) + (AP) + N + (PP)
	- Example: (The) (big) bear (in the forest)
2. **Verb phrase (VP)**
	- Basic phrase structure: (Adv) + V + (NP)
	- Example: (always) bake (the pies)
3. Adjective Phrase (AP)
	- Basic phrase structure: (Deg) + A + (PP)
	- Example: (very) easy (for a student)
4. Prepositional Phrase (PP)
	- Basic phrase structure: (PQ) + P + (NP)
	- Example: (right) outside (the building)
5. Adverb Phrase (AdvP)
	- Basic phrase structure: (Deg) + Adv + (PP)
	- Example: (very) quickly (for a sloth)

The head is the most important element in the sense that it determines the category of the phrase. Note that a phrase can consist of the head alone (e.g. bear, bake).

## Constituency Tests

By identifying a part of a sentence is a phrase, we are claiming that it is a **constituent**. We rely on **constituency tests** and grammatically judgements based on the results of the tests.

We will cover two tests here: **replacement/substitution** and **clefting**. Note that no single test is sufficient to justify constituency, and not all tests will work for all constituency types.

### Substitution/Replacement

If a string of words can be substituted by a single word (e.g. **pro-form**) or pro-verb (e.g. **do so** or **do too**) or a **clear adjective**, and the resulting sentence is grammatical, then the string is a constituent.

For example, 

1. The teacher is happy
	- \[The teacher\] is happy → \[She\] is happy; "The teacher" is a phrase since replacing it yields a grammatical sentence.
	- \[The teacher is\] happy → \*\[She\] happy; "The teacher is" is not a phrase since replacing it yields a ungrammatical sentence.
2. Jake runs in the park
	- Jake \[runs\] in the park → Jake \[does so\] in the park; "runs" is a phrase since replacing it yields a grammatical sentence.
	- Jake \[runs in\] the park → \*Jake \[does so\] the park; "runs in" is not a phrase since replacing it yields a grammatical sentence.

### Clefting

Here, we "cleave" the original sentence into two clauses. The formula for **it-clefts** is

> It is/was \[string being tested\] that/wh-word \[rest of sentence\]

For example

> Lory mends \[broken parts\].
> It is \[broken parts\] that Lory mends

versus

> Lory \[mends broken\] parts
> \*It is \[mends broken\] that Lory _ parts

## Notes

Some notes:

- NPs can be replaced by pronouns.
- VPs can b replaced by **do so** or **do too**.
- Some PPs can be replaced by then or there.
- APs can be replaced with something that you known to be an adjective.
- To cleft a VP, you need to put a present/past tense of do in the original position occupied by the VP. Clefting a VP doesn't always sound totally natural in English but most people find it better than clefting a string that is not a constituent.
	- For example, the student \[saw a movie after class\] → It was \[see a movie after class\] that the student did.

# Basic Word Order

A **sentence** is a string of words that expresses a complete thought, question, statement, comment, etc.

A **clause** is a constituent that include a **subject** and a **predicate**. A sentence is an example of a a clause.

A predicate is the state, event, or activity that the sentence attributes to its subject. A predicate can have a number of arguments (subjects and objects): **intransitive** (the subject but no object), **transitive** (the subject and one **direct object**), or **ditransitive** (the subject and two objects: direct and **indirect objects**).

English has the basic word order **Subject-Verb-Object** (**SVO**). Some examples of languages with other orders are:

1. SOV: Japanese, Basque, Turkish
2. SVO: English, mandarin
3. VSO: Gaelic, Arabic, Tagalog
4. VOS: Malagasy, Fijian
5. OVS: Tualuan, Urarina
6. OSV: Kxoe, Tobati, Nadëb

# Semantics

Semantics deals with the study of the **meaning** of words and sentences

**Lexical semantics** focuses on the sense and denotations of words and the relations among words. We will focus on this subject for this course.

**Sentential semantics** (or propositional semantics) focus on how words combine to form the meaning of sentences; understanding the meanings of sentences (based on their truth values); and the relations along sentences.

## Intension and Extension

Linguistic expressions have their **intension** and **extension**.

The **intension** is the specific concept that the expression evokes, whereas the **extension** is the set of entities that the expression corresponds to in the real worlds (i.e. its **referents**)

For example, the expression "professor" has the intension "a person who teaches at a university" and the extension Prof. Sha, Prof Khesin, ...

## Synonymy

Synonyms are words/phrases that have the same meaning in some contexts (e.g. big and large).

## Antonymy

Antonyms are words/phrases that have opposing meaning with respect to some aspect (e.g. hot and cold).

## Polysemy

**Polysemous** words occur when one word has multiple, **related** meanings

For example, branch has the meanings

1. A bird landed on a tree branch (part of a tree).
2. Our company is opening a new branch (an offshoot division).

## Homonyms

**Homonyms** are words that share the **same pronunciation and spelling**  but have unrelated meanings (e.g. the animal seal and the seal on a har.)

## Homophony

**Homophones** are words that have the **same pronunciation** (and often differ in spelling) by differ in meaning (e.g. bear and bare).

# Ambiguity

## Lexical Ambiguity

**Polysemous words and homonyms** are susceptible to **lexical ambiguity**. This can be resolved though contextual clues, common sense, or further clarifications.

We can also add other words/phrases to disambiguate between the two interpretations. For example, "I found a bat in the attic" → "I found a bat in the attic and it was sleeping".

We can use constituency tests to disambiguate between the interpretation. For example, consider

> They saw the child with the binoculars.

There are two interpretations, which we distinguish using constituency tests:

1. They saw \[the child\] with the binoculars.
	1. They saw \[him\] with the binoculars.
	2. It was \[the child\] that they saw with the binoculars.
2. They saw \[the child with the binoculars\].
	1. They saw \[him\].
	2. It was \[the child with the binoculars\] that they saw.
