# Language and Society

Language is a social phenomenon and part of a larger system of social and cultural practice. There are many social components of language that must be studied in linguistics.

Linguistics considers the impact of language use in different social contexts and how it intersects with **social structures**. A social structure is a system, institution, or organized pattern of relationships within a social e.g. families, friends, government, education system, etc. Each such system has its **norms** and **hierarchies**, and individuals have their respective social "roles".

# Unjust Social Structures

In the real world, there are **unjust social structures**: patterns, systems, institutions, policies, etc in societies that create, maintain, and perpetuate inequality and power imbalance.

The presence of unjust social structures limits people's access to the full extent of privileges within their societies by **arbitrary factors beyond their control**.

In other words: unjust social structures make life easier for some groups and harder for others, often without people noticing.

For example, consider **educational systems**. These usually have a standardized language in which the school teaches; those who already use this language are advantaged and those who do not are disadvantaged.

Also, people from a **particular radicalized group** typically only have access to a subset of the full range of educational opportunities.

## Social Justice

**Social justice** is the **identification** of these unjust social structures and their underlying causes, as well as the **activism** involved in dismantling them. 

Effective activism encompasses two broad approaches: 

1. **Bottom-up**: expanding our own individual awareness and education.
2. **Top-down**: political acts, legislature

# Language is Performative

Language is **performative**: the ways we use and perceive language have real-world consequences. **We can accomplish social things with language**, and in turn, language can change the world.

**Performative speech acts** are utterances that not only convey information (e.g., describing something) but when uttered in the appropriate context, also perform an action that changes social reality. For example, promises (e.g. "I promise to be early"), apologies (e.g. "I'm sorry"), declarations (e.g "you're fired"). Uttering a promise shifts you from a state of non-obligation to a state of obligation.

# Language to Express Identity

Language can also express all aspects of identities. From this perspective, all language is performative and conveys part of your identity.

Language allows us to enact changes or to convey identities.

## Individual and Social Impacts

If language can be used to perform actions and identities, then language has power to do both good and harm. 

The impact of language can be viewed from both the **individual** level and the **social group** level.

Often, prejudice towards an individual can be linked (or can reflect) prejudice towards a larger, marginalized group.

## Names

Names in particular often carry certain expectations and assumptions. These inferences tend to be shaped by our lived experiences against the backdrop of social structures of power and privilege.

Personal names interact with unjust social structure:

- Employers and landlords tend to pick English-sounding names
- Indigenous peoples in Canada were coerced to used English names
- **Indexical bleaching**: replacing someone's name with a more English/Anglo-sounding name; or (mis)pronouncing it to sound more English/Anglo.
- **Deadnaming**: using former names for transgender individuals

Our names are not just **referential**. They are tied to other aspects, including a heritage language, culture, and community.

Out names impact how we navigate social structures e.g. many racialized individuals still use "English" names to access resources.

## Pronouns

In many cultures, there's a general expectation that we use appropriately-

gendered pronouns when referring to people. **Misgendering** someone with the wrong pronouns is generally considered bad in social contexts.

There is an **ongoing change** where singular they/them pronouns are becoming more and more popular, in particular with nonbinary and gender-diverse folks. Use of the singular they/them also varies with the age and gender of the user. Those who use singular they/them more are generally more accepting and familiar with gender diversity.

The use of singular they/them in English is a deliberate change that we can make to lessen the likelihood that non-binary people will be misgendered. This leads to a change in language because the users change the way they use it.

## Communication Styles

Certain communication styles like

- **Vocal fry**: a deeper, creaky voice that is often perceived as "bored" or "relaxed"
- **Uptalk**: a style of speech wherein declarative sentences end with rising intonations; perceived as "uncertain".

are more frequently associated with women despite research suggesting men engage in these styles at similar rates.

Some communication styles are pathologized (e.g. silence).

# Socially-Offensive Behaviours

## Taboos

Taboos are utterances that cause offence when otherwise uttered. Taboos are culturally and contextually avoided in a variety of ways. Some **taboo avoidance strategies** include replacing the taboo with another expression (e.g. "shoot" instead of "shit") or using a more complex circumlocution (e.g. "the four-letter word referring to excrement").

## Derogatory and Pejorative Expressions

Derogatory and pejorative expressions are expressions that disparage people, often involving attitudes of the utterer towards the other person.

Derogatory terms are intended to condemn or degrade individuals. For example, jerk, idiot, etc

## Slurs

Derogatory words (or expressions) that denigrate specific marginalized groups of people.

The great emotional weight of slurs arises from the power differential between the person using the slur and the person targeted by it. They can have physiological and/or mental impacts on the targeted individuals.

# Language, Ideology, and Standards

**Language ideologies** are attitudes, opinions, expectations, and systems of belief about languages and language varieties, which often apply to the users of the language. For example, "some languages aren't good enough"

Many circulating language ideologies often come from those in power; and reflect and reinforce dominant social structures and power dynamics.

## Linguistic Biases

Language ideologies can lead to **linguistic bias**. That is, the unspoken beliefs and assumptions can influence our evaluations of language and its speakers.

Canadians and American tends to perceive all varieties of British English as having high prestige, so speakers of British English are perceived as "better educated and smarter".

Linguistic biases reinforce social inequalities.

## Languages and Dialects

Languages are often difficult to count because there's no clear criteria for what counts as a language versus a dialect.

In practice, the act of naming a "language" versus "dialect" is both a **linguistic** and **socio-political endeavour**

Linguists often rely on **mutual intelligibility**: can speakers of two codes understand each other readily? If they do, then the codes are dialects of the same language.

Mutual intelligibility as a linguistic factor/measure runs into complications: what if there's a lot of borrowings from another language? What if native speakers sort of understand each other, but not entirely?

**Socio-political factors** are arguably more important, and can often override mutual intelligibility. For example, Hindi and Urdu or Bosnian, Serbian, and Croatian. These languages are mutually intelligible but considered separate languages.

**Socio-political factors "override" mutual intelligibility**: languages are used to create a sense of distinct national identities and reinforce political borders.

The opposite way works too. Mandarin, Cantonese, and other Sinitic languages are mutually unintelligible but are considered dialects of the same language. Socio-political factors "override" mutual unintelligibility: languages can be used to create a sense of unified national and cultural identity.

The dividing line between what counts as a dialect and what counts as a

language is somewhat linguistically arbitrary, but that line is drawn by those who have the most **power**. To avoid imposing judgments, we adopt more value-neutral terms: **code** or **variety**.

## Standardized Language

The standardized language is the variety associated with power e.g. used by the social elites, used in the capital city, the medium of instruction in educational institutions, etc.

A "standard" language is a social construct: there is nothing in the mental

grammar that dictates what counts as standard. External forces may define the standards in ways to benefit the social elites (e.g. Académie Française).

There is often a bias toward and priority on the standard. For example, tech tools like Alexa or Siri often have a hard time accommodating nonstandard forms.

Switching to the standard brings a lot of advantages and access to more

resources, benefits, opportunities (and at times, safety and security). For example, poor young racialized men in Brazil (pavelas) switch and use standard language when talking to police officers in order to position themselves as intellectual and defend their civil rights (Roth-Gordon 2016).

## Nonstandard Language

Nonstandard language is the variety associated with less power and often stigmatized. These languages are often associated with marginalized groups (e.g. Black English, Southern US English).

They may not even be perceived as unique systems or ways of speaking; instead, they are seen as deficient or imperfect forms of the standard.

# Linguistic Discrimination

Some people cannot readily **switch** to the standard language. There are many situations wherein speakers of nonstandard varieties (e.g. with nonstandard accents) experience discrimination.

For example:

1. **Law**: people with certain accents may have their credibility quested in court and their testimonies are transcribed less accurately.
2. **Education**: Kreyòl-speaking children in Haiti (> 90%) are taught in French at schools. Children whose accents are not mainstream are disproportionately misdiagnosed with learning disabilities.
3. **Employment**: people who speak nonstandard varieties have lower chances in getting job interviews and receive lower recognition of skills
4. **Housing**: people who speak nonstandard varieties have a harder time accessing rental units and renting.

## Case Study: Baugh 2003

Consider John Baugh's (2003) **matched-guise** study. A **matched-guise** study is a study where one speaker assumes multiple guises/voices/personas.

Baugh called San Francisco landlords asking about the availability of an apartment for which he had seen an apartment. He did so in three different ways: African-American, Latino, and the "standard" (i..e White) way. 

> "…when he spoke with an African-American or Latino accent, his calls routinely were not returned, or he was told apartments or houses were no longer available. When he telephoned using what he calls professional standard English, Baugh generally was invited to see the properties” 

The African-American and Latino guises where subject to **linguistic profiling**, where listeners identified social characteristics based on linguistic cues. In this study, the landlords made **assumptions** about the speaker's racial/ethnic/socioeconomic background. These assumptions were also ideologically linked to expectations and stereotypes about racialized communities.

## Solutions

Expecting people to change their accents is unrealistic and discriminatory. Accents in a second language are shaped by the first-language sound system and are hard to change. Accents are also part of one's identity.

Research has shown that difficulties are often not due to the accept itself, but the listener's lack of exposure to certain ways of speakers.

Psycholinguistic research shows us that it’s much easier to change your
comprehension of unfamiliar accents. This is called **perceptual adaptation**: the more experience we have paying attention to someone, the better we understand them.

We can resist the narrative that pressures everyone to conform to some **arbitrary** standard accent: by taking a descriptive approach to language, by gaining metalinguistic awareness and interrogating our own linguistic biases and engaging in more linguistics education.

# Language Policies

Governments and institutions use language to create unity in some cases and division in others. They wield power to enact these through **language policies**. For example, Canada's *Official Languages Act* (1969) in response to francophone nationalism.

These policies can be used for good e.g. to promote linguistic diversity or for bad e.g. residential schools using English/French as the medium of instruction.

The *Indigenous Languages Act* (2019) provides legal protection to Indigenous languages, including funding for reclamation and revitalization
