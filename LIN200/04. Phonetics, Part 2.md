# Preface

Vowels are often trickier than consonants for many reasons:

1. Vowel quality exists on a **continuum** (vs. discrete qualities for consonants). Unlike a consonant, a slight change in articulation makes little noticeable effect in what a vowel sounds like.
2. Vowels vary a lot between different varieties of English
3. English has a larger inventory of vowels compared to most spoken languages, and conventions differ

In this course, we will use consistent conventions.

# Describing Vowels

![[vowels diagram.png]]

Vowel quality is described according to the following **articulatory parameters**, based on the configuration of the tongue and lips: **height**, **backness**, **rounding**, and **tenseness**.

For example, we may describe \[ʌ\] to be a **mid central unrounded lax vowel**.

All vowels are **voiced** by default. All vowels are made with **minimal obstruction** of the oral cavity.

We tend not to use midsagittal diagrams for vowels since it can be hard to distinguish vowels from them

## Height

**Height** refers to the vertical position of the tongue. Tongue height is facilitated by jaw movement.

We have the following:

- **high**: tongue is high; opening is slightly larger than for approximants.
- **low**: tongue is in a low position; has the largest jaw opening.
- **mid**: intermediate tongue height position.

![[height diagram.png]]

## Backness

**Backness** refers to the horizontal position of the tongue.

- **Front**: tongue is in the front of the oral cavity; highest point of tongue is under the front end of palate.
- **Back**: tongue is farther back of the oral cavity; highest point of the tongue is under the backend of the palate
- **Central**: tongue is in the center of the oral cavity; highest point of the tongue is under the centre of palate.

![[backness.png]]

## Rounding

**Rounding** refers to the shape of the lips

- Rounded: corners of the mouth are pulled together in a circular shape
- Unrounded: corners of the mouth are pulled apart and spread like a smile; lips do not form a circular shape. In English, the rounded vowels are only \[u, ʊ, o, ɔ\].

![[rounding.png]]

## Tenseness

**Tenseness** refers to how advanced the tongue root is, influencing the **rigidity** of the tongue overall

- **Tense**: Tongue root is **advanced forward**, which compresses the tongue, resulting in sender, firmer tongue.
- **Lax**: Tongue root is **retracted**, which expands the tongue, resulting in more relaxed tongue.

![[tenseness.png]]

Tenseness interacts with height and backness:

- Tense vowels are higher and very peripheral (either very front or very back)
- Lax vowels are slightly lower and more centralized.

Mid central vowels are generally lax.

**Low vowels** are rarely tense in any language: jaw lowering and tongue root advancement make contradictory demands on the tongue. We treat them as lax, by default.

## Nasality

Nasality refers to the position of the velum as either lowered or raised (similar to oral stops vs. nasals).

- **Oral**: the velum is raised; airflow into the nasal cavity is blocked.
- **Nasal**: the velum is lowered; airflow into the nasal cavity is allowed.

We represent nasality with the ~ (tilde) diacritic over the IPA segment.

English doesn't contrast nasal vs. oral vowels, so this is not a primary part of the articulatory description of vowels. Languages like French do make a distinction though.

That said, vowels in most dialects of English are nasalized when they are immediately before a nasal stop (e.g. bent \[bɛ̃nt\]).

# Diphthongs

So far, we've looked at **monophthongs**: vowels with a single, table, vowel quality.

On the other hands, **diphthongs** are dynamic sounds. They start in one area of the vowel space and end up somewhere else at the end of the articulation, much like affricates.

There are three major **diphthongs**: \[aɪ\], \[aʊ\], \[ɔɪ\]:

- \[aɪ]: guy, sigh, license, alive
- \[aʊ\]: loud, proud, cow, outside
- \[ɔɪ\]: boy, oyster, soil, noisy

In Canadian English: diphthongs are treated as one segment (but they don't have a tie-bar diacritic like affricates do).

## \[e\] and \[o\]

Canadian English speakers have a tendency to produce mid-tense monophthongs \[e\] and \[o\] as diphthongs.

Consider

- \[e\] in gate vs \[ɛ\] in get: \[e\] is **tense** but speakers tend to move the tongue at the end (and sounds like \[geɪt\]).
- \[o\] in show and toe: at the end of the \[o\], the tongue moves up a little bit so we produce the vowel like \[oʊ\].

Thus, while \[e\] and \[o\] are monophthongs, they are often produced with a slight diphthongal quality (there is very minor change in tongue trajectory). For this reason, they are called **minor diphthongs**.

# Vowel Charts

Vowel charts look a lot like the a diagram of the mouth from the side.

Our jaws are **hinged**: as the jaw moves down (for a low vowel), the jaw also swings backward, carrying the tongue along with it. Consequently, the tongue moves backwards (its centre position also moves backward).

This results in a **skewed** shape (asymmetric quadrangle); the missing corner is in the front represents the space where humans cannot produce a vowel.

We refer to the physical extent of vowel production as the **vowel space**: the space in the oral cavity within which the vowels are produced.

# Vowel Classes

We can group vowels according to certain **classes**, based on their characteristics. For example,

- Front vowels: \[i, ɪ, e, ɛ, æ\]
- Mid vowels: \[e, ɛ, ə, ʌ, ɔ, o\]
- High vowels: \[i, ɪ, ʊ, u\]

# Vowel Length

**Length** refers to the duration of segments. In IPA, vowel length is often represented by the symbol \[ː\] following the vowel symbol. A vowel that has this symbol is described as a "long vowel".

Length distinctions can sometimes entail **semantic differences**.

Consider the vowel length differences: in Hawaiian, we have

- 'eha \[ʔeha\] 'sore/hurt'
- 'ehā \[ʔehaː\] 'four'

# Consonant Length

Consonants can be lengthened as well. Long consonants are called **geminates** while short consonants are called singletons.

Just like vowels, consonant length can signal semantic differences. In Italian, we have:

- fato \[fatɔ\] 'fate'
- fatto \[fatːɔ\] 'fact'

# Notable Patterns

1. \[ə\] and \[ʌ\] sound similar, but
	- \[ʌ\] is for stressed syllables (e.g., but \[bʌt\], cutter \[kʌtəɹ\]).
	- while \[ə\] is for unstressed syllables (e.g., upon \[əpɑn\], adjust \[ədʒʌst\], collect \[kəlɛkt\]).
2. \[ə\] is also used for vowel-r words (e.g., father \[fɑðəɹ\], first \[fəɹst\], third \[θəɹd\])
3. \[a\] is for \[ɑ/ɒ\]-like sounds that come immediately before \[ɹ\], as in start \[staɹt\].
4. \[ɔ\] is for \[o\]-like sounds that come immediately before \[ɹ\], as in board \[bɔɹd\].
5. Tense vowels tend to be inherently longer than lax vowels (e.g. seat \[sit\] versus sit \[sɪt\]).
6. Lax vowels in English almost never appear at the ends of words.
7. In English, most of the back vowels are rounded, and all the rest are unrounded.

# Common Articulatory Processes

**Assimilation** is a sound taking on some or all of the phonetic properties of a sound next to. It is especially prominent in casual, rapid speech. This can be related to **voicing**, **place**, or **manner**:

1. **Voicing assimilation**: a segment's voicing quality may be affected by the voicing quality of the immediately preceding or following sound.
	- For example, the plural -s marker in English: cats \[kæts\] versus dogs \[dɑgz\].
	- For example, c**l**ay \[kl̥e\]; t**r**y \[tɹ̥aɪ\]; **liquids are devoiced when preceded by voiceless consonants**
2. **Place assimilation**: a segment's place feature may be affected by the place feature of an immediately preceding or following sound.
	- For example, **pancake** \[pænkek\] ⟶ \[pæŋkek\]
3. **Manner assimilation**: a segment's manner feature may be affected by the manner feature of an immediately preceding or following sound. This is much less common than the previous two patterns.
	- For example, **good night** \[gʊd naɪt\] ⟶ \[gʊn naɪt\]

**Vowel reduction** is when vowels in unstressed syllables get reduced to a \[ə\], especially in rapid speech. For example, consider **today** \[tude\] ⟶ \[təde\].

**Deletion** is when segments are deleted in certain environments or conditions, especially in rapid speech. For example, consider **surprise** \[səɹpɹ̥aɪz\] ⟶ \[səpɹ̥aɪz\].

**Epenthesis** is when an **extra segment** (often a stop/plosive), usually between a **nasal** and **fricative**. For example, consider **something** \[sʌmθɪŋ\] ⟶ \[sʌm**p**θɪŋ\] or **hamster** \[hæmstəɹ\] ⟶ \[hæm**p**stəɹ\].

**Metathesis** is when a sequence of segments are reordered. For example, consider **prescribe** \[pɹəskɹaɪb\] ⟶ \[pəɹskɹaɪb\] or cavalry \[kævəlɹi\] ⟶ \[kælvəɹi\]

# Suprasegmental Features

**Suprasegmental** properties are features **beyond** the segment.

## Pitch

**Pitch** is an **auditory, perceptual property** observed on a scale of being relatively "lower" or "higher" tone.

- Perception of **higher pitch** happens when there's more tensing of vocal folds (faster vocal fold vibration) and greater air pressure.
- Perception of **lower pitch** happens when there's less tensing vocal folds (slower vocal fold vibration) and lower air pressure.

Pitch differences and changes are relevant linguistically as either

- **Tone**: pitch manipulated at the level of a syllable (one “beat” of sound)
- **Intonation**: pitch manipulated at the level of entire phrases or sentences.

### Tone

Tone refers to pitch level or pitch movement which distinguishes word meanings.

There are two broad classes/categories of tone: **level/register** and **contour tones**. Register tones are distinguished by a steady relative pitch across a syllable. Contour tones are distinguished by a moving or gliding pitch across a syllable

**Association lines** are lines that link tones to the "nucleus" of the beat of the sound (e.g. the **vowel**).

![[tones.png]]

![[tone.png]]

Mandarin is an example of a tonal language. Consider

- H(igh) 八 \[pá\] ‘eight’
- R(ising) 拔 \[pǎ\] ‘to pull’
- L(ow) 把 \[pà\] ‘to hold’
- F(alling) 罢 \[pâ\] ‘to stop’

> [!TIP] Remark
> Note that the tone symbols above in Mandarin are **IPA diacritics**. In Mandarin pinyin/orthography, the tones are represented differently.

### Intonation

**Intonation** refers to pitch changes at the level of phrases and utterances.

Intonation conveys broadly meaningful, functional, discourse-level information (rather than semantic difference between words).

For example, in English, a question will tend have a rising intonation, whereas a statement will have a falling intonation.

![[intonation.png]]

In fact, most languages will have questions with rising intonation and declarative sentences with falling intonation. The opposite is very rare (consider **uptalk** for example).

## Stress

**Stress** refers to the perceived prominence (or loudness) of **syllables** (the "beats"). Stressed syllables are often **perceived** as having longer **vowel** duration and **higher** pitch compared to unstressed syllables.

**Primary stress** is the sound or syllable that is the **most prominent**. It is represented by a \[ˈ\] before the syllable that receives primary stress.

**Secondary stress** is the sound or syllable, especially in longer words, that has **less prominence**. It is represented by \[ˌ\] before the syllable that receives secondary stress.

Consider the following, where the \[.\] separates each syllable.

- **Laboratory** can have two stress patterns: \[ˈlæ.bə.ɹə.ˌtɔ.ɹi\] or \[lə.ˈbɔ.rə.ˌtɔ.ɹi\]
- **Lavatory** can have two stress patterns: \[ˈlæ.və.ˌtɹi\] or \[ˈlæ.və.ˌtɔ.ɹi\]
- **Metropolitan** can have two stress patterns: \[ˌmɛ.tɹə.ˈpɑ.lɪ.tən\] or \[ˈmɛ.trə.ˌpɑ.lɪ.tən\]

Stress placement may signal a difference in meaning. In English, consider

- **Present**: \[ˈpɹε.zεnt\] vs \[pɹə.ˈzεnt\]
- **Object**: \[ˈɑb.ʤεkt\] vs \[əb.ˈʤεkt\]

# Practical Applications

## Dialect Coaching

**Dialect coaching** is a form of voice training to help develop the ability to perform in dialects other than one's own. There is often a focus on **pronunciation** and approximating the "accent".

Dialect coaches often work with actors, singers, broadcasters, lawyers, etc to make their speech more **natural-sounding** and **authentic**.

A typical curriculum would include

1. Learning the general principles of speech production: identifying the (i) articulators and configurations of segments and (ii) suprasegmental properties.
2. Learning the pronunciation of individual segments in the dialect.

**Learning can be superficial** though. It is difficult to acquire subtle, distributional properties, such as the social contexts that favour one form over another.

Note that dialect coaching is different from **dialect reduction**. Dialect reduction (or accent reduction) is an approach towards “standardizing” one’s pronunciation towards some perceived prestige variety in a society. This is rooted in Anglocentrism and prescriptivism; and perpetuate biases and prejudices
