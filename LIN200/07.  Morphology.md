# Introduction

Morphology is the study of how words are put together. Some questions we pursue are

1. What does a "word" look like in different languages?
2. How are words built out of units of meaning?
3. How do word formation processes differ across languages?

# What is a Word?

Consider the expression "Let's go look for the house".

1. **Vietnamese**. Mình Ɠǫ xem nhà Ɠǫ 
2. **Cebuano**. Pangitaon nato ang bahay
3. **Kanien'kéha**. Teninonhsihsákha

A word is variously defined as "the smallest free form found in a language", "the smallest separable unit language", etc. But note that words are not the smallest unit of language overall.

For example, in Kanien'kéha, we can translate "Teninonhsihsákha" literally as

```
Te- ni- nonhs- ihsák- ha
we  two house  seek   go
```

# Morphemes

A **morpheme** is the smallest unit of **meaning** in a language. It is the smallest **individual** meaningful part of a word.

It is also the smallest systematic pairing of form and meaning/grammatical function. For example, "bleed" is one morpheme, and then "bleeding" is two morphemes, with "-ing" indicating that the event is ongoing.

Words can differ in terms of **morphological complexity**:

- **Morphologically complex** words have **more than one morpheme** (e.g. cats, redo, teachers)
- **Morphologically simple** words have **only a single morpheme** (e.g. cat, do, teach)

## Interlinear Glossing

We can represent morphological structures using **interlinear glossing**. Consider the Tsuut'ina language

```
michàdikòdi
mi-  chà-  di-  kòd-   i    (Morphemes)
3SG  tail  be   broad  NOM  (Morpheme meanings)
the one whose tail is broad  (English "gloss" or translation)
```

# Morphological Typology

Languages display different morphological types, from **analytic** to **synthetic**.

**Analytic (or isolating) languages** have a low amount of morphemes in a word (i.e. morphologically simple). No human language is perfectly isolate, but we can get a near one-to-one correspondence between word and morphemes. For example, Yoruba, Vietnamese, and Chinese languages.

Synthetic languages have a high number of morphemes in a word (i.e. morphologically complex. For example, Swahili and most Indo-European languages are moderately synthetic; Blackfoot is highly synthetic.

Synthetic languages fall into two main divisions:

1. **Agglutinative**: words are built from many **easily separated** morphemes, each of which are associated with one consistent piece of meaning. For example, Japanese.
2. **Fusional**: a single morpheme comprise of many (inflectional) meanings. For example, Spanish.

Conventionally, individual morphemes are generally isolated using dashes (-). Each morpheme will be associated with one meaning. But one morpheme can encode two or more (grammatical) features. This is represented by a dot.

Consider Spanish

```
El  hombre habl-ó                    con  la  mujer
the man    speak-INDIC.PAST.PERF.3SG with the woman
The man spoke with the woman
```

# Morpheme Types and Categories

## Free vs Bound Morphemes

One categorization of morphemes is whether the morpheme is free or bound.

1. **Free morpheme**: a morpheme that can stand on its own as a word
2. **Bound morpheme**: a morpheme that **must** be attached to another unit in order for its meaning to be realized

For example, in activeate, "act" is a free morpheme and "ive" and "ate" are bound.

There is validation between what is a free and a bound morpheme in languages. The morphemes "sister" and "sock" are free morphemes in English, while they are bound morphemes in East Cree: "\*mis" is sister, and "nimis" is "my sister".

## Affixes, Bases, and Roots

Morphemes can also be categories according to their status as an affix, base, and root.

An **affix** is a kind of bound morpheme. They need to attach to something (i.e. a base). There are different affixes that attach to different positions relative to the base: **prefix**, **suffix**, **infix**, and **circumfix**.

A **prefix** is an affix that goes before its base. A **suffix** is an affix that goes after its base. An **infix** is an affix that attaches **inside** its base (e.g. bili "to buy"; binili "bought" in tagalog); the base is split. A **circumfix** is an **affix** that attaches around its base (e.g. bata "child"; kabataan "youth" in tagalog)

A **base** (or **stem**) is the morpheme that an affix attaches to (a base can itself be a simple or complex morpheme)

A **root** is the special name for a **simple base**: it is the **smallest possible base**, which cannot be divided into smaller parts. The root is the morpheme in a word that provides the most meaningful content. While affixes must be bound, roots can be free or bound (e.g., in English there are a few roots that must be bound such as -whelmed in overwhelmed or underwhelmed).

# Representing Structures

## Lexical Categories

Morphological complex words have internal **hierarchical structure**. But before we can represent such structures, we must determine the **lexical categories** of words (aka **part of speech**).

In English, for example, there are four lexical categories: (N)oun, (V)erb, (A)djective, (Adv)erbs. The most reliable diagnostics/tests to determine these are **morphological** (and/or **syntactic**) in nature, where we consider their **distribution**.

For example, we have the following:

1. **Morphological test for nouns**. Can take singular or plural forms (e.g. cats)
2. **Morphological test for verbs**. Can take a third-person singular present form -s (e.g. she sings); can take a past-tense form -ed (e.g. they baked the cake); can take a progressive form -ing (e.g. he is eating); can have a perfect form -ed/-en (e.g. they have eaten)
3. **Morphological tests for adjectives**. Can take a suffix -ish (e.g. the smallish bad); can take a comparative (e.g. they are smarter) or superlative form (e.g. it is the coolest).
4. **Morphological test for adverbs**. Many end with a -ly suffix (e.g. it flew quickly)

## Morphological Trees

We use **morphological trees** to represent the structure of morphologically complex words.

For example, darken (V):

 - dark (A) is the root.
 - dark (A) also acts as the base for the affix -en.

# Morphological Processes

## Morphological Phenomena

There are different **word-formation processes**:

- **Affixation**: derivational vs **inflectional**.
- **Internal change**.
- **Suppletion**.
- **Reduplication**.

### Affixation: Derivation

Derivation s a process of affixation (prefixation, suffixation, infixation, circumfixation, etc) that changes the meaning of the word. It often changes the lexical category too (though some affixes \[e.g., prefixes\] do not change the lexical categories of the base).

For example,

- friend (N) to friendly (A), which is a change in meaning and lexical category.
- green (A) to greenish (A), which is a change in meaning but not lexical category

Derivational morphemes show **selection**: they are selective about the types of bases they combine with. For example, the suffix -able combines only with verbs, and the resulting word is an adjective with the meaning "able to be \[verb\]-ed"

While derivational suffixes change the lexical category of the word, **derivational prefixes do not change the lexical category** (but still, they express clear shift in meaning).

Complex derivations involve multiple derivational affixes combining to the base(s) (e.g. interpret to misinterpretability).

The order in which derivational affixes get added to their bases its important. It ensures that we get the **intended meaning**.

### Affixation: Inflection

**Inflextion** is a process of affixation that **does not** involve meaning and/or lexical category changes. Instead, the process typically expresses **grammatical information**.

For example,

- friend (N) to friends (N): no change in category/meaning; expresses **plurality**
- sell (V) to sells (V): no change in category/meaning; expresses **third person singular**

Inflection in English is quite limited: there are only eight inflectional affixes.

1. **Nouns**
	1. Plurality (e.g. books)
	2. Possessive (e.g. Hamza's book)
2. **Verbs**
	1. Present tense (e.g. cooks)
	2. Past tense (e.g. baked)
	3. Progressive (e.g. eating)
	4. Participle (e.g. eaten)
3. **Adjectives**
	1. Comparative (e.g. bigger)
	2. Superlative (e.g. biggest)

> [!TIP] Remark
> Derivational affixes are typically closer to the root; they are typically attached first, followed by inflectional affixes.
> 
> Note that analyzes are done in Modern English. As time has passed, some affixes have become less productive and some roots **do not exist as free morphemes** anymore.
> 
> For example, the word "revive" could be analyzed as "re" and "vive" in the past, but in modern English, -vive is no longer a free morpheme

### Internal Change

**Internal change** is the substitution of a **non-morphemic** segment for another to mark a grammatical contrast (i.e. primarily inflections)

For example, in English, we have

1. **Sing** (present) vs **sang** (past)
2. **Food** (singular) vs **feet** (plural)

### Suppletion

**Suppletion** replaces a morpheme with an entirely different morpheme in order to indicate a grammatical contrast (i.e. primarily inflections).

For example,

1. In English, is (basic form) vs are (suppletive form)
2. In French, avoir "to have" (basic form) vs eu "had" (suppletive form)

### Full Reduplication

**Full reduplication** is the marking of semantic or grammatical contrast by repeating all the base.

For example, 

1. In Turkish, çabuk "quickly" vs çabuk çabuk "very quickly"

### Partial Reduplication

**Partial reduplication** is the marking of semantic or grammatical contrast by repeating all part base.

For example, 

1. In Tagalog, takbo "run" vs tatakbo "will run"
