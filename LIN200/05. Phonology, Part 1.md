# Categorizing Sounds

The **mental lexicon** of a language user stores knowledge of words, including their **pronunciation**, meaning, syntax, and usage. It stores underlying representations of sounds and their variations.

For example, the sounds represented by \<p\> in pat and spat sound different, but the sound \[ph\] and \[p\] are simply variations of the same object: /p/.

Thus, there must be some way the mind stores this information in the form of **categories**. We can organize speech sounds into different abstract, conceptual categories.

**Phonology** is the study of how sounds are organized in the mind to create patterns that we eventually hear in language. It is primarily concerned with **abstract representation** of speech sounds and the system of constraints that determine how those sound pattern within a language: **which sounds can occur, how they can be combined, and how they change in different linguistic contexts**.

Phonology poses the following questions:

1. **How are sounds represented in the minds of speakers of different languages?** For example, \[o\] and \[õ\] don’t signal a change in meaning in English but does so in French
2. **How are different sounds distributed in different languages?** For example, In English, segments \[t\], \[i\], \[n\] can combine into \[tin\] teen or \[nit\] neat in English but not \[nti\] or \[tni\].
3. **How are different sounds organized into larger (abstract) units?** For example, in English, the word happy \[hæpi\] is syllabified as \[hæ.pi\] and not \[hæp.i\].

# Syllables

A layer up from individual speech segments are **syllables**. A syllable is a sequence of segments that act as **one** unit organized around a core element (e.g. a vowel) and additional elements that are associated with this core element.

Syllables can be though of as building blocks of words. Thus, words may contain multiple syllables; each syllable can contain multiple segments.

**We often have a good intuition for how many syllables a word has**, but we may not necessarily know where the syllable boundaries are. Furthermore, our intuitions about how many syllables a word has may be off. Consider **hire**, **higher**, **hour**, **power**, **owl**, **towel**.

## As Phonological Units

Syllables cannot be realized phonetically but they are highly **perceptible** phonological units. We can identify a fricative or a vowel in a spectrogram but not syllables.

But we can strongly perceive syllables because, for one, vowels create natural rhythmic "beats".

## Syllable Structure

If we are using transcription, syllable boundaries are drawn with the symbol \[.\]. For example, \[kæ.nə.də\] for Canada.

As a structural unit, a syllable is abbreviated with the Greek **sigma $\sigma$**. Our mental grammar organizes "words" into syllables.

We express a syllable's structure as hierarchical, with the following components.

1. **Nucleus (N or Nuc)**: the most prominent position (the one with the highest acoustic energy; the loudest; the most sonorous); this is the **core** of the syllable. **This is usually filled by a vowel in most languages**.
2. **Onset (O)**: the position to the left of the nucleus
3. **Coda (C)**: the position to the right of the nucleus
4. **Rhyme (R)**: the combination of nucleus and coda. This is the part that most monosyllabic rhyming words have in common (e.g. \[ɪk\] in lick, sick, tick, stick, brick, pick, click).

The onset and coda are referred to as the margins. The margins of the syllable can be **empty**. If filled, a margin with only one segment is called **simple**. A margin with two or more segments is called **complex**.

![[syllable structure.png]]

## CV Notation

One way to represent syllable structure is through **CV notation**, where C represents each segment in the margins and V represents each segment in the nucleus.

Syllables have the following categories:

- **Open syllable**: a syllable with no coda (e.g., see \[si\] and glow \[glo\] are CV syllables).
- **Closed syllable**: a syllable with a coda (e.g., eat \[it\] is a VC syllable and hat \[hæt\] is a CVC syllable).
- **Onsetless syllable**: a syllable with no onset but can be open or closed (e.g., V or VC syllables like awe \[ɑ\] and all \[ɑl\], respectively).

# Syllable Types

Syllable type is the "template" for all possible **individual** syllables in a particular language. Languages have different syllable types.

Languages vary in terms of the complexity of syllables they allow. However, languages generally **prefer onsets and disprefer or even disallow codas**. Languages also generally prefer simple margins than complex margins.

In the CV Notation, we can use **()** to show a margin C that is **allowed but not required/obligatory**.

## Examples

- **(C)V**. In Hawaiian, we have
	- \[hu.la\]: Hawaiian dance
	- \[ma.ha.lo\]: Thank you
- **CV(C)**. In Tagalog, we have
	- \[da.la\]: to bring
	- \[dal.dal\]: to talk
- **(C)V(C)**. In Korean, we have
	- \[gɛ\]: dog
	- sɔn]: hand
- **(C)(C)V(C)(C)**: In French,
	- \[mas\]: mass
	- \[pri\]: prize
	- \[ɛst\]: east
* **(C)(C)(C)V(C)(C)(C)**: In English,
	* \[bɹɪk\]: brick
	* \[fɪfθs\]: fifths
	* \[stɹɛŋθs\]: strengths

## Phonotactics

There are **constraints** on what sequences of segments can (and cannot) combine in particular environments. For example, in English,

- A **CCC** sequence is possible at the beginning of a syllable **if and only if** $C_1$ is \[s\], $C_2$ is a voiceless stop \[p t k\], and $C_3$ is an approximant \[ɹ l w j\]
- \[tl\] and \[dl\] sequences in onsets are banned.

Languages impose their own set of restrictions on sound sequences. These restrict:

- How many sounds can fit into each slot of the syllable.
- Which sounds can go where.

These matters are related to **phonotactics**, the study of the permissible combinations of segments **in a given language**. Words being borrowed into another language must abide by its phonotactics.

Consider **loanwords in Japanese**. Japanese has the syllable type **(C)V(C)** where the coda C can only be a **nasal**. Thus, English loanwords in Japanese undergo "**repairs**" to fit Japanese syllable types and phonotactics.

These repairs can include breaking apart consonant clusters and vowel epenthesis. For example,

- **Baseball**: English \[bes.bɑl\], Japanese: \[beː.su.boː.ɾu\]
- **Christmas**: English \[kɹɪ.sməs\], Japanese: \[ku.ɾi.su.ma.su\]
- **Victoria**: English \[vɪk.tɔ.ɹjə\], Japanese: \[bi.ku.to.ɾi.ja\]

# Syllabification

Syllabification is the mapping of segments to appropriate positions in the syllable structure. It consists of the following steps.

1. Provide the transcription (i.e., representation in IPA).
2. Identify the nucleus and then **project up** to the rhyme and the syllable.
3. Identify the segments in onset, following the **Maximal Onset Principle**, and then project up to the syllable.
4. Identify the segments in coda and then project up to the rhyme

Consider the word contrast, which has transcription: \[kɑntɹæst\]. First we identify the nuclei and project them up to the rhyme and syllable

![[Second Year/LIN200/Images/syllabification 2.png]]

Then, we identify the segments in the onset and project up to the syllable, following the **Maximal Onset Principle**. We want to assign as many segments in the onset as possible, as phonotactics permits. The sequence of segments in the onset must be able to begin a word in English.

![[syllabification 2 1.png]]

Then, identify the segments in the coda and project up to the rhyme.

![[syllabification 4.png]]

Thus, the word contrast has two syllables \[kɑn.tɹæst\] with the CV notation CVC.CCVCC. Note that the second syllable has exactly 2 segments in the onset i.e. it is a complex onset.

Also remember the following:

1. Each syllable must have a nucleus. Usually this is the most sonorous segment, such as a vowel.
2. Affricates \[t͡ʃ\] and \[d͡ʒ\] count as a single segment in margins.
3. In this course, we will treat English diphthongs \[aɪ\], \[aʊ\], and \[ɔɪ\]  as a **single segment** i.e. \[aʊ\] in \[ɡɹaʊnd\]. 

# Phonemes and Allophones

Certain segments can be pronounced slightly differently yet treated as the same conceptual object by speakers. The conceptual object is referred to as a **phoneme** while its physical realities as segments are called its **allophones**.

We can think of a phoneme as a set, wherein each phonetically-similar member surfaces in **specific** positions called **environments**. The list of environments in which each allophone appears is called the phoneme's **distribution**.

For example, consider the segments \[t\] and \[tʰ\] in English. These two sounds are **phonetically similar**. They can be used to pronounce the grapheme \<t\> in words like **take** and **steak**:

- \[tʰ\] is aspirated. It is used in words like **take**: \[tek\]
- \[t\] is unaspirated. It is used in words like **steak**: \[stek\]

In theory, we can use \[t\] in place of \[tʰ\] (and vice versa): \[tek\] and \[stʰek\].The resulting pronunciation would be offbeat, but it would not change the meaning
of the word.

In English, these two segments are organized into one category in (native) speakers’ minds. Consider the following diagram:

![[phonemes and allophones.png]]

In English, the voiceless stop /t/ is aspirated at the **beginning of a stressed syllable** (but not after \[s\]). Notice that after the release of the stop in take, there is a **lag** before the voicing of the following vowel. This lag is accompanied by a puff of air, which is called aspiration. Aspiration is not present in \[t\] in steak.

Thus, we say that \[t\] and \[tʰ] have different environments.

![[environments.png]]

![[distributions of allophones.png]]

Now consider the segments \[b\] and \[s\]. Substituting one segment for the other would make a big difference in meaning for English listeners; it creates **contrast**.

This suggests that somewhere in the minds of (native) English speakers, there is
one abstract representation that includes \[b\] and another, separate one that
includes \[s\]. Thus, these two sounds have separate phonemes.

![[b and s.png]]

If the two sounds are phonetically similar and appear in different environments, they are simply allophones of the same phoneme. But when a distinction between two sounds is important enough in a language that they are treated as **separate** consonants (i.e., they are contrastive), those two sounds are **allophones of separate phonemes** in that language.

**Phonemes** are abstract mental categories (not sounds). They are the smallest unit of speech that can produce **contrasts** between words.

Individual phonemes in the mind of a speaker are assumed to be **contrastive**. **Contrastive sounds** are what languages use to build words with different meanings.

We will use / / to represent **phonemes** e.g /t/. A **phonemic transcription** records how the sound is stored in the mind.

## Distributions and Natural Classes

The overall pattern of where segments can and cannot occur (i.e., phonetic
environments) is called its **distribution**.

Phonemes that share various phonetic properties form a **natural class**. These classes are **exhaustive**: they are the complete set of sounds that share some phonetic feature.

Phonemes that form a natural class have **similar** distributions and undergo similar phonological processes; we can predict how they will behave in different contexts

For example, in English, we have

| High vowels       | {/i ɪ u ʊ/}           |
| Round vowels      | {/u ʊ o ɔ/}           |
| Mid front vowels  | {/ɛ e/}               |
