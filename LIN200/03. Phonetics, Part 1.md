# Introduction

Phonetics is the study of the **production** and **perception** of sounds and signs. In this course, we focus on **articulatory phonetics**: the physiological mechanisms of speech and sign production.

Other aspect of phonetics are: 

- **Acoustic phonetics**: the physical properties of a speech sounds
- **Perceptual phonetics**: the perception and processing of linguistic signals

# Speech Segments

A speech **segment** (or **phone**) is an individual speech **sound** and hte main unit of phonetic representation in spoken speech.

Our vocal apparatus can produce a variety of sounds , but not all of them are linguistically significant to be used in a language. A **linguistically significant sound** is a sound that can be used as part of ordinate **words** in a particular language.

# Spelling and Phones

In present-day English, it is possible to have

1. **One grapheme** (or letter) can map onto **multiple segments**. For example, the **grapheme** **\<j\>** can represent the **segments** in the words **j**ump, f**j**ord, **j**alapeño, fa**j**ita, etc
2. **Multiple graphemes** can map onto **one segment**. For example, the grapheme sequence **\<ee\>** in **seek** represents just one segment \[i\].
3. **One segment** can map onto multiple **graphemes**. For example, the segment \[u\] and the words t**oo**, tr**ue**, sh**oe**, fl**ew**, thr**ou**gh.
4. **One (or more) grapheme(s)** can map onto **no segments** ("silent letter"). For example, the grapheme \<g\> in the words **g**nome, si**g**n, fi**g**ht.

There is no general one-to-one correspondence between graphemes and segments in present-day English. This is because English spelling has stayed the same while pronunciation has changed over time (e.g. **The Great Vowel Shift** where many vowels changed their pronunciation).

English also borrowed a lot of words from French, Latin, Greek, etc.; and their respective spellings were retained even when English pronunciation did not (e.g. "psychology" from Greek).

Thus, English spelling reflects historical pronunciation patterns, not modern ones.

This leads to a learnability problem: If we do not know the spelling conventions and the mappings between spelling and phones, we would have trouble pronouncing English words.

Orthography/spellings are difficult and an **unreliable reflection** of speech segments. English orthography is not as transparent (in how it reflects segments) today as it did in older forms.

Other languages may have more transparent/shallow orthography e.g. Spanish or Tagalog.

# The International Phonetic Alphabet

One solution to this problem is the **International Phonetic Alphabet** (**IPA**). This is a **conventionalized** system that describes all **possible (and attested) sounds of all human languages** (all sounds that the vocal tract and physiologically produce) with the constraints of orthographic differences.

Each segment gets its own IPA symbol. This we have a **one-to-one mapping** between segment and symbol.

Slight modifications to the sound quality can be represented in the IPA through small symbols called **diacritics**. For example, the sound \[p\] with a strong burst of air is represented as \[pʰ\].

## Notation

We enclose **graphemes** of letters/orthography in angle brackets, whereas **segments**/**phones** are enclosed in square brackets.

These brackets distinguishes between different representations.

# Speech Articulators

Speech is created through a complex physiological process.

## Lungs

Generally, we articulate sounds with air coming out of the lungs through the mouth and/or nose (**egressive** speech sounds).

There are also sounds that are made with airstream flowing inward (**ingressive** speech sounds).

## The Vocal Tract

Air passes through the lungs through various cavities (openings), where air gets modified, resulting in many different segments.

- **Oral cavity**: main interior of the mouth, from back of the lips.
- **Pharyngeal cavity**: behind/lower than oral cavity, upper part of the throat
- **Nasal cavity**: open interior of the head, above the oral cavity from the back of the nostrils down to the pharynx
- **Trachea** (or windpipe): leads down to the lungs. This is important for phonetics as spoken languages mostly uses air from the lungs.
- **Esophagus**: leads down to the stomach. Irrelevant for phonetics (primarily for eating and burping)

The **vocal folds** (or **vocal cords**) are found inside the larynx (or voice box) that surround the trachea. These are membranes on the left and right of the larynx that stretch from the front to the back. The open space between them is known as the **glottis**.

The way the vocal folds are oriented give rise to different **phonation** types, of which the two main types are **voiced** and **voiceless**.

![[voice box.png]]

**Active** (or **lower**) **articulators** are parts of the vocal tract that "move" and initiate contact with another articulator. For example, the **lower lip**, **tongue tip**, **tongue black**, **tongue front** (tongue tip and blade), **tongue back**, **tongue root**, **epiglottis**.

![[active articulators.png]]

> [!TIP] Remark
>  the lower teeth could theoretically be an active articulator (e.g., it can move towards the upper lip), but there is no known language that uses them for this purpose.

**Passive** (or **upper**) **articulators** do not move but are the "target" or landing sites of active articulators. For example, the **upper lip**, **upper teeth**, **alveolar ridge**, **postalveolar region**, **(hard) palate**, **velum**, **uvula**, **pharyngeal wall**

![[passive articulators.png]]

Passive articulators provide a “place” for active articulators to make contact, thereby producing some form of **constriction**.

# Primary Categories of Speech Sounds

Speech sounds can be categorized into several primary categories

1. **Consonants**: These are produced by a period of complete or partial blocking of the vocal track; less audible
2. **Vowels**: These are produced with little obstruction in the vocal tract and are more audible than consonants.

# Articulatory Descriptions of Consonants

![[canadian consonants.png]]

**Articulatory descriptions** of consonants rely on three
parameters

1. **Voicing/phonation**: this can be determined by looking at the shape of the line representing the vocal folds
2. **Place of articulation**: this cam be determined by identifying which active articulator is targeting which passive articulator
3. **Manner of articulation**: this can be determined by assessing how and where airflow is obstructed by the placement of the articulators

For example,
- \[p\]: voiceless bilabial stop
- \[ð\]: voiced interdental fricative
- \[j\]: voiced palatal approximant
- \[tʃ\]: voiceless postalveolar affricate
- \[n\]: voiced alveolar nasal
- \[ŋ\]: voiced velar nasal
## Voicing

**Voicing** (or **phonation**) refers to whether the vocal cords **vibrate** when the sound is produced. A voiced segment has vocal cord vibration whereas voiceless segments do not.

## Place of Articulation

The **place of articulation** is about which active articulator is targeting which passive articulator. In the below chart,

![[canadian consonants.png]]

The header row in the above chart specifies the place of articulation. For instance,

- **Bilabial**: upper and lower lips,
- **Labiodental**: lower lip and upper teeth.
- **Interdental**: tongue against or in-between the upper and lower teeth.
- **Alveolar**: tongue tip and alveolar ridge (the body ridge just behind the teeth)
- **Postalveolar**: tongue blade is behind the alveolar ridge.
- **Palatal**: tongue (front and back) and hard palate.
- **Velar**: tongue back and velum. Some sounds, like \[w\] also involve the lips and are called **labiovelar**
- **Glottal**: vocal folds (and not significant movement of any active articulators)

## Manner of Articulations

The **manner of articulation** refers to how air flows through the vocal tract, given the size and shape of the obstruction/construction created by the articulators.

![[Pasted image 20260924152347.png]]

The header column in the above chart specifies the manner of articulator. For instance,

* **(Oral) stop/plosive**: involves complete closure in the oral cavity; the velum is raised so air escapes through the oral cavity upon burst release
  ![[stop_plosive.png]]
* **Nasal**: also a complete closure in the oral cavity; the **velum is lowered**  so air escapes through the nasal cavity even through the oral closure phase
  ![[nasal.png]]
* **Fricative**: articulators create a narrow passage allowing air to escape. This results in **friction** or **turbulent** noise. Compared to oral stops, airflow is interrupted but not stopped entirely
* **Affricate**: a "hybrid" of a stop and a fricative. It starts out as a stop (i.e. complete closure is created) and ends like a fricative (i.e. creates a narrow opening for air to escape). An affricate is a **single** segment and are often notated with a **tie bar diacritic** over a stop and fricative segment (e.g. \[t͡ʃ\]).
* **Tap/Flap**: the tongue (tip) makes contact with the alveolar ridge very briefly. This is the sound you produce when \[t\] and \[d\] are in between vowel sounds and the preceding vowel is stressed. Consider the words \<bu**tt**er\> or \\<wa**t**er\>.
* **(Central) Approximant**: the tongue gets close to (‘approximates’) a part of the mouth but doesn’t really touch it. There is **less closure/more opening** than a fricative, so air escapes without much turbulence. We use the upside-down r symbol for this.
* **Lateral Approximant**: The obstruction is at the center of the oral cavity (the tongue touches the alveolar ridge); air flows at the sides of the tongue.
* **Labiovelar Approximant**: Like a central approximant (i.e., the tongue back gets close to the velum but doesn’t really touch it. But the lips are also involved; the lips are rounded.

Whether the velum is lowered or raise can result in nasal vs oral sounds.

Nasal sounds are made when the velum is lowered, allowing air to flow through the nasal cavity (There are three such segments in English: \[m, n, ŋ\]). Oral sounds are made when the velum is raised, thus blocking air from flowing through the nasal cavity.

This manner distinction is often called **nasality**.

# Subclasses of Consonants

Consonants are often categorized into **subclasses** of manner of articulation:

- **Glides**: a subclass of approximants; have vowel-like qualities e.g. \[j\] and \[w\].
- **Liquids**: a subclass of approximants e.g \[s\], \[z\], \[ʃ\], and \[ʒ\].
- **Sibiliants**: noisy, high-pitched, "s-like" sounds.
- **Sonorants**: segments with some obstruction but continuous airflow e.g. nasals, liquids, glides, and vowels.
- **Obstruents**: segments with substantial obstruction of airflow e.g. oral stops, fricatives, affricates.

# Practical Applications

## Computer Assisted Language Learning (CALL)

Computers can be used for a variety of language learning tasks. For example, **Lambacher (2010)** created a CALL tool for improve second language acquisition of English consonants by Japanese learnings.

This was a **software** that uses real time acoustic data to help Japanese learners of English improve their production (and perception) of English consonants

The basic workflow is as follows:

> Learners receive instruction on consonant production → Learners produce a consonant → Software analyzes the acoustic properties → Software generates a visual of the pronunciation → Learner can compare their pronunciation with a “model”.

Furthermore, learning articulatory phonetics can 

- Allow language educators to use the relevant terminology to describe consonant sounds.
* Help give feedback to improving pronunciation (and perception).
* Help learners self-evaluate and adjust appropriately based off of the acoustic consequences of certain articulatory configurations.
