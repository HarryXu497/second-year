# Introduction

Recall that **phonology** is the study of how sounds are stored in the mind as abstract, mental representations and how these create patterns that we hear in language. These patterns are governed by phonological rules.

By looking at language, we can get a sense for how sounds pattern in a language. For example, in English, \[p\] and \[pʰ\] are members of one category /p/, but in Hindi, \[p\] and \[pʰ\] are members of two separate categories, namely /p/ and /pʰ

# Phonemes and Allophone

**Phonemes** are abstract mental categories of sounds. Their realizations are the smallest unit of speech that can produce **contrasts** between words. 

We use / / to represent **phonemes**.

Individual phonemes in the mind of a speaker are assume to be **contrastive**. **Contrastive sounds** are what languages use to build different words (with different meanings).

For example, if \[p\] and \[t\] can produce contrasts in English, then we can infer they must have separate representations in the mind (i.e. /p/ and /t/).

If two segments are phonetically similar and appear in different environments, they are **allophones of the same phoneme**. For example, English \[t\] and \[tʰ\] are allophones of the same phoneme /t/.

When a distinction between two segments is important enough in a language that they produce contrasts, those two segments are **allophones of separate phonemes**. For example, English \[b\] and \[s\] contrast \[bæd\] bad and \[sæd\] sad. As such, English \[b\] and \[s\] are allophones of **separate** phonemes /b/ and /s/.

## Environments and Distributions

The specific conditions in which one allophone appears is called the **environment**.

The combined set of all environments of all allophones of a phoneme is called its **distribution**.

# Identifying Phonemes

To determine if two sounds are contrastive, we look for **minimal pairs**. Recall that mental grammar is inaccessible, so we have to look a production patterns and infer what the grammar looks like.

A **minimal pair** is a pair of words that only differ by one segment **in the same position** and have a contrast in meaning. These attest that the two differing segments are separate phonemes.

For example, the minimal pair /kem/ came and /gem/ game show that /k/ and /g/ are phonemes

![[contrasts.png]]

Minimal pairs can be established in different positions: initial, medial, and final.

# Contrastive and Complementary Distributions

Suppose we are comparing two sounds.

## Contrastive Distributions

A **contrastive distribution** is characterized by an **overlap** in the same environment. When two sounds have overlapping environments, they are in a contrastive distribution. Those two sounds are then allophones of separate phonemes.

For example:

- English /pæt/ and /bæt/ have **overlapping environment** \#\_æt. The hashtag denotes the word boundary
- English /kɹæb/ and /kɹɪb/ have **overlapping environment** kɹ_b.

Often, **presence of one minimal pair** in a data set is **sufficient** to conclude a

contrastive distribution; and that two sounds are allophones of separate

phonemes (e.g., /p/ and /b/); this is the approach we will adopt in LIN200.

In some cases, two segments will only have a **near minimal pair**. This is similar to a minimal pair, except there are one ore more additional differences elsewhere in the word besides the crucial position.

For example, in English, \[ð\]~\[θ\] has no minimal pairs. It does have near minimal pairs though: \[ðiz\] these and \[ðisəs\] thesis.

We won't see near minimal pairs in LIN200.

## Complementary Distributions

A **complementary distribution** is characterized by **non-overlapping, non-intersecting** environments.

When two sounds have **non-overlapping** environment, they are in a complementary distribution. We have grounds to conclude that the two sounds in question are **allophones** of the same phoneme.

The two sounds may look superficially different, but they are the same conceptual object at a deeper, abstract level.

Segments that are in complimentary distributions cannot distinguish between words simply because they could never occur in the same place as a word

For example, consider \[tʰek\] take and \[stek\] steak. \[t\] and \[tʰ\] never occur in the same space as a word. You can substitute one for the other, which would change the pronunciation but not the meaning of the word. In other words, they are no contrastive in English

# Phonemic Analysis

The goal of **phonemic analysis** is to determine what the phonemes are in a language, the allophones of those phonemes, and what each allophone's distribution is.

The general workflow is as follows

Recall that a **natural class** is a set of sounds that share certain phonetic features. They can be general/broad or narrow/specific e.g. consonants, vowels, voiced consonants, oral stops, etc. These will help us generalize patterns in the data.

Consider the data below from Tagalog.

From this data, we see three minimal pairs /kaʔon/ and /kahon/, /taʔan/ and /tahan/, and /ʔiliŋ/ and /hiliŋ/ which have different meanings. Thus, \[h\] and \[ʔ\] are contrastive and thus allophones of separate phonemes.

Now, consider the data from Gascon

![[phonemic analysis data 2.png]]

We want to determine if \[g\] and \[ɣ\] are allophones of separate phonemes or allophones of the same phoneme.

There are no minimal pairs here in the data though, so they must not be in a contrastive distribution. Instead, we want to show that they are in a complementary distribution.

1. **Evidence 1**. Phonetic similarity: the two sounds in question should have mostly the same phonetic qualities. \[g\] and \[ɣ\] are phonetically similar since they are both voiced and velar; the difference is in manner: \[g\] is a stop while \[ɣ\] is a fricative.
2. **Evidence 2**. Non-overlapping environments. We need to set up an **environment table** to see if this is the case. See the following section for this.

In conclusion, the Gascon data shows that there are no minimal pairs for \[g\] and \[ɣ\]. They are phonetically similar, and their distributions do not overlap (i.e. they are in complementary distributions)

Thus, **they are allophones of the same phoneme**.

We generally take the allophone with the **widest** distribution as the phoneme. So in thus case, the phoneme is /g/ since it occurs in more cases

# Phonological Rules

**Phonological rules** are statements about a change in the surface form of a target phoneme in a particular environment. Consider the statements from the Gascon analysis:

> /g/ is pronounced as \[ɣ\] in between vowels and /g/ is pronounced as \[g\] elsewhere

We often drop the elsewhere clause, since we are more interested in the conditions that allows an "unfaithful" allophone to surface.

Consider the statements about English aspiration:

- /t/ is pronounced as \[tʰ\] is pronounced as \[tʰ\] at the beginning of stressed syllables but not after \[s\]
- /p/ is pronounced as \[pʰ\] is pronounced as \[pʰ\] at the beginning of stressed syllables but not after \[s\]
- /k/ is pronounced as \[kʰ\] is pronounced as \[kʰ\] at the beginning of stressed syllables but not after \[s\]

All these three statements have the same form. Thus, we can simplify this further with natural classes.

> In English, **voiceless stops** are pronounced as **aspirated** at the beginning of stressed syllables, but not after \[\s]

> [!NOTE] The Principle of Faithfulness
> A phonological rule will only change the specific feature it is targeting. All other features are left unchanged.

## Phonological Derivations

A **phonological derivation** is an application of a phonological rule to a specific word.

**Underlying representations** are how the phoneme(s) (and in turn, the "word") are represented in the mind. Then, given some phonological rule, we can derive the **surface representations**.

For example, in Gascon, /g/ is pronounced as \[ɣ\] in between vowels. Thus, the underlying representations of the surface representation \[biɣar\] mosquito must be /bigar/.
